# Hormonal Triggers in Migraine: Early Warning Prediction System

## Overview

Hormonal fluctuations — particularly involving **estrogen, progesterone, serotonin, cortisol, melatonin, and insulin** — are among the most powerful and consistent migraine triggers. Hormonal migraine affects **up to 60% of women** who experience migraine, with attacks clustered around predictable hormonal events in the reproductive cycle.

Hormonal migraines tend to be **longer (up to 72 hours)**, **more severe**, **more resistant to standard treatment**, and more likely to be accompanied by nausea and vomiting than non-hormonal attacks.

For a prediction system, hormonal patterns offer high-value **time-predictable signal inputs**, particularly when combined with menstrual cycle tracking data.

---

## Primary Hormonal Mechanisms

### 1. Estrogen Withdrawal
The most well-characterized hormonal migraine trigger.

**Pathway:**
- Estrogen maintains serotonergic tone by upregulating serotonin receptors (5-HT₂) and tryptophan hydroxylase
- Estrogen drop → serotonin synthesis decrease → decreased descending pain inhibition → lowered migraine threshold
- Estrogen also modulates CGRP (calcitonin gene-related peptide) release from trigeminal nerve terminals
- Falling estrogen → increased CGRP → meningeal vasodilation → trigeminal pain activation

**When estrogen drops:**
- 2–3 days before menstruation (luteal-to-menstrual transition)
- Ovulation (brief mid-cycle estrogen dip)
- Perimenopause
- Placebo/pill-free week in combined oral contraceptive users
- Post-partum estrogen withdrawal
- Post-surgical menopause

**Critical threshold:** A drop of ≥ **10–25 pg/mL estradiol** within 24–48 hours can precipitate migraine.

---

### 2. Progesterone Effects
- High progesterone in the luteal phase has a mild protective effect via GABA-A modulation (anxiolytic, anti-excitatory)
- Rapid progesterone fall before menstruation disinhibits excitatory systems
- **Net effect:** The combined fall of both estrogen and progesterone just before menstruation is the highest-risk window

---

### 3. Prostaglandins
- During menstruation, **prostaglandin E2 (PGE2)** and **PGF2α** released from the uterine endometrium
- PGE2 directly sensitizes trigeminal pain fibers and promotes neurogenic inflammation
- High prostaglandin levels explain why menstrual migraines often co-occur with dysmenorrhea

## Menstrual Cycle & Migraine Risk Windows

### Cycle Phase Map

```
Day 1        Day 7        Day 14       Day 21       Day 28
|------------|------------|------------|------------|
  MENSTRUAL   FOLLICULAR    OVULATION     LUTEAL     PRE-MENSTRUAL
  ↑ HIGH RISK    Low Risk   ↑ MOD RISK    Low Risk    ↑ HIGH RISK
```

### Detailed Phase Risk Table

| Phase | Days (avg 28-day cycle) | Hormonal State | Migraine Risk | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Menstrual | Days 1–3 | Low E2, Low P4, High PGs | **Very High** | Worst window; perimenstrual migraine |
| Late Menstrual | Days 4–5 | Rising E2 | Low | Recovery phase |
| Follicular | Days 6–12 | Rising E2, Low P4 | Low | Most protected phase |
| Ovulation | Day 13–15 | E2 peaks then drops | **Moderate** | Mid-cycle dip trigger |
| Early Luteal | Days 16–19 | High E2 + High P4 | Low | Protective phase |
| Late Luteal | Days 20–25 | Falling E2, Falling P4 | Moderate | PMS phase begins |
| Pre-menstrual | Days 26–28 | Very low E2, Very low P4 | **High** | Premenstrual migraine |

> **Menstrual Migraine Definition (ICHD-3):** Migraine occurring on Day -2 to +3 of menstruation in ≥2 of 3 consecutive cycles.
> **Pure Menstrual Migraine:** Occurs ONLY perimenstrually with no other timing.

## Life Stage Hormonal Events & Migraine Patterns

### Puberty / Menarche
- Migraine prevalence rises sharply at **menarche** in girls (ages 12–16)
- Pre-pubescent migraine prevalence is roughly equal between sexes; post-pubescent: female:male ratio increases to ~3:1
- Irregular cycles in adolescence → unpredictable estrogen fluctuations → high attack frequency

### Pregnancy
| Trimester | Estrogen Status | Migraine Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| First trimester | Rapidly rising E2 | Worsening in some; improvement in others |
| Second trimester | Sustained high E2 | **Significant improvement in ~70% of patients** |
| Third trimester | Peak E2 | Continued improvement |
| Postpartum | Rapid E2 crash | **High relapse risk within 24–72 hours of delivery** |

- Post-partum period: estrogen drops more sharply than any other non-surgical state
- Breastfeeding suppresses estrogen (via prolactin-mediated suppression of GnRH)
- Post-partum sleep deprivation compounds risk significantly

### Perimenopause
- Erratic, unpredictable estrogen fluctuations (both high peaks and low troughs)
- Most migraineurs **worsen** significantly during perimenopause
- Attacks may become more frequent, longer, and more severe
- Duration: typically 4–8 years before natural menopause

### Menopause
- Once estrogen stabilizes at a consistently low level, **~70% of women experience improvement or remission**
- **Exception:** Surgical menopause (abrupt estrogen loss) typically worsens migraine dramatically
- HRT (Hormone Replacement Therapy) effects vary — transdermal estrogen at stable doses is generally preferred

## Contraceptives & Hormonal Medications

### Combined Oral Contraceptives (COC)
- **Active pill phase:** Stable synthetic estrogen → usually protective
- **Placebo week (pill-free interval):** Estrogen withdrawal → very high migraine risk
- **Recommendation:** Continuous pill use (no placebo break) can prevent estrogen-withdrawal migraine
- **Absolute contraindication:** COC is contraindicated in **migraine with aura** due to increased stroke risk (estrogen + hypercoagulability + aura vasospasm)

### Progestin-Only Contraceptives
- Depo-Provera, Mirena IUD, Nexplanon: generally safer for migraine with aura patients
- Variable effect: amenorrhea induced by Depo-Provera can reduce menstrual migraine frequency

### Hormone Replacement Therapy (HRT)
| Type | Migraine Impact |
|---|---|
| Oral estrogen | Variable; peaks and troughs can trigger |
| Transdermal estrogen (patch/gel) | More stable levels; preferred |
| Cyclical (with monthly progesterone withdrawal) | Can trigger monthly attacks |
| Continuous combined HRT | Preferred for migraine patients |

## Thyroid Hormones & Migraine

- **Hypothyroidism** is associated with increased migraine frequency
- TSH elevation correlates with increased headache days per month
- **Mechanism:** Low T3/T4 → increased TRH → serotonergic dysfunction
- **Hyperthyroidism:** Less well-studied; some association with tension-type headache

---

### Insulin & Blood Sugar (Hormonal Component)
- **Insulin-mediated hypoglycemia** is a powerful migraine trigger (see also food_triggers.md)
- Reactive hypoglycemia (post-high-carbohydrate meal) causes rapid insulin spike followed by glucose crash
- Blood glucose < 70 mg/dL triggers cortisol + adrenaline release → CNS stress response → migraine
- Insulin resistance (PCOS-associated) correlates with higher migraine prevalence

---

## Cortisol & Stress Hormones (Overlap with Stress Triggers)

- **Cortisol drop** after a stress period is a key trigger ("weekend migraine" / "let-down migraine")
- During acute stress: high cortisol is somewhat protective (suppresses inflammatory response)
- Post-stress cortisol drop: histamine release, substance P upregulation → migraine window
- Morning cortisol surge (circadian cortisol peak at ~6–8 AM) aligns with early-morning migraine onset in some patients
- See also: stress_triggers.md

---

## Melatonin & Circadian Hormones

- Melatonin has anti-CGRP and anti-inflammatory properties; low melatonin → increased migraine susceptibility
- Disrupted circadian rhythm → melatonin dysregulation → increased migraine risk
- Night shift workers, jet-lagged individuals, and those with insomnia have higher migraine frequency
- **Melatonin (3 mg nightly)** is used preventively for both migraine and cluster headache
- See also: sleep_triggers.md

---

## CGRP: The Central Hormonal-Neural Interface

**Calcitonin Gene-Related Peptide (CGRP)** is the primary neuropeptide mediating hormonal migraine:
- Released from trigeminal nerve terminals upon activation
- Potent vasodilator of meningeal blood vessels
- Estrogen upregulates CGRP expression; estrogen withdrawal → rebound CGRP surge
- CGRP levels are elevated in women during menstrual migraine attacks
- **CGRP-targeted therapies** (gepants, anti-CGRP monoclonal antibodies: erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab) are particularly effective for menstrual migraine

## Diagnostic Criteria: Menstrual Migraine (ICHD-3)

**A1.1.1 – Pure Menstrual Migraine Without Aura:**
- Attacks occur exclusively on Day -2 to +3 of menstruation
- In at least 2 of 3 consecutive cycles
- No attacks at other times of the month

**A1.1.2 – Menstrually Related Migraine Without Aura:**
- Attacks occurring Day -2 to +3 of menstruation in ≥2/3 cycles
- Additional attacks occurring at other times of the cycle
