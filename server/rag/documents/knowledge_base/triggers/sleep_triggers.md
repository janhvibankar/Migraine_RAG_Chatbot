# Sleep Triggers in Migraine: Early Warning Prediction System

## Overview

Sleep disturbances are bidirectionally linked with migraine — they are both a **trigger** and a **consequence** of migraine attacks. Approximately **50% of migraineurs** report sleep problems as a significant trigger, and conversely, migraine pain disrupts healthy sleep in the majority of patients. The relationship is mediated through shared neurological substrates: the hypothalamus, brainstem raphe nuclei, and thalamo-cortical circuits regulate both migraine susceptibility and the sleep-wake cycle.

The **most dangerous sleep-related trigger windows** are early morning (4–9 AM), when circadian cortisol surges and REM sleep peaks — a period when over 70% of migraine attacks that begin during sleep occur.

---

## Mechanisms: How Sleep Disruption Triggers Migraine

### 1. Serotonin & Hypothalamic Dysregulation

- The **dorsal raphe nucleus** (primary serotonin source) governs both migraine susceptibility and sleep-wake transitions
- Sleep deprivation → serotonin synthesis and receptor sensitivity decrease
- Low serotonin → increased cortical excitability, lowered migraine threshold
- The hypothalamus acts as a "migraine generator" — its disruption through sleep irregularity explains prodromal symptoms (yawning, food cravings, mood changes) 24–48 hours before attack

### 2. Orexin (Hypocretin) System

- Orexin-A and Orexin-B neurons in the lateral hypothalamus regulate sleep stability and arousal
- Orexin system dysfunction is implicated in both **narcolepsy** and **migraine**
- Orexin modulates trigeminal pain transmission; orexin receptor agonists are under investigation as migraine therapeutics
- Sleep fragmentation disrupts orexin tone → trigeminal sensitization

### 3. REM Sleep & Migraine Timing

- Most sleep-related migraine attacks begin during **REM sleep** or upon awakening from it
- During REM: increased cerebral blood flow, variable heart rate, fluctuating cortical activity
- REM sleep is rich in cortical spreading depression-like activity in migraine-prone individuals
- The longest REM periods occur in the **early morning hours (4–7 AM)** — explaining early morning migraine onset

### 4. Cortisol Circadian Rhythm

- Cortisol rises steeply from ~4–8 AM (the cortisol awakening response, CAR)
- This rapid cortisol rise, if timed poorly with sleep disruption, creates a stress-hormone surge that precipitates migraine
- Abnormal CAR is documented in chronic migraine patients

### 5. Adenosine Accumulation

- Prolonged wakefulness → adenosine buildup → cerebral vasodilation → increased migraine susceptibility
- Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors, explaining its acute migraine-relieving effect
- But caffeine used to compensate for sleep deprivation ultimately disrupts sleep architecture → worsening the cycle

## Sleep-Related Migraine Triggers: Classification

### A. Sleep Deprivation (Too Little Sleep)

**Definition:** Less than 6 hours in adults (age-adjusted)

**Mechanisms:**
- Serotonin depletion, cortisol disruption, systemic inflammatory cytokine elevation (IL-6, TNF-α)
- Pain threshold decreases measurably after even one night of poor sleep

**Clinical patterns:**
| Sleep Duration | Migraine Risk |
|---|---|
| ≥ 8 hours (individual optimal) | Baseline |
| 6–7 hours | Mildly elevated |
| 5–6 hours | Moderately elevated |
| < 5 hours | Very High |
| < 4 hours | Extreme; near-certain trigger in susceptible individuals |

**Cumulative deficit:** Sleep debt accumulates over multiple nights, not just acute deprivation. A 3-night period of 5-hour sleep (15 hours total lost) carries higher risk than one single night of 3-hour sleep.

---

### B. Oversleeping (Too Much Sleep)

Often counterintuitive but equally important:

**Definition:** >9–10 hours, or sleeping significantly beyond habitual wake time

**Mechanisms:**
- Late sleep-in delays the cortisol awakening response → hormonal mis-timing
- Caffeine withdrawal if habitual morning caffeine is delayed
- Hypoglycemia from extended fasting during extended sleep
- Disruption of serotonin cycling that depends on regular wake time

**Classic "Weekend Migraine" Pattern:**
- Sleeping 2+ hours later on weekend
- Combining: delayed cortisol surge + missed morning coffee + hypoglycemia + post-stress let-down
- Often misattributed to "relaxing after the week" when it is primarily a sleep-schedule disruption

---

### C. Irregular Sleep Schedules

- Variable bedtimes/wake times across days destabilize circadian rhythms
- Shift workers have 2–3x higher migraine prevalence than day workers
- Jet lag creates acute circadian desynchronization — high migraine risk during adjustment period

**Regularity principle:** Consistency of **wake time** is more important than total sleep duration for migraine prevention. Fixed wake time anchors the circadian clock.

---

### D. Poor Sleep Quality (Without Reduced Duration)

- **Sleep fragmentation:** Multiple awakenings interrupt restorative slow-wave and REM phases
- **Insufficient SWS (Slow Wave Sleep / Stage 3):** This is the most physically restorative phase; deficiency impairs pain modulation
- **Sleep onset latency >30 minutes:** Chronic difficulty falling asleep = chronic sympathetic hyperarousal

**Objective sleep quality measures:**
| Metric | Normal | Migraine-Risk Threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Sleep efficiency | >85% | <75% |
| Sleep onset latency | <20 min | >30 min |
| WASO (wake after sleep onset) | <30 min | >45 min |
| # of awakenings | <3 | >5 |
| REM % | 20–25% | <15% (suppressed) or >30% (excessive) |
| SWS % | 15–20% | <10% |

### E. Specific Sleep Disorders Associated with Migraine

| Sleep Disorder | Prevalence in Migraineurs | Interaction |
|---|---|---|
| Insomnia (chronic) | 30–50% | Most common; bidirectional |
| Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA) | 20–40% | Hypoxia, fragmented sleep, morning headache |
| Restless Legs Syndrome (RLS) | 17–25% | Dopaminergic dysfunction (shared pathway) |
| Periodic Limb Movement Disorder | 15–20% | Fragmented sleep, arousal response |
| REM Sleep Behavior Disorder | Elevated | Brainstem dysregulation |
| Bruxism | 25–50% | Stress + sleep; TMJ activation → trigeminal |
| Circadian Rhythm Sleep-Wake Disorders | Elevated in shift workers | See above |
| Hypersomnia / Narcolepsy | Rare overlap | Orexin system dysfunction |

#### Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA) — Special Focus

OSA is particularly significant for migraine prediction:
- Each apnea event causes brief **hypoxia and hypercapnia** → cerebral vasodilation
- Repetitive overnight hypoxia causes morning headache in ~35% of OSA patients
- High CPAP adherence in OSA patients significantly reduces migraine frequency
- OSA risk factors: obesity, neck circumference >40 cm, snoring, witnessed apneas, excessive daytime sleepiness

**STOP-BANG screening:**
Snoring | Tired | Observed apnea | Pressure (high BP) | BMI >35 | Age >50 | Neck circumference | Gender (male)

### F. Napping Patterns

| Nap Type | Effect on Migraine |
|---|---|
| Short nap (10–20 min, before 3 PM) | Generally protective; reduces sleep debt |
| Long nap (>60 min) | Induces sleep inertia; disrupts nighttime sleep |
| Late nap (after 4 PM) | Delays sleep onset; disrupts circadian rhythm |
| Migraine-aborted by nap | Common; sleep is a natural acute treatment for some patients |

## Sleep-Migraine Timing Patterns

### Morning Migraine (4–9 AM)
- Most common timing: 72% of "awakening from sleep" migraines
- Driven by: late REM sleep, cortisol surge, serotonin low point
- Often the most severe attacks

### Night-Onset Migraine (11 PM – 2 AM)
- Less common; often associated with stress, oversleeping the day prior, or sleep-phase delays

### Nap-Triggered
- Oversleeping during daytime nap; particularly in those with irregular nighttime sleep

## Prodromal Sleep Changes (Pre-Attack Warning Signs)

These changes precede migraine by **12–48 hours** and are valuable early warning signals:

| Prodromal Sleep Symptom | % of Patients | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Excessive yawning | 30–50% | Hypothalamic dopaminergic activation |
| Fatigue / drowsiness | 70–80% | Most common prodromal sign |
| Insomnia (night before attack) | 25–35% | Difficulty sleeping before attack |
| Hypersomnia (day before attack) | 15–20% | Sleeping more than usual |
| Neck stiffness/pain | 40–70% | Cervicogenic component |
| Difficulty concentrating | 50–60% | Pre-attack cognitive prodrome |
| Mood changes | 50–70% | Irritability, depression, euphoria |

## Sleep Hygiene Recommendations for Migraine Prevention

| Recommendation | Rationale |
|---|---|
| Fixed wake time (7 days/week) | Anchors circadian rhythm; reduces migraine frequency |
| Consistent bedtime (±30 min) | Stabilizes sleep architecture |
| Avoid caffeine after 2 PM | Reduces sleep-onset latency disruption |
| Sleep in dark, cool room (65–68°F / 18–20°C) | Promotes melatonin secretion, reduces light trigger |
| Avoid screens 1 hour before bed | Blue light suppresses melatonin (see screen_light_triggers.md) |
| Avoid alcohol before sleep | Alcohol fragments REM sleep despite facilitating sleep onset |
| OSA screening for chronic morning headache | CPAP therapy significantly reduces migraine frequency |
| Short relaxation routine before bed | Reduces cortisol, promotes parasympathetic tone |
| Avoid long naps (>20 min) after 3 PM | Prevents circadian disruption |
