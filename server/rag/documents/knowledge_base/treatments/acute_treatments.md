# Acute Migraine Treatments

## Overview

Acute (abortive) migraine treatments are medications and interventions taken at the **onset of a migraine attack** to stop or reduce the severity of the episode. They are not taken daily but used on-demand when an attack begins. Timely administration — ideally within the first 30–60 minutes of symptom onset — is critical for maximum efficacy.

Acute treatments are classified into **migraine-specific** (triptans, ditans, gepants, ergotamines) and **non-specific** (NSAIDs, antiemetics, analgesics, opioids) categories.

---

## 1. Migraine-Specific Acute Treatments

### 1.1 Triptans (5-HT1B/1D Receptor Agonists)

Triptans are the **gold standard** for moderate-to-severe migraine. They work by constricting cranial blood vessels and inhibiting the release of pro-inflammatory neuropeptides (CGRP, substance P).

#### Mechanism of Action
- Selective agonists at serotonin 5-HT1B and 5-HT1D receptors
- Cause vasoconstriction of dilated intracranial arteries
- Inhibit trigeminal nerve activation and neurogenic inflammation
- Block pain signal transmission at the trigeminal nucleus caudalis

#### Available Triptans

| Drug | Route | Onset | Half-life | Notes |
|------|-------|-------|-----------|-------|
| Sumatriptan | Oral, SC, Nasal, Patch | 30–60 min (oral) | 2 hrs | First-in-class; SC fastest onset (10 min) |
| Rizatriptan | Oral, ODT | 30 min | 2–3 hrs | High efficacy; ODT useful with nausea |
| Eletriptan | Oral | 30 min | 4 hrs | High efficacy, longer duration |
| Zolmitriptan | Oral, Nasal | 45 min | 3 hrs | Nasal useful when vomiting present |
| Almotriptan | Oral | 45–60 min | 3–4 hrs | Good tolerability |
| Naratriptan | Oral | 1–3 hrs | 6 hrs | Slower onset; lower recurrence; gentle side-effect profile |
| Frovatriptan | Oral | 2–4 hrs | 26 hrs | Longest half-life; useful for menstrual migraine |

#### Dosing (Standard Adult)
- **Sumatriptan**: 50–100 mg oral; 6 mg SC; 5–20 mg nasal
- **Rizatriptan**: 5–10 mg oral
- **Eletriptan**: 20–40 mg oral
- **Frovatriptan**: 2.5 mg oral (menstrual migraine: 2.5 mg BID × 6 days)

#### Contraindications
- Ischemic heart disease, coronary vasospasm (Prinzmetal's angina)
- Uncontrolled hypertension
- Hemiplegic or basilar migraine
- Cerebrovascular disease or peripheral vascular disease
- Concurrent MAOI use (serotonin syndrome risk)
- Pregnancy (relative contraindication)

#### Side Effects
- Chest tightness/pressure (non-cardiac), flushing, tingling, dizziness
- Triptan sensations: heaviness, warmth in limbs
- Medication overuse headache (MOH) if used >10 days/month

#### Triptan Non-Responders
- ~30–40% of patients do not respond to the first triptan tried
- Switching to a different triptan or route of administration is recommended
- Combination with an NSAID (e.g., sumatriptan + naproxen) improves outcomes

### 1.2 Ditans (5-HT1F Receptor Agonists)

#### Lasmiditan (Reyvow)
- **Mechanism**: Selective 5-HT1F agonist — no vasoconstriction, neurally-mediated
- **Dose**: 50 mg, 100 mg, or 200 mg oral
- **Onset**: ~1–1.5 hours
- **Key Advantage**: Safe in cardiovascular disease (no vasoconstrictive properties)
- **Side Effects**: Dizziness, somnolence, paresthesia, CNS depression
- **Warning**: Do not drive for ≥8 hours after taking; Schedule V controlled substance (USA)
- **Indication**: Moderate-to-severe migraine with or without aura

---

### 1.3 Gepants (CGRP Receptor Antagonists)

Gepants block CGRP (calcitonin gene-related peptide) at its receptor, targeting the key peptide responsible for migraine pain and vasodilation.

#### Available Gepants for Acute Treatment

| Drug | Dose | Route | Onset | Notes |
|------|------|-------|-------|-------|
| Ubrogepant (Ubrelvy) | 50–100 mg | Oral | ~1.5 hrs | Safe with CV disease; no vasoconstriction |
| Rimegepant (Nurtec ODT) | 75 mg | ODT | ~1.5 hrs | Dual use: acute + preventive (every other day) |

#### Advantages over Triptans
- No risk of MOH (medication overuse headache) — key benefit
- No vasoconstrictive effects → safe in cardiovascular/cerebrovascular disease
- Can be used in hemiplegic migraine

#### Side Effects
- Nausea, somnolence, dry mouth (generally well-tolerated)

#### Drug Interactions
- CYP3A4 inhibitors increase drug levels (e.g., ketoconazole, clarithromycin)
- Strong CYP3A4 inducers (e.g., rifampin) reduce efficacy

---

### 1.4 Ergotamines (Ergot Alkaloids)

Older class; less commonly used due to side effects but still relevant for refractory cases.

#### Dihydroergotamine (DHE)
- **Routes**: IV, IM, SC, Nasal (Migranal), Inhaled (Trudhesa)
- **Mechanism**: Mixed 5-HT, dopamine, and adrenergic agonist; potent vasoconstrictor
- **Indication**: Severe, refractory, or prolonged migraine; status migrainosus
- **Dose**: 0.5–1 mg IV/IM/SC (can repeat up to 3 mg/day); 0.5 mg intranasal per nostril
- **IV Protocol (Raskin Protocol)**: Metoclopramide 10 mg IV + DHE 0.5–1 mg IV q8h × 3 days for status migrainosus
- **Side Effects**: Nausea, vomiting (pretreat with antiemetic), peripheral vasoconstriction, ergotism
- **Contraindications**: CAD, PVD, uncontrolled HTN, pregnancy, concurrent triptans or CYP3A4 inhibitors

#### Ergotamine Tartrate
- Oral/sublingual (often combined with caffeine — Cafergot)
- Less preferred due to variable absorption and higher side effect profile

## 2. Non-Specific Acute Treatments

### 2.1 NSAIDs (Non-Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs)

First-line for mild-to-moderate migraine; also used as adjuncts with triptans.

| Drug | Dose | Notes |
|------|------|-------|
| Ibuprofen | 400–800 mg | First-line; OTC available |
| Naproxen sodium | 500–825 mg | Longer duration; combined with sumatriptan (Treximet) |
| Aspirin | 900–1000 mg | Effective; often combined with metoclopramide |
| Diclofenac potassium | 50–100 mg | Faster absorption vs sodium salt; powder formulation (Cambia) |
| Ketorolac | 15–30 mg IM/IV | For ED/hospital use; rapid parenteral NSAID |
| Indomethacin | 25–50 mg | Also useful for HC (hemicrania continua) |

**Combination**: Aspirin 900 mg + metoclopramide 10 mg = comparable to sumatriptan 50 mg

---

### 2.2 Antiemetics / Neuroleptics

Dual role: treat nausea AND provide analgesic benefit (dopamine antagonists reduce migraine pain centrally).

| Drug | Dose | Route | Notes |
|------|------|-------|-------|
| Metoclopramide | 10 mg | Oral/IV/IM | Also enhances gastric motility → aids drug absorption; IV preferred in ED |
| Prochlorperazine | 5–10 mg | IV/IM/PR | Effective IV monotherapy in ED; watch for akathisia |
| Promethazine | 12.5–25 mg | Oral/IV/IM/PR | Sedating; antiemetic + analgesic |
| Chlorpromazine | 0.1 mg/kg IV | IV | Effective in ED; hypotension risk; prehydrate |
| Droperidol | 2.5 mg IV | IV | High efficacy; QT prolongation risk (ECG monitoring) |
| Haloperidol | 5 mg IV | IV | Evidence-based for ED migraine; slower onset |

**Diphenhydramine 25–50 mg IV** often co-administered to prevent akathisia from dopamine antagonists.

---

### 2.3 Acetaminophen (Paracetamol)
- **Dose**: 1000 mg oral
- Effective for mild migraine
- Useful when NSAIDs contraindicated (renal disease, GI issues, anticoagulation)
- Combination: Acetaminophen 500 mg + Aspirin 500 mg + Caffeine 130 mg (Excedrin Migraine) — OTC, proven effective

---

### 2.4 Combination Analgesics
- **Excedrin Migraine**: Acetaminophen + Aspirin + Caffeine — FDA-approved OTC
- **Treximet**: Sumatriptan 85 mg + Naproxen 500 mg — superior to either alone
- **Cafergot**: Ergotamine + Caffeine — older combination

**Caffeine** (65–200 mg) potentiates analgesics by enhancing absorption and providing vasoconstriction.

---

### 2.5 Opioids / Barbiturates (Discouraged)

- **Butalbital combinations** (Fioricet, Fiorinal): Barbiturate + caffeine ± acetaminophen/aspirin
  - High risk of MOH; associated with chronification of migraine
  - Reserve only when all other options fail
- **Opioids** (codeine, hydrocodone, oxycodone, tramadol):
  - High MOH risk; not recommended for routine migraine management
  - Acceptable in pregnancy when safer options are unavailable
  - Can mask escalating neurological symptoms

---

### 2.6 Corticosteroids
- **Dexamethasone**: 10–25 mg IV or 8–10 mg oral single dose
- Used to **prevent early headache recurrence** (within 24–72 hrs)
- Not effective as primary abortive agent but reduces relapse rate by ~26%
- Often added to ED treatment regimen

## 3. Routes of Administration

| Route | Indication | Examples |
|-------|------------|---------|
| Oral | Standard; mild-moderate | Most triptans, NSAIDs, gepants |
| SC Injection | Rapid onset; severe attacks | Sumatriptan 6 mg SC |
| Intranasal | Nausea/vomiting present; faster than oral | Zolmitriptan, sumatriptan, DHE nasal, zavegepant |
| IV/IM | ED, hospital, refractory migraine | DHE, ketorolac, antiemetics, valproate |
| Suppository/PR | Vomiting, cannot take oral | Prochlorperazine, promethazine |
| Orally Disintegrating | Nausea; faster dissolution | Rizatriptan ODT, rimegepant ODT |
| Transdermal Patch | Motion-sensitive; poor oral tolerance | Sumatriptan iontophoretic patch (Zecuity) |

## 4. Newer/Emerging Acute Agents

### Zavegepant (Zavzpret)
- **Class**: Intranasal gepant (CGRP receptor antagonist)
- **Dose**: 10 mg intranasal
- **FDA Approval**: 2023
- **Benefit**: Fast intranasal route without triptan vasoconstrictive risks

### Celecoxib DFN-15
- Liquid-filled celecoxib capsule with faster absorption for acute migraine

## 5. Special Populations

### Pregnancy
- **First-line**: Acetaminophen 1000 mg
- **Second-line**: Metoclopramide for nausea; antiemetics generally preferred
- **Avoid**: NSAIDs in 3rd trimester (premature ductus arteriosus closure), ergotamines, high-dose opioids
- **Triptans**: Category C; sumatriptan has largest safety registry data — use if benefit outweighs risk

### Menstrual Migraine
- Frovatriptan (long half-life) or naratriptan taken 2 days before and during menses
- Perimenstrual NSAIDs (naproxen) as short-term prophylaxis
- Consider estrogen supplementation around menses (estradiol gel/patch)

### Children and Adolescents
- Ibuprofen 7.5–10 mg/kg (preferred first-line)
- Acetaminophen 15 mg/kg
- Sumatriptan nasal (>12 years): 5–20 mg
- Zolmitriptan nasal (>12 years): 2.5–5 mg
- Rizatriptan oral (>6 years with weight-based dosing)
- Antiemetics: ondansetron preferred (fewer side effects than prochlorperazine in children)

### Elderly Patients
- Prefer NSAIDs with GI protection (PPI co-prescription)
- Triptans with caution (cardiovascular screening mandatory)
- Gepants/ditans preferred if cardiovascular comorbidities

## 6. Emergency Department (ED) Protocols

### Standard ED Migraine Protocol
1. IV access + fluid hydration (NS 1L if dehydrated)
2. **Prochlorperazine 10 mg IV** + **Diphenhydramine 25 mg IV** (to prevent akathisia)
3. Alternative: **Ketorolac 15–30 mg IV** ± antiemetic
4. Refractory: **DHE 0.5–1 mg IV** (after antiemetic pretreatment)
5. Consider: **Dexamethasone 10 mg IV** to reduce 24-hour recurrence
6. Valproate sodium 500–1000 mg IV (evidence-based alternative)
7. Magnesium sulfate 1–2 g IV (especially if with aura)

### Status Migrainosus (>72 hrs)
- IV DHE protocol (Raskin protocol)
- IV steroids
- IV valproate
- Consider brief inpatient admission

## 7. Medication Overuse Headache (MOH)

### Definition
Headache occurring ≥15 days/month as a result of regular overuse of acute headache medications.

### Risk Thresholds
| Medication | MOH Risk Threshold |
|------------|-------------------|
| Triptans, ergots, opioids, combination analgesics | ≥10 days/month |
| Simple analgesics (NSAIDs, acetaminophen) | ≥15 days/month |
| Gepants | No MOH risk established |

### Management
- Withdrawal of offending medication (can cause rebound headache for 1–2 weeks)
- Bridge therapy: NSAIDs, steroids, DHE during withdrawal
- Concurrent initiation of preventive therapy
- Patient education crucial

## 9. Key RAG-Relevant Facts for Migraine Prediction System

- **Early warning signal → acute treatment window**: Prodrome symptoms (yawning, food cravings, neck stiffness) precede headache by 1–48 hours — this is the ideal prediction and preemptive treatment window
- **Aura phase** (if present): Lasts 20–60 minutes; taking triptan at aura onset is controversial but generally recommended just before or at headache onset
- **Best outcomes**: Treating within 30 minutes of pain onset, before central sensitization occurs (cutaneous allodynia onset marks reduced triptan efficacy)
- **Allodynia indicator**: If patient develops skin sensitivity during attack, triptan efficacy drops significantly — switch to DHE or antiemetic-based protocol
- **CGRP levels** rise during prodrome — gepants may theoretically be effective even earlier
- **Recommended maximum frequency**: Triptans ≤2 days/week; gepants safer for more frequent use
