# Preventive (Prophylactic) Migraine Treatments

## Overview

Preventive migraine treatment aims to **reduce the frequency, severity, and duration** of migraine attacks over time. It is distinct from acute treatment and requires daily or regular administration regardless of headache occurrence.

Preventive therapy is an essential complement to early warning prediction systems — identifying high-risk windows can help optimize timing and patient adherence, while effective prevention reduces the overall attack burden that prediction systems must manage.

---

## 1. Indications for Preventive Treatment

Preventive therapy should be considered when:

- **Frequency**: ≥4 migraine days/month
- **Duration**: Attacks lasting >24 hours regularly
- **Disability**: Significant functional impairment (MIDAS score ≥11, HIT-6 ≥56)
- **Acute medication overuse**: Risk or established MOH (medication overuse headache)
- **Inefficacy of acute treatments**: Poor/incomplete response to acute medications
- **Contraindications to acute therapy**: Patient cannot use triptans (CV disease, hemiplegic migraine)
- **Special conditions**: Hemiplegic migraine, migraine with prolonged aura, frequent aura
- **Patient preference**: Patient strongly desires fewer attacks

### Rule of Thumb
> If a patient uses acute medication ≥2 days/week, preventive therapy is indicated.

---

## 2. General Principles

- **Onset of effect**: Most preventive medications require **4–12 weeks** to show full benefit
- **Trial duration**: Minimum 3–6 months before declaring treatment failure
- **Dose escalation**: Start low, titrate slowly to minimize side effects
- **Outcome measure**: Success = ≥50% reduction in migraine days/month
- **Patient diary/tracking**: Critical for assessing efficacy and predicting future attacks
- **Comorbidity-driven selection**: Choose agents that treat both migraine and comorbid conditions (e.g., topiramate for obesity, amitriptyline for depression, propranolol for hypertension)

## 3. First-Line Preventive Medications

### 3.1 Beta-Blockers

Most evidence for **propranolol** and **metoprolol**; considered first-line by most guidelines.

#### Mechanism
- Inhibit central and peripheral β-adrenergic receptors
- Reduce cortical spreading depression susceptibility
- Modulate serotonergic neurotransmission
- Decrease norepinephrine activity in pain pathways

#### Agents and Dosing

| Drug | Starting Dose | Target Dose | Notes |
|------|--------------|-------------|-------|
| Propranolol (IR) | 20 mg BID | 80–240 mg/day | Most studied; Level A evidence |
| Propranolol (ER) | 60 mg QD | 120–240 mg/day | Better adherence |
| Metoprolol succinate | 25–50 mg QD | 100–200 mg/day | Cardioselective; Level A evidence |
| Timolol | 5 mg BID | 10–30 mg/day | FDA-approved for migraine |
| Atenolol | 25 mg QD | 50–100 mg/day | Cardioselective; Level B evidence |
| Nadolol | 20–40 mg QD | 80–240 mg/day | Once daily; long half-life |

#### Efficacy
- ~50% reduction in migraine frequency in 50–70% of patients
- Comparable to topiramate and amitriptyline in head-to-head trials

#### Contraindications
- Asthma / reactive airway disease
- Decompensated heart failure
- AV block (2nd or 3rd degree)
- Raynaud's phenomenon
- Insulin-dependent diabetes (masks hypoglycemia symptoms)

#### Side Effects
- Fatigue, exercise intolerance, cold extremities
- Depression, cognitive slowing, vivid dreams
- Bradycardia, hypotension
- Weight gain

---

### 3.2 Antidepressants

#### Amitriptyline (Tricyclic Antidepressant — TCA)
- **Evidence Level**: Level B (AAN), strong evidence from multiple RCTs
- **Mechanism**: Serotonin/norepinephrine reuptake inhibition; sodium channel blockade; anticholinergic effects
- **Dose**: Start 10–25 mg at bedtime; target 25–150 mg/day
- **Onset**: 4–8 weeks
- **Key Benefit**: Concurrent insomnia, depression, anxiety, chronic daily headache
- **Side Effects**: Sedation, dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, weight gain, QTc prolongation
- **Contraindications**: Recent MI, arrhythmias, glaucoma, BPH, concurrent MAOI use

#### Nortriptyline
- Similar to amitriptyline; slightly better tolerated (less anticholinergic)
- Dose: 10–75 mg at bedtime

#### Venlafaxine (SNRI)
- **Evidence**: Level B
- **Dose**: 37.5–150 mg/day (ER formulation preferred)
- **Benefits**: Comorbid depression or anxiety; less anticholinergic burden
- **Side Effects**: Nausea, insomnia, sexual dysfunction, hypertension at higher doses

#### SSRIs (Fluoxetine, etc.)
- Limited evidence for migraine prevention specifically
- May be appropriate when comorbid depression is primary concern

---

### 3.3 Anticonvulsants (Antiepileptic Drugs — AEDs)

#### Topiramate (Topamax)
- **Evidence**: Level A — FDA-approved for migraine prevention
- **Mechanism**: Multiple: sodium channel blockade, GABA-A potentiation, AMPA/kainate receptor inhibition, carbonic anhydrase inhibition
- **Dose**: Start 25 mg at night; increase by 25 mg/week; target 50–100 mg/day (divided BID)
- **Efficacy**: ~50% reduction in migraine frequency in ~50% of patients
- **Key Benefits**: Weight loss (useful in obese migraineurs), may reduce MOH
- **Side Effects**:
  - Cognitive effects ("Dopamax"): word-finding difficulty, memory impairment, slowed thinking
  - Paresthesias (tingling, especially digits — managed with potassium supplementation)
  - Weight loss (average 2–4 kg)
  - Kidney stones (2–4%)
  - Acute glaucoma (rare but serious — sudden eye pain/visual changes = emergency)
  - Metabolic acidosis
  - Teratogenicity: **Category D — oral cleft defects; avoid in pregnancy**
- **Contraindications**: Nephrolithiasis (relative), pregnancy, cognitive sensitivity

#### Valproate / Divalproex Sodium (Depakote)
- **Evidence**: Level A — FDA-approved for migraine prevention
- **Mechanism**: GABA potentiation, sodium channel blockade, NMDA receptor modulation
- **Dose**: Start 250 mg BID; target 500–1500 mg/day (extended release preferred)
- **Efficacy**: Comparable to topiramate and propranolol
- **Key Benefits**: Comorbid epilepsy, bipolar disorder
- **Side Effects**:
  - Weight gain, alopecia, tremor
  - GI upset, nausea (use enteric-coated ER formulation)
  - Hepatotoxicity (rare; monitor LFTs)
  - Thrombocytopenia, pancreatitis (rare)
- **Contraindications**: **Pregnancy (Category X — neural tube defects, PCOS features, teratogen); women of childbearing age — use with extreme caution; hepatic disease**
- **Monitoring**: LFTs, CBC, valproate levels, ammonia if encephalopathy suspected

#### Gabapentin
- **Evidence**: Level C (weaker evidence base)
- **Dose**: 900–2400 mg/day in divided doses
- **Benefits**: Neuropathic pain comorbidity, anxiety, insomnia
- **Side Effects**: Sedation, dizziness, weight gain, edema

## 4. CGRP-Targeted Preventive Therapies (Novel)

This represents the **most significant advance in migraine prevention in 25 years**.

### 4.1 Anti-CGRP Monoclonal Antibodies (mAbs)

CGRP (calcitonin gene-related peptide) plays a central role in migraine pathophysiology — elevated during attacks, in the trigeminovascular system, and during prodrome phase.

#### Anti-CGRP Ligand Antibodies

| Drug | Brand | Dose | Route | Frequency | Notes |
|------|-------|------|-------|-----------|-------|
| Erenumab | Aimovig | 70–140 mg | SC | Monthly | Anti-CGRP *receptor* antibody (unique) |
| Fremanezumab | Ajovy | 225 mg or 675 mg | SC | Monthly or quarterly | Anti-CGRP *ligand* |
| Galcanezumab | Emgality | 240 mg loading → 120 mg | SC | Monthly | Anti-CGRP ligand; also approved for cluster headache |
| Eptinezumab | Vyepti | 100–300 mg IV | IV infusion | Quarterly | IV only; fastest onset (24–48 hrs) |

#### Key Advantages
- **High efficacy**: 30–50%+ reduction in monthly migraine days (MMDs)
- **Rapid onset**: Benefit often seen within 1 month (eptinezumab within days)
- **Excellent tolerability**: No CNS side effects, no cognitive impairment
- **No need for titration**: Full dose from day 1
- **No MOH risk**
- **Convenient**: Monthly or quarterly dosing
- **Safe in comorbidities**: No renal/hepatic dose adjustment needed

#### Efficacy Data Summary
- ~50% of patients achieve ≥50% reduction in MMDs
- ~25% achieve ≥75% reduction
- ~10–15% achieve ≥100% (complete response)
- Superior to placebo in chronic migraine (≥15 headache days/month)

#### Side Effects
- Injection site reactions (mild; most common)
- Constipation (erenumab — most common with anti-receptor mAb; CGRP involved in GI motility)
- Hypertension (rare; CGRP is vasodilatory)
- Alopecia (rare reports with galcanezumab)

#### Indications
- Episodic migraine (≥4 days/month, inadequate response to 2+ preventive agents)
- Chronic migraine (≥15 headache days/month, ≥8 migraine features)
- Approved regardless of prior preventive failure in some guidelines

#### Special Considerations
- Pregnancy: Avoid (insufficient data; CGRP may have role in fetal vasodilation)
- Not combined with each other
- Response assessment at 3–6 months; discontinue if no ≥30% improvement
- Cost/insurance: Often requires prior authorization and failure of 2 oral preventives

---

### 4.2 Oral Gepants for Prevention

#### Atogepant (Qulipta)
- **Class**: Oral CGRP receptor antagonist (small molecule)
- **Dose**: 10 mg, 30 mg, or 60 mg QD
- **Indication**: Episodic migraine prevention (FDA-approved 2021)
- **Efficacy**: ~30–60% reduction in MMDs vs placebo
- **Advantages**: Oral daily dosing; no MOH risk; suitable for patients who refuse injections
- **Side Effects**: Nausea, constipation, fatigue
- **Drug Interactions**: CYP3A4 inhibitors/inducers

#### Rimegepant (Nurtec ODT)
- **Dual use**: 75 mg ODT every other day for prevention AND as-needed for acute treatment
- **Indication**: Both episodic migraine prevention and acute treatment
- **Unique advantage**: Patients can use same drug for both purposes
- **Efficacy**: ~27% reduction in MMDs vs placebo in prevention trials

## 5. Second-Line and Adjunctive Preventive Agents

### 5.1 Calcium Channel Blockers

#### Flunarizine
- **Evidence**: Level A in European guidelines; not FDA-approved in USA
- **Dose**: 5–10 mg at bedtime
- **Benefits**: Especially effective in migraine with aura; also vestibular migraine
- **Side Effects**: Sedation, weight gain, depression, extrapyramidal effects (tardive dyskinesia with long-term use)
- **Availability**: Available in Europe, Asia, Latin America; not USA/Canada

#### Verapamil
- **Evidence**: Level C for migraine; Level A for cluster headache prevention
- **Dose**: 80 mg TID → up to 480 mg/day
- **Monitoring**: ECG (PR interval; risk of heart block at high doses)

### 5.2 Candesartan (ARB)
- **Evidence**: Level B; comparable to propranolol in RCTs
- **Dose**: 8–16 mg/day
- **Benefits**: Excellent tolerability; no CNS side effects; useful in hypertension
- **Side Effects**: Hypotension, hyperkalemia
- **Preferred in**: Diabetics, renal disease (renoprotective), patients intolerant to beta-blockers

### 5.3 Lisinopril (ACE Inhibitor)
- **Evidence**: Level B
- **Dose**: 10–20 mg/day
- **Side Effects**: ACE inhibitor cough (switch to ARB if cough develops), hyperkalemia
- **Avoid**: Pregnancy (teratogenic)

### 5.4 Magnesium
- **Evidence**: Level B (oral); effective in migraine with aura particularly
- **Mechanism**: NMDA receptor antagonism; reduces cortical spreading depression; cofactor in neurotransmitter regulation
- **Dose**: Magnesium citrate 400–600 mg/day or magnesium glycinate 200–400 mg/day
- **Notes**: Low serum magnesium associated with migraine (especially menstrual); diarrhea limits dose
- **IV Magnesium**: 1–2 g IV for acute migraine in ED (particularly aura patients); also in eclamptic migraine

### 5.5 Riboflavin (Vitamin B2)
- **Evidence**: Level B
- **Mechanism**: Mitochondrial energy metabolism support (migraine linked to mitochondrial dysfunction)
- **Dose**: 400 mg/day
- **Side Effects**: Yellow/orange discoloration of urine (harmless); excellent tolerability
- **Onset**: 3 months for full effect
- **Notes**: OTC supplement; safe in pregnancy; often combined with other preventives

### 5.6 CoQ10 (Coenzyme Q10)
- **Evidence**: Level C
- **Dose**: 100–300 mg/day
- **Mechanism**: Mitochondrial electron transport chain support
- **Notes**: Safe, OTC; often used in combination with riboflavin and magnesium ("nutraceutical trio")

### 5.7 Melatonin
- **Evidence**: Level B (3 mg QHS)
- **Mechanism**: Regulates circadian rhythm; antioxidant; serotonergic activity
- **Dose**: 3 mg at bedtime
- **Notes**: Particularly useful for patients with disrupted sleep-wake cycles as migraine trigger; safe, OTC

## 6. OnabotulinumtoxinA (Botox) for Chronic Migraine

### Indication
- **FDA-approved**: Chronic migraine only (≥15 headache days/month, ≥8 with migraine features)
- **Not indicated**: Episodic migraine

### Mechanism
- Inhibits presynaptic release of CGRP, substance P, and glutamate at peripheral sensory nerve terminals
- Reduces peripheral sensitization → prevents central sensitization

### PREEMPT Protocol (Standard)
- **Total dose**: 155–195 units per session
- **Injection sites**: 31 fixed sites across 7 head/neck muscle groups:
  - Frontalis, Corrugator, Procerus, Occipitalis
  - Temporalis, Trapezius, Cervical paraspinal
- **Frequency**: Every 12 weeks
- **Onset**: 2–4 weeks post-injection
- **Durability**: Benefit increases over multiple injection cycles

### Efficacy
- ~10 fewer headache days/month vs placebo
- ~50% of patients achieve ≥50% reduction in monthly headache days
- Effect cumulative over first 3–5 cycles

### Side Effects
- Injection site pain, bruising
- Neck weakness, ptosis (dose-dependent)
- Dysphagia (if neck injections too deep)
- Generally well-tolerated; no systemic side effects at therapeutic doses

## 7. Non-Pharmacological Preventive Strategies

### 7.1 Lifestyle Modifications
- **Regular sleep**: Fixed wake time, 7–9 hours; irregular sleep is a major trigger
- **Meal regularity**: Avoid skipping meals; maintain stable blood glucose
- **Hydration**: Minimum 2L water/day; dehydration is a common prodrome and trigger
- **Exercise**: Regular aerobic exercise (3×/week, 40 min moderate intensity) shown to equal topiramate in prevention
- **Caffeine management**: Consistent intake (not excessive); sudden cessation triggers rebound headache
- **Stress management**: Daily relaxation routine; identify and address psychosocial stressors
- **Trigger identification**: Headache diary tracking (food, sleep, stress, hormones, weather, sensory stimuli)

### 7.2 Behavioral and Psychological Interventions

#### Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)
- Level A evidence for chronic migraine prevention
- Targets pain catastrophizing, fear-avoidance behaviors, and coping strategies
- Most effective when combined with pharmacological prevention
- Typically 8–12 sessions

#### Biofeedback
- **EMG biofeedback**: Muscle tension reduction via real-time feedback
- **Thermal biofeedback**: Hand-warming technique (vasodilation)
- Level A evidence; particularly useful in pediatric migraine
- Can be self-administered with devices after training

#### Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR)
- 8-week structured program
- Evidence Level B; reduces headache frequency and migraine-related disability
- Particularly effective in patients with anxiety comorbidity

#### Relaxation Therapy
- Progressive muscle relaxation, autogenic training
- Reduces sympathetic arousal and stress-related triggers
- Level B evidence

### 7.3 Neuromodulation Devices

Non-pharmacological devices targeting migraine neurocircuitry.

| Device | Target | Type | Indication | Evidence |
|--------|--------|------|------------|---------|
| Cefaly | Supraorbital nerve (V1) | Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) | Episodic migraine prevention + acute | Level A |
| gammaCore (nVNS) | Vagus nerve (cervical) | Non-invasive vagus nerve stimulation | Episodic + cluster headache | Level B |
| SpringTMS / eNeura | Occipital cortex | Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) | Migraine with aura prevention + acute | Level B |
| Nerivio | Arm (brachial) | Remote electrical neuromodulation | Episodic migraine prevention + acute | Level B |
| SAVI Dual | Supraorbital + occipital | Dual TENS | Prevention | Emerging |

## 8. Preventive Treatment by Migraine Subtype

### Episodic Migraine (< 15 days/month)
- First-line: Propranolol, topiramate, amitriptyline, valproate
- Second-line: Candesartan, venlafaxine, magnesium, riboflavin
- CGRP mAb if 2+ first-line failures

### Chronic Migraine (≥ 15 days/month)
- OnabotulinumtoxinA (Botox) — FDA-approved specifically
- CGRP mAbs (all four approved)
- Topiramate
- Combination therapy (e.g., Botox + CGRP mAb — increasingly used)

### Menstrual Migraine
- Perimenstrual prophylaxis: Frovatriptan, naratriptan, or naproxen 500 mg BID (2 days before and during menses)
- Hormonal: Estrogen supplements (estradiol patch/gel) around menses
- Long-cycle or continuous OCP (avoid hormone-free week)
- Magnesium during luteal phase

### Vestibular Migraine
- Flunarizine (most evidence)
- Propranolol, amitriptyline
- Venlafaxine (particularly if anxiety)
- Betahistine (adjunctive)

### Hemiplegic Migraine
- Avoid triptans, ergotamines
- Use: Flunarizine, verapamil, acetazolamide
- CGRP mAbs: Emerging evidence; avoid in severe neurological aura until more data

### Migraine with Brainstem Aura (Basilar-type)
- Avoid vasoconstrictors (triptans, ergotamines)
- Valproate, verapamil, topiramate

## 9. Monitoring and Outcome Assessment

### Tools for Measuring Prevention Efficacy
- **MIDAS (Migraine Disability Assessment)**: Measures headache-related disability (Grade I–IV)
- **HIT-6 (Headache Impact Test)**: 6-question quality-of-life impact score
- **Monthly Migraine Days (MMDs)**: Primary outcome in clinical trials (aim: ≥50% reduction)
- **Headache Diary**: Essential for tracking frequency, severity, triggers, medication use
- **Patient Global Impression of Change (PGIC)**: Self-reported treatment benefit

### Monitoring Requirements by Drug

| Drug | Monitoring |
|------|-----------|
| Valproate | LFTs, CBC, drug levels, ammonia |
| Topiramate | Serum bicarbonate, kidney stones, eye pressure |
| Beta-blockers | Heart rate, blood pressure, depression symptoms |
| Verapamil | ECG (PR interval), BP |
| CGRP mAbs | Injection site reactions, constipation, BP |
| Amitriptyline | ECG (QTc), weight, anticholinergic symptoms |

---

## 10. Combining Preventive Agents

### Evidence-Based Combinations
- **Topiramate + CGRP mAb**: Additive efficacy; commonly used
- **Botox + CGRP mAb**: Growing evidence for chronic migraine; superior to either alone
- **Beta-blocker + TCA**: Complementary mechanisms; monitor for additive bradycardia/hypotension
- **Magnesium + Riboflavin + CoQ10**: "Nutraceutical trio" — safe, additive, OTC
- **Oral preventive + neuromodulation device**: Additive and well-tolerated

---

## 11. Discontinuation of Preventive Therapy

- Consider tapering after **6–12 months** of successful prevention
- Taper gradually to avoid rebound
- Criteria for stopping: ≥50% reduction maintained; patient prefers trial off medication
- CGRP mAbs: Consider pausing after 12 months to assess ongoing need
- Botox: Reassess after 2 years of continuous treatment

---

## 12. Key RAG-Relevant Facts for Migraine Early Warning System

- **Preventive therapy reduces baseline attack frequency** — the prediction system must account for current preventive medication as a baseline modifier
- **CGRP is elevated during prodrome** — CGRP mAb users may have blunted prodromal biomarker signals
- **Sleep disruption overrides prevention** — even well-controlled patients may break through during circadian disturbances
- **Hormonal cyclicity** — preventive efficacy often lower perimenstrually; prediction model should incorporate menstrual cycle phase
- **Stress as breakthrough trigger** — even on preventives, psychological stressors can precipitate attacks
- **CGRP mAbs work continuously** — no bolus effect; steady-state drug levels achieved by week 4 (mAbs) or day 1–3 (eptinezumab IV)
- **Atogepant/rimegepant as prevention** — oral gepant use every-other-day changes CGRP dynamics relevant to biomarker-based prediction
- **Exercise as prevention** — regular aerobic exercise reduces CSD susceptibility and CGRP sensitization
