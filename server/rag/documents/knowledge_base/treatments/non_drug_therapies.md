# Non-Drug Therapies for Migraine

## Overview

Non-pharmacological (non-drug) therapies play a critical and often underutilized role in comprehensive migraine management. They serve as **preventive interventions, acute relief measures, adjuncts to pharmacotherapy, and long-term disease modifiers**. For a RAG-based migraine early warning prediction system, non-drug therapies are especially relevant because many directly target the **neurobiological mechanisms** implicated in migraine prediction — cortical excitability, autonomic dysregulation, circadian rhythm, stress response, and sensory sensitization.

Non-drug approaches are classified into:
1. **Neuromodulation devices**
2. **Behavioral and psychological therapies**
3. **Physical therapies and manual interventions**
4. **Lifestyle interventions and trigger management**
5. **Mind-body practices**
6. **Dietary and nutritional approaches**
7. **Complementary and integrative medicine**
8. **Interventional procedures (non-pharmacological component)**

---

## 1. Neuromodulation Devices

Neuromodulation is the most evidence-rich non-drug category for migraine, with multiple FDA-cleared devices now available.

### 1.1 Transcutaneous Supraorbital Nerve Stimulation (tSNS)

#### Cefaly Device
- **Target**: Supraorbital and supratrochlear branches of the ophthalmic nerve (V1 of trigeminal)
- **Mechanism**: External electrical stimulation desensitizes trigeminal pain pathways; reduces cortical excitability; modulates thalamo-cortical connectivity
- **FDA Status**: FDA-authorized (De Novo) — both acute (2021) and preventive (2014) use
- **Preventive Protocol**: 20 minutes daily at 60 Hz, 250 μs pulse width, 16 mA
- **Acute Protocol**: 60-minute session at 100 Hz at headache onset
- **Efficacy (Preventive)**: ~37–50% reduction in migraine days/month; ~53% responder rate
- **Efficacy (Acute)**: ~60% abortive success within 1 hour; reduces need for rescue medication
- **Side Effects**: Forehead skin irritation, paresthesia, drowsiness (mild); excellent safety profile
- **Contraindications**: Implanted metal/electronic devices in head region
- **Patient Profile**: Particularly useful for patients who dislike injections or oral medications
- **Relevance to Prediction System**: Cefaly-preventive mode measurably changes cortical excitability — EEG habituation patterns normalize with regular use; baseline excitability state is relevant to predicting breakthrough attacks

---

### 1.2 Non-Invasive Vagus Nerve Stimulation (nVNS)

#### gammaCore (electroCore)
- **Target**: Left cervical vagus nerve trunk (anterior neck)
- **Mechanism**: Activates vagal afferents → reduces trigeminovascular activation; modulates locus coeruleus, nucleus tractus solitarius; suppresses cortical spreading depression (CSD); reduces CGRP and glutamate release
- **FDA Status**: FDA-authorized for acute and preventive treatment of episodic and chronic migraine; cluster headache
- **Acute Protocol**: 2-minute stimulation applied bilaterally at headache onset (can repeat × 3 per episode)
- **Preventive Protocol**: 2 minutes × 3 times daily
- **Stimulation Parameters**: 25 Hz; 1 ms pulse width; 0–24 V
- **Efficacy (Acute)**: ~30% pain-free at 30 min (vs 14% placebo) in episodic migraine
- **Efficacy (Preventive)**: ~1–2 fewer migraine days/month vs sham
- **Side Effects**: Neck muscle twitching, voice change during stimulation, local skin irritation; rare dizziness
- **Contraindications**: Implanted electronic devices (pacemakers, defibrillators), carotid artery disease, vasovagal syncope history
- **Relevance to Prediction System**: Vagal tone (measured by HRV — heart rate variability) is a known predictor of migraine susceptibility; nVNS normalizes autonomic balance — HRV changes with device use may serve as a monitoring biomarker

---

### 1.3 Single-Pulse Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (sTMS)

#### SpringTMS / eNeura (sTMS mini)
- **Target**: Occipital cortex / V1 visual cortex
- **Mechanism**: Single-pulse TMS delivered to posterior head generates brief magnetic field → interrupts cortical spreading depression propagation; inhibits visual cortex hyperexcitability underlying aura
- **FDA Status**: FDA-cleared for acute and preventive migraine (with and without aura)
- **Acute Protocol**: 2 pulses at aura onset or pain onset; up to 3 sets per episode
- **Preventive Protocol**: 4 pulses twice daily (morning and evening)
- **Efficacy (Acute — Aura)**: ~39% pain-free at 2 hrs vs 22% sham (ESPOUSE trial)
- **Efficacy (Preventive)**: ~44% responder rate (≥50% reduction)
- **Side Effects**: Mild tingling/twitching at application site; rare tinnitus
- **Contraindications**: Epilepsy, cochlear implants, ferromagnetic head implants, pregnancy
- **Optimal Use**: Most effective for migraine with aura — applied at aura onset
- **Relevance to Prediction System**: Aura is a key early warning signal; a system predicting aura onset can trigger timely sTMS application, potentially aborting the migraine before headache develops

---

### 1.4 Remote Electrical Neuromodulation (REN)

#### Nerivio (Theranica)
- **Target**: Upper arm (brachial nerve); conditioned pain modulation (CPM) pathway
- **Mechanism**: Activates descending inhibitory pain pathways via CPM — remote stimulation of the arm triggers endogenous pain inhibition in trigeminal nucleus caudalis
- **FDA Status**: FDA-authorized for acute and preventive use in adults and adolescents (≥12 years) with and without aura
- **Acute Protocol**: 45-minute session (Bluetooth-controlled via smartphone) at headache onset
- **Preventive Protocol**: 45 minutes every other day (preventive app mode)
- **Efficacy (Acute)**: ~66% pain relief at 2 hrs; ~37% pain freedom at 2 hrs (CLASS-3 trial)
- **Efficacy (Preventive)**: ~4 fewer migraine days/month
- **Side Effects**: Paresthesia, warmth at arm device; minimal
- **Advantages**: Non-invasive wearable; app-controlled; no head device needed; adolescent-approved; can be used during pregnancy
- **Relevance to Prediction System**: Smartphone connectivity = potential data source for usage patterns, attack frequency trends, and treatment response data feeding into prediction model

---

### 1.5 Transcutaneous Occipital Nerve Stimulation

#### Relivion (Neurolief)
- **Target**: Supraorbital + occipital nerves simultaneously (bilateral; dual-site)
- **FDA Breakthrough Device Designation**: Received 2020
- **Mechanism**: Combined trigeminal and occipital nerve neuromodulation
- **Acute Protocol**: 20–40 minute session at migraine onset
- **Emerging Evidence**: RIME trial showed significant acute efficacy; full FDA clearance process ongoing

---

### 1.6 Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation (tDCS)
- **Mechanism**: Low-level direct current modulates cortical excitability (anodal = excitatory; cathodal = inhibitory)
- **Target**: Motor cortex or visual cortex
- **Evidence Level**: Preliminary (Level C); research-stage for migraine
- **Dose**: 1–2 mA, 15–30 minutes per session

## 2. Behavioral and Psychological Therapies

### 2.1 Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)

#### Overview
CBT is the most evidence-based psychological intervention for migraine, targeting the thoughts, behaviors, and coping strategies that influence migraine frequency and disability.

#### Mechanism
- Reduces pain catastrophizing (magnifying pain, perceived helplessness)
- Addresses avoidance behaviors that perpetuate disability
- Teaches active coping strategies (vs passive: lying in dark, canceling activities)
- Reduces allodynia-promoting stress responses via HPA-axis regulation
- Improves self-efficacy and treatment adherence

#### Evidence
- **Level A**: Multiple RCTs; equivalent to topiramate at 6 months when combined with pharmacotherapy
- **Most Effective**: Combined CBT + pharmacological prevention > either alone (MMPP trial)
- **Format**: 8–12 weekly sessions; individual or group; increasingly available via telehealth

#### Core CBT Techniques for Migraine
1. **Headache Diary Analysis**: Identifying cognitive and behavioral triggers
2. **Cognitive Restructuring**: Challenging catastrophic thoughts about pain
3. **Activity Pacing**: Balancing activity and rest (avoiding boom-bust cycles)
4. **Behavioral Activation**: Gradual return to avoided activities despite pain
5. **Sleep Hygiene CBT-I**: Insomnia-focused CBT component (sleep is a major trigger)
6. **Problem-Solving Therapy**: Addressing life stressors fueling migraine chronification

#### Relevance to Prediction System
- Catastrophizing scores (PCS — Pain Catastrophizing Scale) correlate with attack frequency
- CBT-related behavioral markers (sleep regularity, activity pacing) are measurable signals for prediction models

---

### 2.2 Biofeedback

#### Types
| Type | Mechanism | Efficacy |
|------|-----------|---------|
| Thermal (hand-warming) | Peripheral vasodilation to counteract cephalic vasodilation | Level A |
| EMG (muscle tension) | Reduces pericranial/neck muscle tension (a trigger and prodromal symptom) | Level A |
| EEG/Neurofeedback | Trains cortical alpha/theta patterns; reduces hyperexcitability | Level B |
| HRV Biofeedback | Increases vagal tone; restores autonomic balance | Level B |

#### Thermal Biofeedback
- Patient learns to raise hand temperature by 2–4°F via relaxation
- Counter-intuitive: induces cephalic vasoconstriction as compensatory response
- Highly effective in pediatric migraine; equal efficacy to propranolol (meta-analysis)

#### EMG Biofeedback
- Surface EMG sensors on frontalis, temporalis, trapezius
- Visual/auditory feedback allows voluntary muscle relaxation
- Particularly effective when neck/shoulder tension is predominant trigger

#### HRV Biofeedback
- Targets respiratory sinus arrhythmia at resonant frequency (~0.1 Hz, ~6 breaths/min)
- Increases baroreflex sensitivity and vagal tone
- Relevance to prediction system: **HRV is a measurable wearable biomarker** (smartwatches, fitness trackers) — reduced HRV 24–72 hours before attack is an early warning signal

---

### 2.3 Mindfulness-Based Interventions

#### Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR)
- Structured 8-week program; 2.5 hrs/week group sessions + 45 min/day home practice
- Includes: body scan, sitting meditation, mindful movement (yoga), mindful awareness of pain
- **Evidence**: Level B — significant reduction in migraine frequency, depression, pain catastrophizing
- **Mechanism**: Reduces emotional reactivity to pain; alters default mode network activity; downregulates amygdala response to pain signals

#### Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT)
- Combines MBSR with CBT cognitive techniques
- Particularly effective in patients with comorbid depression and migraine

#### Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)
- Focuses on psychological flexibility; accepting pain without struggle
- Reduces disability and catastrophizing even when headache frequency unchanged

---

### 2.4 Relaxation Therapies

| Technique | Description | Evidence |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| Progressive Muscle Relaxation (PMR) | Sequential tensing and releasing muscle groups; reduces physical tension | Level A |
| Autogenic Training | Self-induced warmth and heaviness sensations; autonomic balance | Level B |
| Diaphragmatic Breathing | Slow deep breathing (4-7-8 or box breathing); increases HRV | Level B |
| Guided Imagery | Visualization of peaceful/cooling scenes; pain diversion | Level C |

#### Recommendation
PMR combined with thermal biofeedback has synergistic efficacy and is considered first-line behavioral treatment for pediatric migraine.

---

### 2.5 Acceptance of Chronic Pain Framework
- **Key concept**: Migraine is a chronic neurological disorder, not a personal failure
- Reducing struggle against pain (which amplifies CNS sensitization) can reduce attack impact even when frequency is unchanged
- Relevant for patients with high disability and anxiety

## 3. Physical Therapies and Manual Interventions

### 3.1 Physical Therapy (Physiotherapy)

#### Cervicogenic Headache vs Migraine Overlap
- Cervicogenic factors (C1–C3 joint dysfunction, myofascial trigger points, suboccipital muscle tightness) can **trigger or exacerbate migraine** via trigemino-cervical complex convergence
- Physical therapy targeting cervical spine is evidence-based for this subgroup

#### Manual Techniques
- **High-velocity low-amplitude (HVLA) manipulation**: Cervical/thoracic joint manipulation
- **Mobilization**: Gentle joint movement (safer, less force than manipulation)
- **Myofascial release**: Soft tissue work on pericranial muscles, trapezius, sternocleidomastoid
- **Trigger point therapy**: Dry needling or manual pressure on active trigger points (temporalis, suboccipital, trapezius, SCM)
- **Cervical traction**: Mechanical or manual; decompresses upper cervical facets

#### Evidence
- Level B for manual therapy in migraine prevention
- Most effective in patients with associated neck pain, cervicogenic headache, or postural abnormalities
- RCT: 6 weeks of physical therapy = equivalent to topiramate in migraine frequency reduction

#### Postural Assessment and Correction
- Forward head posture (FHP) and upper cross syndrome increase pericranial muscle tension
- Ergonomic corrections (screen height, chair position, pillow type) — often overlooked migraine contributors

---

### 3.2 Acupuncture

- **Evidence**: Level A (Cochrane review 2016; updated 2022) — superior to sham acupuncture and equivalent to topiramate for prevention
- **Mechanism (proposed)**: Modulates serotonin, opioid, and CGRP pathways; activates periaqueductal gray (PAG) pain modulation; reduces cortical excitability
- **Protocol**: 6–12 sessions over 8–12 weeks; each session 30–45 minutes; retained needles at traditional points + trigger points
- **Key Points for Migraine**: GB20 (Fengchi), LI4 (Hegu), ST36 (Zusanli), GV14, Yin Tang, BL10
- **Side Effects**: Minimal — minor bruising, transient soreness; serious adverse events extremely rare
- **Guideline Status**: Recommended by NICE (UK), EHF, and WHO for migraine prevention
- **Relevance to Prediction System**: Acupuncture normalizes cortical excitability (EEG measures) and serotonin levels — regular users may have different baseline biomarker profiles

---

### 3.3 Massage Therapy

- **Types effective for migraine**: Craniosacral therapy, myofascial massage, suboccipital release, scalp massage
- **Evidence**: Level B — reduces migraine frequency, pericranial muscle tenderness, anxiety
- **Mechanism**: Reduces pericranial myofascial tension; increases serotonin; reduces cortisol; promotes parasympathetic activity
- **Session Frequency**: 1–2×/week for 4–6 weeks; then maintenance monthly
- **Self-massage**: Temporal, suboccipital, and trapezius self-massage 5–10 min/day feasible

---

### 3.4 Trigger Point Dry Needling (TDN)

- Differs from acupuncture in targeting myofascial trigger points (hyperirritable nodules in taut muscle bands)
- Administered by trained physiotherapists
- Particularly useful for pericranial and cervical muscle trigger points associated with migraine
- **Evidence**: Level B — reduces trigger point sensitivity and migraine frequency

---

### 3.5 Occipital Nerve Blocks (ONBs)

While technically injectable, no pharmacological preventive intent — used as interventional non-drug procedure:
- **Injection**: Local anesthetic (lidocaine 1%) ± corticosteroid at greater/lesser occipital nerve
- **Indication**: Refractory chronic migraine; bridge therapy; status migrainosus; menstrual migraine
- **Mechanism**: Modulates trigemino-cervical complex; reduces peripheral and central sensitization
- **Duration of Effect**: Variable; typically 4–12 weeks
- **Frequency**: Maximum every 3–4 months (if steroid-containing); can repeat local anesthetic more frequently

## 4. Lifestyle Interventions and Trigger Management

### 4.1 Sleep Optimization

Sleep is bidirectionally related to migraine — poor sleep triggers migraine, and migraine disrupts sleep.

#### Key Principles
- **Fixed wake time**: Most critical — consistent wake time anchors circadian rhythm
- **Duration**: 7–9 hours; oversleeping ("weekend migraine") and undersleeping both trigger attacks
- **Sleep hygiene**: No screens 1 hour before bed; dark cool quiet room; no caffeine after 2 PM
- **CBT-I**: Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia — superior to sleep medications long-term

#### Sleep Disorders and Migraine
| Disorder | Association |
|----------|------------|
| Insomnia | Bidirectional; increases attack frequency |
| Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA) | Strong association; CPAP treatment reduces migraine |
| Restless Legs Syndrome (RLS) | Comorbid; iron/dopamine pathway overlap |
| Bruxism (teeth grinding) | Pericranial tension; refer to dentist for nightguard |
| Circadian rhythm disorders | Night shift workers — high migraine prevalence |

#### Relevance to Prediction System
- **Sleep quality metrics** (wearable data: sleep duration, sleep stage, HRV during sleep) are among the strongest early warning predictors of next-day migraine — integrating wearable sleep data is high-value for prediction models

---

### 4.2 Regular Aerobic Exercise

- **Evidence**: Level A — 3×/week moderate-intensity aerobic exercise equals topiramate in migraine prevention (Varkey RCT, 2011)
- **Types**: Brisk walking, swimming, cycling, jogging — any sustained aerobic activity
- **Protocol**: 40 minutes, 3 times/week; gradual intensity increase
- **Mechanism**:
  - Increases beta-endorphin and BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor)
  - Reduces CGRP baseline levels
  - Improves HRV (vagal tone)
  - Reduces cortisol and stress reactivity
  - Enhances sleep quality
  - Reduces obesity (BMI is a migraine chronification risk factor)
- **Caution**: Intense exercise can trigger migraine in some patients during an active attack; warm up gradually; stay well-hydrated
- **Exercise Avoidance Cycle**: Many migraineurs avoid exercise fearing attacks — breaking this cycle is a key behavioral intervention goal

### 4.3 Dietary Approaches and Trigger Foods

#### Common Dietary Migraine Triggers
| Trigger | Prevalence | Proposed Mechanism |
|---------|------------|-------------------|
| Alcohol (red wine, beer) | ~33% | Histamine, tyramine, congeners, dehydration |
| Caffeine withdrawal | Very common | Adenosine rebound after dependency |
| Aged cheese | ~10–15% | Tyramine |
| Processed meats | ~5–10% | Nitrates, tyramine |
| MSG (monosodium glutamate) | ~10% | Glutamate receptor activation |
| Chocolate | ~5–10% | Phenylethylamine, caffeine, sugar |
| Artificial sweeteners (aspartame) | Low-moderate | Phenylalanine metabolism |
| Citrus fruits | ~11% | Octopamine, histamine |
| Skipped meals / fasting | High | Hypoglycemia → cortical excitability ↑ |
| Dehydration | Very high | Reduced cerebral perfusion, electrolyte shifts |

**Important**: Trigger foods are highly individual and should be identified via headache diary, not presumed. Over-restriction can cause nutritional deficiency and heighten anxiety around eating.

#### Low-Tyramine Diet
- Reduces dietary vasoactive amines
- Evidence for effectiveness is moderate; best for patients with clear food-related triggers
- Foods to limit: aged/fermented cheeses, red wine, beer, cured meats, soy sauce, miso

#### High-Omega-3 / Low-Omega-6 Diet (Dietary Fatty Acid Modification)
- **Evidence**: RCT (Ramsden et al., 2021) — high omega-3, low omega-6 diet significantly reduced headache days vs control
- **Mechanism**: Reduces pro-inflammatory prostaglandins; shifts oxylipins toward anti-nociceptive profiles
- **Practical**: Increase fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines); reduce vegetable oils (corn, soybean oil)

#### Low-Glycemic Index Diet
- Prevents blood sugar fluctuations that trigger cortical excitability
- Emphasizes: whole grains, lean protein, fiber-rich vegetables
- Avoid: refined carbohydrates, sugary beverages, long fasting intervals

#### Ketogenic Diet
- **Evidence**: Emerging; small studies show significant reduction in migraine frequency (possibly reduces cortical excitability via ketone-mediated changes)
- Mechanism: Beta-hydroxybutyrate may reduce glutamate; anti-inflammatory; mitochondrial optimization
- **Challenge**: Strict adherence required; "keto flu" during transition; not for everyone

#### Hydration
- Daily target: 2–2.5 liters water/day
- Dehydration equivalent to 2% body weight loss can precipitate migraine
- **Wearable relevance**: Hydration tracking apps + prediction systems can flag dehydration as risk signal

### 4.4 Trigger Identification and Management

#### Headache Diary
- Gold standard tool for trigger identification
- Track: date/time, duration, severity (0–10), location, associated symptoms, potential triggers (sleep, food, stress, hormones, weather, activity, medications)
- Digital diary apps (Migraine Buddy, Curelator, N1-Headache) offer automated trigger analysis

#### Common Non-Food Triggers

| Category | Specific Triggers |
|----------|------------------|
| Hormonal | Menstruation (estrogen drop), ovulation, OCP hormone-free week, menopause |
| Environmental | Bright/flickering light (photic trigger), loud noise, strong odors (perfume, smoke) |
| Meteorological | Barometric pressure drops, high humidity, hot/dry wind (Chinook, Foehn), thunderstorms |
| Psychological | Acute stress, "let-down" headache (weekend after work stress), anxiety, emotional upset |
| Physical | Intense exercise (exertional migraine), head trauma, prolonged posture |
| Sensory | Strong visual patterns, 3D screens, VR, blue light |
| Sleep | Sleep deprivation, oversleeping, jet lag, shift work |

#### "Let-Down" Migraine
- Attacks occurring on days off, weekends, or post-deadline
- Caused by rapid cortisol drop after sustained stress → adenosine surge
- Management: Avoid sharp stress transitions; maintain moderate weekend schedule

#### Trigger Stacking (Threshold Model)
- No single trigger causes migraine consistently; **cumulative threshold model** applies
- Multiple sub-threshold triggers simultaneously exceed the individual's migraine threshold
- **Prediction System Application**: Monitoring multiple concurrent trigger signals (poor sleep + high stress + hormonal phase + weather change) enables threshold-based attack probability calculation

## 5. Mind-Body Practices

### 5.1 Yoga
- **Evidence**: Level B — multiple RCTs show significant migraine frequency reduction
- **Protocol**: 3–5 sessions/week; 45–60 minutes; combination of postures, breathing (pranayama), and meditation
- **Mechanism**: Reduces cortisol and sympathetic activation; improves HRV; reduces pericranial muscle tension; promotes regular sleep
- **Best Postures**: Forward bends (child's pose, downward dog), supported inversions with caution; avoid headstands during active migraine
- **Contraindications**: Active migraine attack; certain inversions with hypertension

### 5.2 Tai Chi / Qigong
- Slow movement, breath coordination, mindfulness
- **Evidence**: Emerging; improves HRV, reduces stress-related migraine triggers
- Suitable for elderly, deconditioned, or severely disabled patients

### 5.3 Hypnotherapy
- **Evidence**: Level C; limited RCT data
- Most evidence in pediatric migraine: self-hypnosis + thermal biofeedback shown effective
- Mechanism: Alters pain perception through attentional redirection

## 6. Dietary Supplements and Nutraceuticals

Supplements with clinically meaningful evidence for migraine prevention.

| Supplement | Dose | Evidence | Mechanism |
|-----------|------|----------|-----------|
| Magnesium (citrate/glycinate) | 400–600 mg/day | Level B | NMDA antagonism; reduces CSD; deficiency common in migraineurs |
| Riboflavin (Vitamin B2) | 400 mg/day | Level B | Mitochondrial energy metabolism |
| Coenzyme Q10 | 100–300 mg/day | Level C | Mitochondrial electron transport |
| Melatonin | 3 mg at bedtime | Level B | Circadian regulation; serotonin precursor |
| Feverfew (Tanacetum parthenium) | 50–150 mg/day | Level B | Platelet aggregation inhibition; serotonin release inhibition |
| Butterbur (Petasites hybridus) | 50–75 mg BID | Level A | Reduces CGRP; anti-inflammatory; PA-free extract only (hepatotoxicity risk with pyrrolizidine alkaloids) |
| Ginger (Zingiber officinale) | 250–500 mg | Level B | Acute nausea; possible anti-inflammatory; comparable to sumatriptan in one RCT |
| Vitamin D | 1000–2000 IU/day | Level C | Deficiency associated with migraine; supplement if deficient |
| Alpha-lipoic acid | 600 mg/day | Level C | Antioxidant; mitochondrial cofactor |
| Omega-3 fatty acids | 1–3 g EPA+DHA/day | Level B | Anti-inflammatory oxylipins; reduces CGRP release |

**Safety Note**: Even "natural" supplements can interact with medications. Butterbur must be PA-free certified; feverfew can cause rebound headache on withdrawal.

## 7. Complementary and Integrative Medicine

### 7.1 Green Light Therapy
- **Evidence**: Emerging (Burstein lab, Harvard); pilot RCTs positive
- **Mechanism**: Green light wavelength (~530 nm) uniquely activates intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells in a way that inhibits trigeminal activation and reduces photophobia; narrow-band green light may reduce nociceptor activity
- **Protocol**: 1–2 hours daily exposure to pure green light (Allay Lamp); dim otherwise
- **Applications**: Both acute (during attack — reduces photophobia) and preventive
- **Advantage**: Completely non-pharmacological; safe in all populations including pregnancy

### 7.2 Blue Light Blocking / Light Management
- **Photophobia** (light sensitivity) is common interictal and worsens during attacks
- Blue light (400–490 nm) maximally stimulates melanopsin (ipRGCs) → activates trigeminovascular pain
- **Interventions**: Blue-light blocking glasses (FL-41 tint), screen filters, dimmer lighting environments
- **Evidence**: Level B — FL-41 tinted lenses significantly reduce photophobia and migraine frequency

### 7.3 Aromatherapy
- **Lavender oil**: Inhaled; RCT showed significant reduction in headache severity during acute migraine
- **Peppermint oil**: Topical application (temples, forehead); menthol activates TRPM8 channels → counteracts pain signaling; comparable to acetaminophen in one RCT
- **Evidence**: Level C — limited but positive; mechanism-plausible

### 7.4 TENS (Transcutaneous Electrical Nerve Stimulation)
- Different from neuromodulation devices above; portable home TENS units for neck/cervical
- **Target**: Cervical nerve roots; reduces referred pain to head via gate control theory
- **Evidence**: Level C for migraine specifically; stronger evidence for cervicogenic headache

## 8. Interventional Non-Pharmacological Procedures

### 8.1 Sphenopalatine Ganglion (SPG) Blocks
- **Target**: SPG — parasympathetic ganglion behind nasal cavity
- **Non-drug route**: Intranasal device (Tx360 catheter) delivers local anesthetic (lidocaine 4%) to SPG via transnasal approach — no injection needle
- **Indication**: Acute refractory migraine; cluster headache; chronic migraine prevention
- **Evidence**: Level B — significant acute pain relief; preventive benefit with repeated procedures
- **Mechanism**: Blocks parasympathetic outflow that drives cranial vasodilation and neuroinflammation

### 8.2 TENS-Based SPG Stimulation
- Externally applied device at lateral face; transcutaneous SPG stimulation
- Emerging evidence; non-invasive alternative to SPG block procedures

## 10. Special Populations — Non-Drug Preference

### 10.1 Pregnancy
- **Preferred**: All behavioral therapies (CBT, biofeedback, relaxation, mindfulness)
- **Safe devices**: Cefaly, Nerivio (limited data; generally considered safe), nVNS (limited data)
- **Contraindicated devices**: sTMS (insufficient safety data)
- **Lifestyle**: Sleep optimization, hydration, dietary trigger management, exercise (moderate)
- **Supplements**: Magnesium (safe), riboflavin (safe), melatonin (caution), avoid butterbur
- **Avoidance**: All CGRP-targeted medications; topiramate; valproate; ergotamines

### 10.2 Children and Adolescents
- **First-line**: Behavioral therapies (biofeedback, CBT, relaxation) — equally effective to pharmacotherapy; preferred to minimize medication exposure
- **Devices**: Nerivio (FDA-approved ≥12 yrs), Cefaly (adolescent use growing)
- **Exercise and sleep**: Particularly high-impact in adolescents
- **School interventions**: Accommodation letters; trigger management in school environment

### 10.3 Elderly
- Behavioral therapies well-tolerated and effective regardless of age
- Exercise (gentle aerobic; water aerobics) particularly valuable
- Device-based therapies preferred to reduce polypharmacy

## 11. Integration with Migraine Early Warning Prediction System

Non-drug therapies generate and respond to multiple prediction-relevant variables:

### Biomarker Signals Relevant to Prediction

| Signal | Source | Relevance |
|--------|--------|-----------|
| Heart Rate Variability (HRV) | Wearable (Apple Watch, Garmin, Fitbit, Oura) | Reduced HRV 24–72 hrs before attack; biofeedback normalizes it |
| Sleep duration/quality | Wearable + self-report | Disrupted sleep = strongest single predictor in many patients |
| Resting heart rate | Wearable | Elevated RHR can predict next-day migraine |
| Physical activity level | Wearable | Low activity days correlated with higher attack probability |
| Skin temperature | Wearable | Peripheral vasoconstriction during prodrome detectable |
| Stress markers (EDA/GSR) | Wearable | Elevated electrodermal activity = stress arousal → attack risk |
| Barometric pressure | Weather API | Drops correlate with attack onset in weather-sensitive patients |
| Menstrual cycle phase | Patient app tracking | High-risk window: 2 days before to day 2 of menstruation |
| Screen exposure (blue light) | Phone usage data | Excess late-night blue light → sleep disruption → migraine cascade |
| Caffeine intake pattern | Self-report | Withdrawal timing predictable from intake logs |

### Prediction-Relevant Behavioral Triggers to Monitor
- Sleep timing irregularity (>1 hour deviation from normal)
- Meal skipping (fasting >5 hours)
- Physical inactivity (steps below individual baseline)
- Alcohol consumption
- Dehydration (low fluid intake logged)
- High psychological stress self-report
- Sedentary screen time spike

### Non-Drug Intervention Timing Based on Prediction
- **Predicted attack 24–48 hrs out**: Initiate preemptive biofeedback session; prioritize sleep; hydrate; apply TENS device (preventive session); reduce sensory load
- **Predicted attack 6–12 hrs out**: Cefaly acute protocol; gammaCore acute; cold pack readiness; dark room preparation; Nerivio session at prodrome onset
- **At aura onset**: sTMS (SpringTMS) — most effective non-drug intervention at this exact moment

## 12. Summary: Evidence Levels by Non-Drug Therapy

| Therapy | Preventive Evidence | Acute Evidence |
