# What Is Migraine

## Overview

Migraine is a genetically influenced, complex neurological disorder characterized by recurrent episodes of moderate-to-severe headache, typically unilateral and pulsating, frequently accompanied by nausea, vomiting, and heightened sensitivity to light (photophobia) and sound (phonophobia). It is far more than a bad headache — it is a brain network disorder with distinct neurobiological mechanisms.

- **Global prevalence:** ~12–16% of the worldwide population; affects approximately 1.1 billion people
- **Sex distribution:** 3× more common in women than men (post-puberty), largely due to hormonal influences
- **Onset:** Most commonly begins between ages 10 and 40
- **Chronicity:** Can be episodic or chronic; attacks last 4 to 72 hours if untreated
- **Disability:** Ranked among the top causes of years lived with disability globally (WHO)

---

## Definition and Classification

### ICD / ICHD Criteria (International Classification of Headache Disorders)

Migraine without aura (ICHD-3 1.1):
- At least 5 attacks lasting 4–72 hours
- At least 2 of: unilateral location, pulsating quality, moderate/severe intensity, aggravated by routine activity
- At least 1 of: nausea/vomiting, photophobia AND phonophobia

Migraine with aura (ICHD-3 1.2):
- All of the above PLUS at least one fully reversible aura symptom (visual, sensory, speech/language, motor, brainstem, retinal)
- Aura lasts 5–60 minutes, spreads gradually

## Core Characteristics

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Pain location | Unilateral (one-sided) in ~60% of cases; can be bilateral |
| Pain quality | Throbbing, pulsating |
| Pain intensity | Moderate to severe (often 6–10/10) |
| Duration (untreated) | 4–72 hours |
| Associated symptoms | Nausea, vomiting, photophobia, phonophobia, osmophobia |
| Aggravating factors | Physical activity, bright light, loud noise, strong odors |
| Relieving factors | Rest in dark quiet room, sleep, cold compresses |

---

## Neurobiological Nature

Migraine is now understood as a **neurovascular disorder** — not purely vascular or purely neural, but involving a complex interplay between:

- **Cortical hyperexcitability:** The migraine brain is inherently more excitable between attacks; reduced habituation to sensory stimuli
- **Trigeminovascular activation:** The trigeminal nerve innervates pain-sensitive meningeal and cerebral blood vessels; its activation releases CGRP and other inflammatory neuropeptides
- **Central sensitization:** During attacks, central pain pathways become sensitized, producing allodynia (pain from normally non-painful stimuli, e.g., brushing hair)
- **Brainstem and hypothalamic involvement:** Imaging studies show activation of brainstem nuclei and the hypothalamus even before headache starts

---

## Genetic Basis

- Migraine has a strong hereditary component; ~50% heritability estimated
- Multiple susceptibility genes identified (polygenic disorder)
- Familial hemiplegic migraine (FHM) is a rare monogenic subtype caused by mutations in CACNA1A (calcium channel), ATP1A2 (sodium-potassium pump), or SCN1A (sodium channel)
- These mutations reveal that ion channel dysfunction and cortical excitability are central mechanisms

---

## Key Neurotransmitters and Neuropeptides

| Substance | Role in Migraine |
|---|---|
| CGRP (Calcitonin Gene-Related Peptide) | Primary vasodilatory neuropeptide; markedly elevated during attacks; key therapeutic target |
| Serotonin (5-HT) | Drops during attack; triptans work via 5-HT1B/1D receptor agonism |
| Substance P | Co-released with CGRP; promotes neurogenic inflammation |
| Glutamate | Excitatory neurotransmitter; involved in cortical spreading depression |
| Dopamine | Prodrome symptoms (yawning, nausea, food cravings) linked to dopaminergic activation |
| Nitric oxide (NO) | Potent vasodilator; triggers migraine in susceptible individuals |

## Epidemiology

- Affects ~1 billion people worldwide
- Leading cause of disability in people under 50
- Women: 18–25% lifetime prevalence; Men: 6–8%
- Peaks between ages 35–45
- Most prevalent type: migraine without aura (~75% of cases)
- ~2–3% of the general population has chronic migraine (≥15 headache days/month)
- Economic burden: billions in lost productivity and healthcare costs annually

---

## Comorbidities

Migraine frequently co-occurs with:
- Depression and anxiety disorders
- Epilepsy (shared cortical excitability)
- Stroke (especially migraine with aura in women who smoke or use oral contraceptives)
- Sleep disorders (insomnia, restless leg syndrome)
- Fibromyalgia and chronic pain syndromes
- Irritable bowel syndrome
- Patent foramen ovale (PFO)
- Obesity (increases risk of chronification)

---

## Diagnosis

Migraine is a **clinical diagnosis** based on history — no biomarker test or imaging confirms it.

### Diagnostic Steps
1. Detailed headache history (frequency, duration, character, location, associated symptoms)
2. Neurological examination
3. Screening for red flags (to rule out secondary headaches):
   - Sudden thunderclap onset ("worst headache of life")
   - Progressive worsening over days/weeks
   - New headache in patient >50 years
   - Headache with fever, stiff neck, rash
   - Headache with focal neurological deficit
   - Headache after head trauma

### Diagnostic Tools
- **Headache diary:** Gold standard for pattern recognition and trigger identification
- **MIDAS scale:** Migraine Disability Assessment Score
- **HIT-6:** Headache Impact Test

---

## Treatment Overview

### Acute (Abortive) Treatment
- **NSAIDs:** Ibuprofen, naproxen, aspirin (first-line for mild-moderate)
- **Triptans:** Sumatriptan, rizatriptan, eletriptan — 5-HT1B/1D agonists (gold standard for moderate-severe)
- **Gepants (CGRP receptor antagonists):** Ubrogepant, rimegepant — newer class, no cardiovascular contraindications
- **Ditans:** Lasmiditan — 5-HT1F agonist, no vasoconstrictive effects
- **Ergotamines:** Older class; limited use due to side effects
- **Anti-emetics:** Metoclopramide, ondansetron (for nausea)

### Preventive Treatment
Indicated when attacks ≥4/month, severely disabling, or acute medications overused:
- **Beta-blockers:** Propranolol, metoprolol
- **Antidepressants:** Amitriptyline, venlafaxine
- **Antiepileptics:** Valproate, topiramate
- **CGRP monoclonal antibodies:** Erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab — highly targeted preventive therapy
- **Botulinum toxin (Botox):** Approved for chronic migraine

---

## Lifestyle and Non-Pharmacological Management

- Regular sleep schedule (consistent wake/sleep times)
- Staying hydrated
- Regular meals (avoid fasting)
- Stress management (CBT, mindfulness, biofeedback)
- Avoiding known personal triggers
- Regular aerobic exercise
- Limiting caffeine and alcohol
- Maintaining headache/trigger diary

---

## Differential Diagnosis

Migraine must be distinguished from:
- Tension-type headache (bilateral, pressing/tightening, no nausea/photophobia)
- Cluster headache (unilateral periorbital, autonomic features, shorter duration)
- Subarachnoid hemorrhage (sudden-onset thunderclap)
- Cervicogenic headache (neck-origin)
- Sinusitis-related headache
- Medication overuse headache (MOH / rebound headache)
- Brain tumor or intracranial hypertension headache

## Migraine and the RAG/Prediction Context

For early warning prediction systems, key extractable features from "what is migraine" knowledge:
- Migraine is recurrent and pattern-based — making it predictable
- Neurobiological changes begin 24–48 hours before pain (prodrome)
- Hormonal, environmental, behavioral, and physiological triggers are identifiable
- CGRP and cortical excitability serve as potential biomarker indicators
- Standardized scales (MIDAS, HIT-6, headache diary data) provide structured inputs for ML models
