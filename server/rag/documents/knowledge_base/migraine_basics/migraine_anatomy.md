# Migraine Anatomy

## Overview

Migraine anatomy encompasses the neuroanatomical structures, pathways, and neurochemical systems involved in the generation, propagation, and perception of migraine pain. Understanding the anatomy is essential for interpreting why certain symptoms occur, which brain regions are involved at each phase, and how therapeutic targets are localized.

Migraine is fundamentally a **brain network disorder** — not a localized event — involving the cortex, brainstem, diencephalon, trigeminal system, and pain modulation pathways.

---

## 1. The Trigeminovascular System

The trigeminovascular system is the **central anatomical pathway** of migraine pain. It consists of:

### 1.1 Trigeminal Nerve (Cranial Nerve V)

- Three divisions: **V1 (ophthalmic)**, **V2 (maxillary)**, **V3 (mandibular)**
- V1 (ophthalmic branch) is most relevant to migraine: innervates the meninges, dura mater, cerebral blood vessels, and pericranial muscles
- Cell bodies located in the **trigeminal ganglion (Gasserian ganglion)**

#### Peripheral Sensitization
- Trigeminal sensory fibers innervate:
  - **Dura mater** (the outer meningeal layer — pain-sensitive)
  - **Large cerebral arteries** (Circle of Willis)
  - **Pial vessels**
  - **Venous sinuses**
  - **Pericranial muscles**
- These pain-sensitive structures do not contain pain receptors in brain parenchyma itself (brain tissue is insensate)

### 1.2 Trigeminal Ganglion (TG)

- Pseudounipolar sensory neurons
- Located in **Meckel's cave** (near petrous apex of temporal bone)
- Contains cell bodies of first-order trigeminal afferents
- Expresses CGRP, substance P, and neuropeptide Y
- Site of peripheral sensitization during migraine

### 1.3 Trigeminal Nucleus Caudalis (TNC)

- Located in the **dorsal horn of the upper cervical spinal cord (C1–C2) and caudal medulla**
- Also known as the **medullary dorsal horn**
- The primary brainstem relay for trigeminal pain signals
- Second-order neurons here become sensitized during prolonged attacks (central sensitization)
- Explains why neck pain and upper cervical muscle tenderness occur in migraine

### 1.4 Trigeminovascular Pathway — Full Sequence

```
Pain-sensitive structures (dura, meningeal vessels)
          ↓
Trigeminal nerve fibers (Aδ and C fibers)
          ↓
Trigeminal Ganglion (CGRP, substance P release)
          ↓
Trigeminal Nucleus Caudalis (TNC) — brainstem/upper cervical cord
          ↓
Second-order neurons cross to contralateral side
          ↓
Thalamus (VPM — ventral posteromedial nucleus)
          ↓
Third-order neurons
          ↓
Somatosensory Cortex (SI, SII) — pain perception
          ↓
Frontal/Insular Cortex — affective component of pain
```

## 2. Key Brain Structures in Migraine

### 2.1 Hypothalamus

**Role:** Initiates the migraine attack (prodrome generator)

- Involved hours to days before headache
- PET imaging shows **hypothalamic activation in prodrome** phase
- Controls: circadian rhythms, appetite, thirst, autonomic function, arousal
- Explains prodrome symptoms: yawning (dopaminergic hypothalamic circuits), food cravings, sleep changes, fatigue, urination
- The hypothalamus may act as the **migraine "pacemaker"** — initiating attack sequences
- Connected to trigeminal system via descending projections

**Subregions involved:**
| Region | Function |
|---|---|
| Lateral hypothalamus | Arousal, appetite |
| Posterior hypothalamus | Autonomic, histamine neurons (orexin) |
| Paraventricular nucleus | Stress response, autonomic regulation |
| Suprachiasmatic nucleus | Circadian rhythm — explains morning migraine prevalence |

---

### 2.2 Brainstem

**Role:** Pain modulation, sensory processing, autonomic control

#### Periaqueductal Gray (PAG)
- Major descending pain modulation center
- Normally suppresses pain via descending inhibitory pathways
- In migraine, PAG function is **impaired** → reduced descending inhibition → pain amplification
- "Migraine generator" hypothesis — PAG and surrounding brainstem structures may generate the migraine state
- Contains opioid, serotonin, and noradrenaline receptors

#### Locus Coeruleus (LC)
- Main noradrenergic nucleus in pons
- Involved in arousal, attention, stress response
- Activation during prodrome phase
- Noradrenaline modulates pain and sensory sensitivity

#### Dorsal Raphe Nucleus (DRN)
- Main serotonergic nucleus
- Serotonin (5-HT) drops during migraine attack
- Triptans (5-HT1B/1D agonists) target downstream receptors
- DRN projects widely to cortex, limbic system, and spinal cord

#### Superior Salivatory Nucleus
- Parasympathetic nucleus
- Controls cranial parasympathetic outflow via the sphenopalatine ganglion
- Involved in autonomic symptoms (lacrimation, nasal congestion in some attacks)

---

### 2.3 Cortex

**Role:** Aura generation, sensory hypersensitivity, pain perception

#### Occipital Cortex (Visual Cortex — V1, V2, V3)
- Origin of **cortical spreading depression (CSD)** — the basis of visual aura
- Most hyperexcitable region in migraine brain
- Transcranial magnetic stimulation studies confirm reduced phosphene threshold

#### Somatosensory Cortex (S1, S2)
- Processes pain signals from thalamus
- Structural thickening observed in migraine patients (neuroplastic changes)

#### Frontal Cortex / Prefrontal Cortex
- Affective and emotional component of pain
- Involved in attention to pain and disability

#### Insular Cortex
- Visceral pain processing
- Interoception — body awareness during migraine
- Explains nausea, GI, and autonomic symptoms

#### Cingulate Cortex (ACC — Anterior Cingulate Cortex)
- Emotional aspects of pain
- Catastrophizing and mood effects of migraine

---

### 2.4 Thalamus

- **VPM nucleus (Ventral Posteromedial):** Main thalamic relay for trigeminal pain
- **VPL nucleus:** Relay for body pain (cutaneous allodynia)
- Thalamic neurons sensitize during prolonged attacks → **extracephalic allodynia**
- Thalamus also regulates sensory gating (explaining photophobia, phonophobia)
- Thalamocortical dysrhythmia may contribute to migraine interictal hyperexcitability

---

### 2.5 Limbic System

- **Amygdala:** Emotional response to pain; fear of next attack; comorbid anxiety in migraine
- **Hippocampus:** Memory consolidation; affected by chronic migraine (volume changes in chronic migraine)
- **Hypothalamus (also limbic):** See above

---

## 3. Pain-Sensitive Structures of the Head and Cranium

Not all cranial structures are pain-sensitive. Migraine pain arises from:

### Pain-Sensitive Structures (Yes)
- Dura mater (especially the dural sinuses)
- Cerebral arteries (especially proximal portions — Circle of Willis, MCA, ACA, PCA)
- Meningeal vessels (middle meningeal artery)
- Venous sinuses
- Scalp arteries (temporal, occipital)
- Pericranial muscles (temporalis, masseter, sternocleidomastoid, trapezius)
- Skull periosteum
- Orbit and periorbital tissues

### Pain-Insensitive Structures (No)
- Brain parenchyma (neurons and glia themselves)
- Arachnoid mater
- Ependyma
- Choroid plexus
- Skull bone (diploë)

## 4. Neurochemical Anatomy

### 4.1 CGRP System

**CGRP (Calcitonin Gene-Related Peptide)**
- A 37-amino acid neuropeptide; most potent vasodilator in the cerebrovascular system
- Expressed abundantly in trigeminal ganglion neurons (especially small-diameter C fibers)
- **CGRP receptor:** Heterodimer of CLR (Calcitonin Receptor-Like Receptor) + RAMP1
- Receptor expressed in: trigeminal ganglion, TNC, cerebral vessels, dura mater, brainstem, thalamus

**CGRP in migraine:**
- Released from trigeminal nerve endings during attack
- Causes meningeal vasodilation
- Promotes neurogenic inflammation
- Stimulates mast cell degranulation
- Increases pain signaling
- **Plasma CGRP levels rise during attacks and normalize with triptan treatment**
- **Therapeutic targets:** Gepants (receptor antagonists), CGRP monoclonal antibodies (erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab)

---

### 4.2 Serotonin (5-HT) System

- 5-HT levels **drop during migraine attacks**
- Tryptamine derivatives (triptans) are 5-HT1B/1D agonists:
  - 5-HT**1B** receptors on meningeal vessel smooth muscle → vasoconstriction
  - 5-HT**1D** receptors on trigeminal nerve terminals → reduce CGRP/substance P release
  - 5-HT**1F** receptors (targeted by lasmiditan) — neuronal, not vascular
- Dorsal raphe nucleus is the central serotonergic hub

---

### 4.3 Dopamine System

- Dopaminergic dysfunction underlies prodrome symptoms
- Hypothalamic dopamine circuits produce: yawning, food cravings, nausea
- D2 receptor agonism → yawning (dopamine agonists induce yawning)
- Anti-dopaminergic antiemetics (prochlorperazine, metoclopramide) are effective abortives partly through this mechanism

---

### 4.4 Glutamate System

- Primary excitatory neurotransmitter
- Elevated glutamate in cortex and trigeminal system during attacks
- NMDA and AMPA receptors involved in CSD initiation and central sensitization
- Explains cortical hyperexcitability

---

### 4.5 Nitric Oxide (NO) System

- Potent vasodilator; produced by endothelial and neuronal nitric oxide synthase (eNOS, nNOS)
- Glyceryl trinitrate (nitroglycerin) reliably triggers migraine in susceptible patients — used in human experimental models
- NO contributes to meningeal vasodilation and CGRP release
- NOS inhibitors explored as therapeutic targets

## 5. Cortical Spreading Depression (CSD) — Detailed Anatomy

### Definition
A slow (~2–6 mm/min) propagating wave of neuronal and glial **depolarization** followed by sustained **depression** of electrical activity across the cerebral cortex.

### Ion Changes During CSD
| Ion | Extracellular | Intracellular |
|---|---|---|
| K⁺ | Markedly elevated | Decreased |
| Na⁺ | Decreased | Elevated |
| Ca²⁺ | Decreased | Elevated |
| Cl⁻ | Decreased | Elevated |

### Anatomical Propagation
1. Originates in **occipital cortex** (most hyperexcitable region)
2. Propagates across cortical surface at ~3 mm/min
3. Can involve parietal, temporal, frontal cortex depending on spread
4. Cortical region affected determines aura type (visual = occipital, sensory = parietal, speech = temporal/frontal)

### CSD-to-Pain Mechanism
1. CSD → activates meningeal nociceptors via K⁺ and other mediators
2. Trigeminal ganglion activation → CGRP release
3. Meningeal vasodilation and plasma extravasation
4. Trigeminal nerve signals → TNC → brainstem → thalamus → cortex
5. Headache perceived

### Blood Flow Changes During CSD
1. Initial brief hyperemia (increased blood flow)
2. Prolonged oligemia (reduced blood flow) — up to 2 hours
3. These hemodynamic changes detectable on fMRI

## 6. Structural Brain Changes in Migraine

Chronic migraine is associated with lasting structural neuroanatomical changes:

| Finding | Details |
|---|---|
| White matter hyperintensities (WMH) | Small subcortical lesions; more common in migraine with aura; slightly elevated stroke risk |
| Cortical thickening | Somatosensory and visual cortices thicker in migraine patients |
| Hippocampal changes | Volume loss in chronic migraine; may affect memory |
| Cortical thinning | Some reports of thinning in pain processing regions with longer disease duration |
| Iron deposition | Periaqueductal gray and other brainstem nuclei |
| Altered functional connectivity | Resting-state fMRI shows abnormal connectivity in default mode network, salience network |

---

## 7. Descending Pain Modulation Anatomy

The brain has an endogenous system to suppress pain — this system is **dysregulated** in migraine:

```
Frontal Cortex / Anterior Cingulate Cortex
          ↓
Periaqueductal Gray (PAG) — midbrain
          ↓
Rostral Ventromedial Medulla (RVM)
          ↓
Dorsal Horn of spinal cord / TNC
          ↓ (inhibition ↓ in migraine)
Pain signal amplification
```

- PAG dysfunction → reduced inhibition → pain amplification
- Descending serotonergic and noradrenergic tracts modulate nociception
- Opioid receptors abundant in PAG — part of endogenous pain suppression

---

## 8. Autonomic Nervous System Involvement

### Parasympathetic (Cranial)
- Superior salivatory nucleus → greater petrosal nerve → sphenopalatine ganglion
- Causes vasodilation of cerebral and meningeal vessels
- In cluster headache: prominent; in migraine: minor autonomic features

### Sympathetic
- T1–T2 spinal cord → superior cervical ganglion → follows blood vessels to cranium
- Sympathetic tone reduced during attacks
- Contributes to pallor, nausea

---

## 9. The Role of the Sphenopalatine Ganglion (SPG)

- A parasympathetic ganglion in the pterygopalatine fossa
- Receives input from superior salivatory nucleus
- Innervates cerebral vessels, lacrimal glands, nasal mucosa
- **SPG blockade** (local anesthetic injection) — used as acute migraine treatment
- Also targeted by non-invasive neurostimulation devices

## 10. Neuroimaging Findings Summary

| Modality | Finding in Migraine |
|---|---|
| PET | Hypothalamic activation in prodrome; brainstem activation during attack |
| fMRI | Altered connectivity: default mode network, salience network, somatosensory cortex |
| MRI (structural) | White matter hyperintensities, cortical thickening in chronic migraine |
| TMS | Reduced phosphene threshold (occipital cortex hyperexcitability) |
| EEG | Deficient habituation to repeated stimuli; abnormal visually evoked potentials |
| SPECT | Oligemia during aura phase; spreading over occipital lobe |

## 11. Anatomy of Common Migraine Triggers

| Trigger Category | Anatomical Target/Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Bright light | Retina → suprachiasmatic nucleus → hypothalamus → trigeminovascular |
| Strong odors | Olfactory bulb → piriform cortex → limbic → hypothalamus |
| Stress | HPA axis → cortisol → CSD threshold lowering; noradrenergic LC activation |
| Hormones (estrogen drop) | Estrogen receptors in TG and cortex; CGRP upregulation; CSD threshold lowering |
| Alcohol (especially red wine) | Tannins, histamine, tyramine → vasodilation, serotonin release |
| Weather changes | Barometric pressure receptors → trigeminal activation |
| Sleep disruption | Suprachiasmatic nucleus → hypothalamic dysregulation |
| Fasting/low blood glucose | Hypothalamic glucose-sensing neurons; serotonin fluctuation |
| Caffeine withdrawal | Adenosine antagonism reversal → vasodilation; serotonin effects |

## 12. Anatomy Relevant to Therapeutic Targets

| Therapeutic | Target Structure/Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Triptans | 5-HT1B receptors on meningeal vessels (vasoconstriction) + 5-HT1D on TG neurons (CGRP inhibition) |
| Gepants (ubrogepant, rimegepant) | CGRP receptor on TNC, TG, meningeal vessels |
| CGRP mAbs (erenumab, etc.) | CGRP or CGRP receptor in peripheral/central trigeminovascular system |
| Botulinum toxin | Pericranial muscle afferents; prevents substance P and CGRP release |
| SPG blockade | Sphenopalatine ganglion (parasympathetic) |
| Occipital nerve block | Greater occipital nerve → TNC convergence |
| Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) | Occipital cortex — interrupts CSD |
| Non-invasive vagal nerve stimulation (nVNS) | Vagus nerve → NTS → LC/raphe → descending modulation |
