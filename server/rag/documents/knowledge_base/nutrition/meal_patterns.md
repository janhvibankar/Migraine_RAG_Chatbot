## 1. Overview: Meal Patterns and Migraine

Irregular eating patterns are among the most modifiable and underappreciated migraine triggers. Meal skipping, prolonged fasting, large glycemic spikes followed by crashes, and inconsistent meal timing all disrupt metabolic homeostasis in ways that directly lower migraine threshold. The hypothalamus — the brain's metabolic regulation center — is also central to migraine pathogenesis, creating a direct anatomical link between nutrition behavior and headache biology.

**Key clinical facts:**
- Meal skipping / fasting is reported as a migraine trigger by 57% of patients — one of the most common triggers
- Hypoglycemia (blood glucose < 70 mg/dL) directly activates nociceptive pathways
- Migraine attacks are 2–3× more likely on days with a skipped meal vs. days with regular meals
- Regular meal timing (within ±30 min of usual time) reduces migraine frequency by up to 30%
- Caloric restriction without medical supervision worsens migraine in susceptible individuals

## 2. The Hypoglycemia–Migraine Mechanism

### Pathophysiology
1. **Blood glucose drop:** Prolonged fasting → blood glucose falls below ~70 mg/dL → hypoglycemic state
2. **Counterregulatory hormones:** Glucagon, epinephrine, cortisol released → HPA axis activation
3. **Cortisol spike:** Stress hormone surge → central sensitization → lowered migraine threshold
4. **Epinephrine:** Causes vasoconstriction → compensatory vasodilation → throbbing headache
5. **Neuronal energy deficit:** Brain consumes ~20% of total body glucose; neurons in trigeminal nucleus are sensitive to glucose fluctuations
6. **Neuropeptide Y release:** Fasting activates NPY pathways in hypothalamus → modulates pain processing and increases susceptibility to CSD
7. **Serotonin drop:** Fasting reduces tryptophan availability → reduced 5-HT synthesis → trigeminovascular activation

### Blood Glucose Thresholds
| Blood Glucose Level | Status | Migraine Risk |
|--------------------|--------|---------------|
| > 100 mg/dL | Normal (fasting) | Low |
| 80–100 mg/dL | Low-normal | Low |
| 70–80 mg/dL | Borderline | Low-Moderate |
| 54–70 mg/dL | Mild hypoglycemia | **HIGH** |
| < 54 mg/dL | Severe hypoglycemia | **VERY HIGH** + other neurological symptoms |

### Reactive Hypoglycemia
- Occurs 1–4 hours after a large high-glycemic meal
- Blood glucose spikes → insulin surge → glucose overcorrection → hypoglycemia
- Common with refined carbohydrate meals (white bread, sugary drinks, candy)
- Triggers migraine more insidiously than frank fasting hypoglycemia
- Symptoms: sweating, shakiness, hunger, headache 2–3 hours post-meal

## 3. Meal Timing and Frequency

### Optimal Meal Frequency for Migraineurs
- **Recommended:** 3 main meals + 1–2 snacks; no gap > 4–5 hours between eating
- **Maximum fasting window:** 4 hours during waking hours (beyond this, hypoglycemia risk elevates)
- **Overnight fast:** Should not exceed 10–12 hours; breakfast within 1–2 hours of waking
- **Consistency:** Same meal times ± 30 minutes daily is more protective than any specific diet

### Meal Timing Windows
| Meal | Optimal Timing | Migraine Risk if Skipped |
|------|---------------|--------------------------|
| Breakfast | Within 1–2 hours of waking | HIGH (overnight fast extended; cortisol peak) |
| Mid-morning snack | 2–3 hours after breakfast | Moderate (if breakfast was small) |
| Lunch | 4–5 hours after breakfast | HIGH if > 6 hours from breakfast |
| Afternoon snack | 2–3 hours after lunch | Low-Moderate |
| Dinner | 4–5 hours after lunch | Moderate if very late (> 8 PM) |
| Bedtime snack | If > 5 hours since dinner | Low; prevents overnight hypoglycemia |

### Morning Eating — Critical Window
- Morning cortisol peak (6–9 AM "cortisol awakening response") is highest of the day
- Breakfast blunts cortisol peak and stabilizes blood glucose through the morning
- Skipping breakfast extends overnight fast → glucose nadir → migraine-vulnerable window
- Most migraines occur between 6–11 AM, correlating with extended overnight fasting + cortisol peak
- **Clinical recommendation:** Breakfast within 60 minutes of waking; include protein + complex carbohydrate

## 4. Macronutrient Composition and Migraine

### Carbohydrates

#### Glycemic Index (GI) and Glycemic Load (GL)
- High-GI foods cause rapid blood glucose spikes → reactive hypoglycemia → migraine
- Low-GI foods provide sustained glucose release → stable blood glucose → reduced migraine risk

| GI Category | Range | Examples | Migraine Risk |
|------------|-------|---------|---------------|
| Low GI | ≤ 55 | Oats, legumes, sweet potato, most fruits, whole grains | LOW |
| Medium GI | 56–69 | Basmati rice, whole wheat bread, banana | Moderate |
| High GI | ≥ 70 | White bread, white rice, potatoes, sugary cereals | HIGH (reactive hypoglycemia) |

**Glycemic Load (GL) = GI × carbohydrate content / 100**
- GL < 10: Low risk
- GL 11–19: Moderate
- GL > 20: High risk for reactive hypoglycemia

#### Fiber
- Slows glucose absorption → blunts glycemic spikes
- Target: 25–35 g dietary fiber/day
- Good sources: vegetables, legumes, whole grains, nuts, seeds

### Protein
- Stabilizes blood glucose by slowing gastric emptying
- Provides tryptophan → serotonin precursor → migraine-protective
- Protein at every meal is a key migraine prevention strategy
- Target: 0.8–1.2 g protein/kg body weight/day
- Important sources: eggs, fish, legumes, poultry, dairy, tofu

### Fats
- Omega-3 fatty acids (EPA + DHA): anti-inflammatory; reduce prostaglandin synthesis; evidence for migraine reduction
  - Sources: fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines), flaxseed, walnuts, chia seeds
  - Supplementation: 1–3 g EPA+DHA/day — some RCT evidence for migraine prevention
- Omega-6 fatty acids: pro-inflammatory when in excess; high omega-6:omega-3 ratio worsens migraine frequency
  - Common sources: seed oils (corn, soybean, sunflower), processed foods
- Saturated fats: do not directly trigger migraine but high consumption → systemic inflammation → indirect risk

## 5. Skipped Meals — Patterns and Risk

### Why Meals Are Skipped (Behavioral Patterns)
| Reason | Additional Risk Factors |
|--------|------------------------|
| Busy work schedule | Stress + skipped meal = compound trigger |
| Intentional dieting / fasting | Caloric deficit + metabolic stress |
| Nausea from previous migraine | Migraine-induced anorexia perpetuates attack |
| Travel or disrupted routine | Irregular timing + possible dehydration |
| Intermittent fasting protocol | Extended fasting window risky for migraineurs |
| Social eating disruption | Missing meals, eating at different times |
| Morning rush / no time for breakfast | Highest-risk skip |

### Intermittent Fasting and Migraine
- **16:8 fasting (most common):** 16-hour fast window frequently exceeds migraine-safe fasting threshold
- Studies show mixed results: some find reduction in migraine frequency (through weight loss, metabolic improvement); many report worsening, especially during adaptation period
- **Risk factors for IF-induced migraine:** pre-existing hypoglycemia tendency, skipping breakfast, fasting through morning cortisol peak
- **If patients insist on IF:** Recommend 12:12 (safer window), break fast in the morning, ensure electrolytes, avoid fasting on high-stress days
- **Ramadan fasting:** Documented increase in migraine frequency during Ramadan; dehydration + circadian disruption + fasting compound effects

### Calorie-Restricted Diets
- Very-low-calorie diets (< 1000 kcal/day) uniformly worsen migraine
- Crash diets → rapid weight loss → ketosis (which can trigger or relieve migraine depending on individual)
- Ketogenic diet: controversial in migraine; reduces seizure-like CSD events in some; worsens attacks in others during adaptation; not recommended without supervision

## 6. Meal Size and Eating Speed

### Large Meals
- Large caloric bolus → rapid insulin release → potential reactive hypoglycemia 2–4 hours later
- Gastrointestinal distension → vagal nerve activation → autonomic shifts → headache
- High-fat large meals delay gastric emptying → impair absorption of oral migraine medications
- Late-night large meals → impair sleep quality → compound next-day migraine risk

### Eating Speed
- Rapid eating → larger bolus effect → faster glycemic spike → higher reactive hypoglycemia risk
- Mindful eating (slow chewing, 20-minute meals) → improved glycemic control
- Eating while stressed (desk lunch, rushed eating) → impairs digestion, amplifies cortisol

### Meal Size Recommendations
- Moderate-sized meals (400–600 kcal/main meal)
- Snacks: 100–200 kcal, primarily protein + complex carb
- Avoid single meals > 800 kcal

## 7. Specific Dietary Patterns and Migraine Evidence

### Mediterranean Diet
- Consistently associated with reduced migraine frequency (20–30% reduction in studies)
- High in omega-3, fiber, antioxidants, magnesium; low in processed foods and tyramine
- Components: olive oil, fish, vegetables, legumes, whole grains, moderate red wine (note: wine is a trigger for some)
- Reduces systemic inflammation (CRP, IL-6) which is elevated during migraine attacks

### Low-Tyramine Diet
- Tyramine is a direct migraine trigger for sensitive individuals (see trigger_foods.md)
- Meal patterns that concentrate tyramine-rich foods in single meals are higher risk than distributing them
- Important in context of MAO inhibitor use (tyramine accumulates → hypertensive crisis + severe headache)

### Ketogenic Diet
- Mechanism of potential benefit: ketone bodies may stabilize neuronal membranes, reduce CSD susceptibility
- Clinical evidence: small studies show 40–60% reduction in migraine days in some patients
- Risks: adaptation phase (first 2–4 weeks) commonly causes worsening headache ("keto flu")
- Not recommended as first-line; consider only for patients refractory to standard prevention

### Anti-Inflammatory Diet
- Reduces prostaglandins, cytokines involved in neuroinflammation and migraine
- Key components: eliminate processed foods, trans fats, refined sugar; emphasize turmeric, ginger, leafy greens, omega-3s

### Elimination Diets
- Systematic removal of top trigger foods (dairy, gluten, citrus, chocolate, alcohol, processed meats)
- Reintroduction one at a time over 4–8 weeks to identify individual triggers
- Should be supervised to prevent nutritional deficiency

## 8. Eating Patterns Around Menstrual Cycle

### Perimenstrual Nutritional Changes
- Days 1–3 (menstruation): Prostaglandin-mediated inflammation peaks; nausea + appetite loss common
- Luteal phase (days 14–28): Increased appetite, carbohydrate cravings, progesterone effects on glycemic control
- Premenstrual period (days 24–28): Estrogen drop → serotonin decline → cravings for chocolate/sweets → reactive hypoglycemia risk

### Perimenstrual Dietary Recommendations
- Increase magnesium-rich foods in luteal phase (dark leafy greens, seeds, legumes)
- Avoid high-GI carbohydrate binges during premenstrual carbohydrate craving
- Maintain regular meal timing strictly; hormonal fluctuation already lowers migraine threshold
- Increase complex carbohydrate, protein, and omega-3 intake during menstruation

## 9. Micronutrients Critical to Meal Pattern Planning

### Riboflavin (Vitamin B2)
- Mitochondrial cofactor; energy production in neurons
- Deficiency linked to increased migraine frequency
- Evidence: 400 mg/day riboflavin → 50% reduction in migraine attack frequency (RCT, Class A)
- Sources: dairy, eggs, lean meat, almonds, leafy greens
- Recommendation: Ensure dietary adequacy; supplement 400 mg/day for prevention

### Magnesium
- Intracellular magnesium deficiency in 50% of migraine patients during attacks
- Regulates NMDA receptors and neuronal excitability
- Sources: pumpkin seeds, spinach, almonds, black beans, avocado
- Recommendation: 400–600 mg/day as magnesium glycinate or citrate

### Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10)
- Mitochondrial energy production cofactor
- RCT evidence: 300 mg/day CoQ10 reduces migraine days by 50%
- Found in small amounts in meat, fish, nuts; supplementation usually needed for therapeutic dose

### Vitamin D
- Low serum vitamin D (< 30 ng/mL) associated with higher migraine frequency
- Influences serotonin synthesis and neuroinflammation
- Sources: sunlight, fatty fish, fortified foods; supplementation 1000–2000 IU/day commonly needed

### Iron
- Iron deficiency (ferritin < 50 ng/mL) → Restless legs syndrome → sleep disruption → migraine
- Also impairs dopamine synthesis (dopamine is migraine-modulating)
- Sources: red meat, legumes, dark leafy greens; vitamin C improves non-heme iron absorption

### Folate
- B9; involved in homocysteine metabolism
- Elevated homocysteine associated with migraine with aura
- Sources: leafy greens, legumes, fortified cereals
- MTHFR gene variants (common) reduce folate metabolism; methylated folate supplementation may be needed

## 10. Meal Pattern Disruption Scenarios with Migraine Risk

| Scenario | Mechanism | Risk Level |
|---------|-----------|-----------|
| Skipped breakfast after overnight fast | Extended fasting → hypoglycemia + cortisol peak | **VERY HIGH** |
| Skipped lunch (> 6 hours since breakfast) | Hypoglycemia, energy deficit | **HIGH** |
| Large sugary meal followed by crash | Reactive hypoglycemia 2–4 hours later | **HIGH** |
| Very small meals all day (< 1000 kcal) | Sustained caloric deficit → metabolic stress | **HIGH** |
| Irregular meal times (> 1 hour from usual) | Circadian metabolic disruption | **Moderate** |
| Late dinner > 8 PM | Impairs sleep + late glucose spike | **Moderate** |
| Intermittent fasting 16:8 | Exceeds safe fasting threshold | **Moderate-HIGH** |
| Regular meals, consistent timing | Stable glucose, minimal cortisol variation | **Protective** |

## 11. RAG System Usage Notes

### High-Priority Meal Pattern Trigger Phrases
- "I skipped breakfast / didn't eat this morning" → VERY HIGH risk flag
- "I haven't eaten in X hours" (where X > 5) → HIGH risk
- "I barely ate today / didn't have time to eat" → HIGH risk
- "I've been dieting / restricting calories" → Moderate-High risk
- "I ate a lot of junk food / sweets" → Reactive hypoglycemia risk
- "I didn't eat dinner / ate very late" → Moderate risk
- "I've been fasting / doing intermittent fasting" → Flag extended fasting window
- "I felt nauseous and couldn't eat" → Migraine-induced anorexia loop flag

### Risk Scoring Contribution
| Meal Pattern Factor | Suggested Risk Weight |
|--------------------|----------------------|
| Breakfast skipped | Very High (+3) |
| Any meal skipped (> 5 hr gap) | High (+3) |
| < 1000 kcal/day total | High (+3) |
| Fasting > 6 hours | High (+3) |
| High-GI meal without protein | Moderate (+2) |
| Meal > 1 hour later than usual | Moderate (+2) |
| Irregular eating pattern this week | Moderate (+2) |
| High-GI snack only (no substantial meal) | Low-Moderate (+1) |
| Regular meals, consistent timing | Protective (−2) |
| Protein + complex carb at each meal | Protective (−1) |
