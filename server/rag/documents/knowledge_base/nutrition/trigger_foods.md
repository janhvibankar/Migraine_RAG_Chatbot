## 1. Overview: Food Triggers and Migraine

Dietary triggers account for approximately 20–30% of migraine attacks in susceptible individuals. Food triggers do not act uniformly — the same food may trigger an attack in one person but not another, and may trigger an attack one day but not another in the same person. This variability reflects the cumulative "trigger threshold" model: migraine occurs when the sum of active triggers exceeds an individual's threshold, which varies daily.

**Key clinical facts:**
- ~20–30% of migraineurs identify at least one food trigger
- Alcohol and caffeine are the most consistently identified dietary triggers
- Food triggers typically cause migraine within 30 minutes to 12 hours of consumption
- Delayed reactions (6–12 hours) are common and make identification difficult
- Trigger sensitivity increases when food triggers combine with other triggers (stress, hormones, poor sleep)
- Elimination of suspected triggers reduces migraine frequency in ~50% of patients who try it
- Perceived food triggers may sometimes reflect cravings during the migraine prodrome (the food is not the trigger — the prodrome is)

## 2. The Threshold Model of Migraine Triggers

Food triggers rarely act in isolation. The migraine threshold is a dynamic concept:

```
Migraine Threshold (variable) = Biological resilience - (Sum of active triggers)

When: Sum of triggers > Current threshold → MIGRAINE ATTACK
```

**Implications for RAG:**
- A food that "sometimes" triggers migraine is operating near threshold
- Same food + additional trigger (stress, poor sleep, hormonal change) → attack more likely
- Trigger stacking: 2–3 minor triggers combine to produce attack even if no single one would alone
- Keeping trigger load low on high-risk days (menstrual, high-stress, poor sleep) is protective

## 3. Tyramine

### Overview
Tyramine is a biogenic amine formed by bacterial decarboxylation of the amino acid tyrosine during fermentation, aging, and protein breakdown. It is the most studied dietary migraine trigger.

### Mechanism
- Tyramine → releases norepinephrine → vasoconstriction followed by vasodilation → throbbing headache
- Normally metabolized by monoamine oxidase (MAO) in gut wall and liver
- Patients with low MAO activity (genetic variants, MAO inhibitor medications) have dramatically higher tyramine sensitivity
- Tyramine may also deplete neuronal serotonin → trigeminovascular activation

### Tyramine Content of Foods
| Food | Tyramine Content | Migraine Risk |
|------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Very High (> 15 mg/serving)** | | |
| Aged cheddar | 15–100 mg/oz | VERY HIGH |
| Blue cheese (Roquefort, Stilton) | 20–200 mg/oz | VERY HIGH |
| Parmesan | 15–50 mg/oz | VERY HIGH |
| Feta (aged) | 5–20 mg/oz | HIGH |
| Soy sauce | 0.4–3 mg/tsp | Moderate (high volume risk) |
| Miso paste | 5–40 mg/tbsp | HIGH |
| Fermented pickles | 10–50 mg/serving | HIGH |
| Sauerkraut | 5–10 mg/serving | Moderate-High |
| Kimchi | 5–15 mg/serving | HIGH |
| Salami, pepperoni | 10–30 mg/oz | HIGH |
| Aged/cured meats | 10–50 mg/oz | VERY HIGH |
| Red wine | 0.5–25 mg/glass | HIGH (+ histamine + alcohol) |
| Beer (draft) | 0.5–8 mg/pint | Moderate-High |
| Brewer's yeast | 15–40 mg/serving | HIGH |
| Broad beans / fava beans | 10–15 mg/serving | HIGH |
| **Low Tyramine (safe)** | | |
| Fresh meat, poultry (unaged) | < 0.1 mg | LOW |
| Fresh fish (same-day) | < 0.1 mg | LOW |
| Pasteurized milk | < 0.1 mg | LOW |
| Cream cheese, ricotta | < 1 mg | LOW |
| Eggs | None | NONE |
| Most fresh fruits and vegetables | Trace | LOW |

**Key principle:** Tyramine accumulates with aging/fermentation time. The SAME food has very different tyramine content depending on how long it has been stored, ripened, or fermented.

### Clinical Note on Tyramine and MAO Inhibitors
- Patients taking MAOIs (phenelzine, tranylcypromine, selegiline) must strictly avoid tyramine foods
- Tyramine + MAOI → hypertensive crisis → severe "thunderclap" headache (distinct from migraine but dangerous)
- Also relevant: some migraine preventives have mild MAO-inhibiting properties

## 4. Histamine

### Overview
Histamine is a biogenic amine produced by bacterial decarboxylation of histidine; also produced endogenously. Histamine intolerance (inadequate DAO enzyme activity) causes pseudoallergic reactions including headache.

### Mechanism
- Histamine → H1 receptor activation → vasodilation → throbbing headache
- Histamine → mast cell degranulation → neurogenic inflammation → trigeminovascular activation
- Histamine intolerance: DAO (diamine oxidase) enzyme degrades histamine; low DAO → histamine accumulates after dietary intake
- Conditions reducing DAO: intestinal inflammation, alcohol, certain medications

### High-Histamine Foods
| Food Category | Specific Foods | Risk |
|--------------|---------------|------|
| Fermented foods | Sauerkraut, kimchi, kombucha, kefir | HIGH |
| Aged cheeses | All aged varieties | VERY HIGH |
| Cured/smoked meats | Salami, prosciutto, smoked fish, hot dogs | HIGH |
| Alcohol | All types; especially red wine, beer | VERY HIGH |
| Fish | Canned tuna, sardines, anchovies, mackerel | HIGH |
| Shellfish | All (especially if not fresh) | HIGH |
| Vinegar | Wine vinegar, balsamic, pickled foods | Moderate-High |
| Tomatoes | Fresh and processed (ketchup, sauce) | Moderate |
| Spinach | High histamine in this vegetable | Moderate |
| Avocado | Moderate histamine + histamine liberator | Moderate |
| Eggplant | Moderate histamine content | Low-Moderate |

### Histamine Liberators (Trigger Histamine Release)
These foods have low intrinsic histamine but trigger endogenous histamine release:
- Citrus fruits (oranges, lemons, grapefruit, limes)
- Strawberries
- Tomatoes
- Chocolate / cocoa
- Pineapple
- Papaya
- Nuts (especially walnuts, peanuts, cashews)
- Egg whites

### Testing for Histamine Intolerance
- Low-histamine elimination diet × 4 weeks → reintroduction
- Serum DAO activity test (not widely available)
- Oral provocation test under supervision
- DAO supplementation trial (enzyme replacement before meals)

## 5. Monosodium Glutamate (MSG) and Glutamates

### Overview
MSG is the sodium salt of glutamic acid, used as a flavor enhancer (umami). Glutamate is an excitatory neurotransmitter; excess dietary glutamate can increase neuronal excitability in sensitive individuals.

### Mechanism
- Glutamate → NMDA and AMPA receptor activation → neuronal hyperexcitability → lowers CSD threshold
- NMDA receptors are already dysregulated in migraine brains; additional glutamate may push toward attack
- "Chinese Restaurant Syndrome" (flushing, headache, pressure after Chinese food) — historically attributed to MSG; evidence is controversial (likely multifactorial)

### Glutamate/MSG Sources
| Source | MSG/Glutamate Level | Notes |
|--------|--------------------|----|
| Flavor enhancer (added MSG) | HIGH | Fast food, chips, instant noodles, canned soups |
| Hydrolyzed vegetable protein | HIGH | Common in processed foods, sauces |
| Yeast extract / yeast autolysate | HIGH | Marmite, Vegemite, bouillon cubes |
| Parmesan and aged cheeses | HIGH | Naturally high glutamate |
| Tomatoes | Moderate (natural) | Especially concentrated/cooked |
| Soy sauce, fish sauce | HIGH (fermented) | Also high tyramine |
| Mushrooms | Moderate | Especially shiitake, portobello |
| Walnuts | Moderate | Also histamine liberator |
| Protein powders | Variable | Check for added flavor enhancers |

**Note:** Free glutamate (added MSG or from fermentation) may pose higher risk than bound glutamate (in whole foods).

## 6. Nitrates and Nitrites

### Overview
Nitrates/nitrites are preservatives used in processed meats. They are also present naturally in some vegetables. In the body they convert to nitric oxide (NO), a potent vasodilator.

### Mechanism
- Nitrate → Nitrite → Nitric oxide (NO) via nitric acid reductase bacteria in the gut
- NO causes cerebral vasodilation → headache in NO-sensitive individuals
- Organic nitrate drugs (nitroglycerin) reliably induce migraine attacks in research settings (used as an experimental model)
- "Hot dog headache" — classic example of nitrate-triggered migraine after processed meat

### Nitrate/Nitrite-Rich Foods
| Food | Nitrate/Nitrite Level | Migraine Risk |
|------|----------------------|---------------|
| Processed meats: hot dogs | HIGH (added nitrite) | HIGH |
| Bacon, ham, bologna | HIGH | HIGH |
| Pepperoni, salami | HIGH | HIGH (also tyramine) |
| Smoked meats | HIGH | HIGH |
| Cured fish (lox, smoked salmon) | Moderate-High | Moderate-High |
| Beets | HIGH (natural) | LOW (different form; actually beneficial for most) |
| Spinach, arugula | HIGH (natural) | LOW (beneficial; anti-inflammatory context) |
| Celery | HIGH (natural) | LOW |

**Note:** Natural vegetable nitrates behave differently from processed meat nitrites — vegetable nitrates co-occur with antioxidants (vitamin C) that divert conversion away from harmful nitrosamines.

## 7. Phenylethylamine (PEA)

### Overview
PEA is a neuroactive amine found in chocolate, wine, and some fermented foods. Like tyramine, it is normally metabolized by MAO; low MAO activity increases sensitivity.

### Mechanism
- PEA → releases monoamines (dopamine, norepinephrine, serotonin) → cerebral vasoconstriction → rebound vasodilation → throbbing headache
- Mimics the neurochemical cascade of a "natural amphetamine"
- Acts within 30–120 minutes of consumption

### PEA-Rich Foods
- Dark chocolate (highest concentration)
- Milk chocolate (lower but significant)
- Red wine
- Aged cheeses (overlap with tyramine)
- Fermented soy products
- Some citrus fruits (limited)

### Chocolate and Migraine: The Prodrome Debate
- Chocolate cravings are a well-documented migraine prodrome symptom (occurs hours before attack)
- Many patients attribute their migraine to chocolate when the chocolate craving was actually caused by the prodrome
- Studies: chocolate may be a trigger in a subset of patients, but "trigger" is overidentified due to prodrome confounding
- **Clinical guidance:** Track temporal relationship carefully; if headache begins > 6 hours after chocolate, prodrome confounding is likely

## 8. Alcohol — Detailed Breakdown

### Why Alcohol Is a Dominant Food Trigger
- Multiple independent trigger mechanisms:
  1. **Ethanol itself:** Vasodilatory; dehydrating (ADH inhibition)
  2. **Tyramine** (especially red wine, aged spirits)
  3. **Histamine** (especially red wine, beer)
  4. **Sulfites** (preservatives in wine; trigger in sulfite-sensitive individuals)
  5. **Congeners** (fermentation byproducts: acetaldehyde, tannins)
  6. **Phenylethylamine** (red wine)
  7. **Tryptamine** (beer, wine)

### Alcohol Type by Migraine Risk
| Alcohol | Risk Level | Primary Mechanism |
|---------|-----------|-------------------|
| Red wine | VERY HIGH | Tyramine + histamine + PEA + sulfites + ethanol |
| Champagne/sparkling wine | HIGH | Carbonation speeds absorption; sulfites; ethanol |
| White wine | Moderate-High | Sulfites; histamine; ethanol |
| Beer (dark/craft) | HIGH | Sulfites; tyramine; histamine; ethanol |
| Beer (light lager) | Moderate | Less congeners; still dehydrating |
| Vodka | Lowest among alcohols | Fewest congeners; still vasodilatory + dehydrating |
| Whisky/bourbon | HIGH | High congener content; tyramine |
| Gin | Moderate | Juniper compounds; ethanol |
| Rum | Moderate-High | Congeners; ethanol |

### Alcohol Trigger Timing
- Immediate-onset (within 3 hours): typically cocktail-type headache, not true migraine
- Delayed-onset (12–24 hours): "hangover migraine" — combined dehydration, acetaldehyde, sleep disruption
- Even 1–2 units can trigger attacks in highly sensitive migraineurs
- No "safe" alcohol amount exists for all migraineurs; individual threshold varies widely

## 9. Aspartame and Artificial Sweeteners

### Aspartame
- Dipeptide of aspartic acid and phenylalanine
- Metabolized to phenylalanine → may alter brain amino acid balance → affects serotonin synthesis in susceptible individuals
- Small case reports and some RCT evidence for headache induction; effect size is debated
- Individuals with PKU (phenylketonuria) must avoid aspartame; also affects catecholamine synthesis
- **Sources:** Diet sodas (Diet Coke, Pepsi Max), sugar-free gum, flavored low-calorie products, some medications

### Other Sweeteners
| Sweetener | Migraine Evidence | Notes |
|-----------|-----------------|-------|
| Aspartame | Some evidence | Phenylalanine pathway |
| Sucralose | Minimal evidence | Generally considered safe |
| Saccharin | Minimal evidence | |
| Acesulfame-K | Minimal evidence | |
| Stevia | No evidence of trigger | May be protective (no glycemic spike) |
| Sugar alcohols (xylitol, sorbitol) | Low risk headache; GI risk | Bloating, diarrhea can trigger secondary headache |

## 10. Other Notable Triggers

### Caffeine (in Food Context)
- Chocolate, coffee-flavored foods, energy drinks, tea
- See caffeine.md for full detail
- In food context: double risk when combined with tyramine (chocolate) or histamine (wine + chocolate)

### Citrus Fruits
- Act as histamine liberators
- Mechanism: trigger endogenous histamine release (not intrinsic histamine)
- Oranges, lemons, limes, grapefruit, tangerines
- Vitamin C in citrus (protective factor in other contexts) does not fully counter this
- Low-acid citrus varieties (navel orange vs. Valencia) have lower trigger potential

### Sulfites
- Preservatives used in wine, dried fruits, packaged foods, certain juices
- Present in: wine (especially white and rosé), beer, dried apricots, raisins, prunes, packaged shrimp, grape juice, bottled lemon juice
- Mechanism: sulfite → sulfur dioxide → mast cell degranulation → histamine release → vasodilation
- Sulfite sensitivity is more common in asthmatics; overlap with migraine

### Gluten (in Sensitive Individuals)
- Celiac disease and non-celiac gluten sensitivity (NCGS) associated with higher migraine frequency
- Mechanism: intestinal inflammation → systemic inflammatory cytokines → neuroinflammation
- Gluten-free diet reduces migraine frequency in confirmed celiac/NCGS patients by 25–50%
- Not a trigger for migraineurs without gluten sensitivity; routine gluten elimination without evidence of sensitivity is not recommended

### Dairy
- Complex relationship: reported trigger by some patients; may be due to tyramine in aged cheeses (not fresh dairy)
- Fresh dairy (milk, cream, ricotta, cottage cheese) is low-tyramine and not a consistent trigger
- Dairy elimination without evidence-based rationale can risk riboflavin and calcium deficiency

### Fermented / Pickled Foods
- Combined tyramine + histamine + high sodium → multiple trigger pathways
- Includes: pickles, fermented vegetables, kombucha, kefir, yogurt (full-fat, long-fermented)
- Greek yogurt: moderate risk (short-fermented varieties lower risk)

## 11. Food Trigger Diary Methodology

### Tracking Format
For each meal/snack, record:
1. Foods consumed (specific, not just "salad")
2. Time of consumption
3. Hours since last meal
4. Hydration status
5. Other concurrent triggers (stress, sleep, hormonal phase)
6. Headache onset time (if any)
7. Time gap: food → headache onset

### Identifying True Triggers vs. Confounders
- **True trigger:** Consistent relationship; headache within 30 min–12 hours; reproducible on challenge
- **Prodrome craving:** Food consumed 1–6 hours before attack begins; craving was part of early attack (not causal)
- **Coincidence:** Food consumed same day as attack; no temporal or reproducible pattern
- **Elimination challenge:** Remove food for 4–6 weeks; reintroduce; compare headache frequency

### LEAP (Lifestyle, Eating, and Activity Plan) Method
- Systematic elimination and reintroduction of 6 most common dietary triggers
- Phase 1 (weeks 1–4): Eliminate all 6 categories simultaneously
- Phase 2 (weeks 5–10): Reintroduce one category per week, monitoring for 72 hours
- Record: headache frequency and severity before vs. during each phase

## 13. RAG System Usage Notes

### High-Priority Trigger Food Phrases
- "I had red wine / alcohol last night" → VERY HIGH next-day risk
- "I ate aged cheese / parmesan / blue cheese" → Tyramine + histamine HIGH risk
- "I had a lot of chocolate" → PEA + caffeine; check prodrome timing
- "I ate at a Chinese restaurant / fast food" → MSG flag
- "I had processed meats / deli meat / bacon" → Nitrite trigger
- "I had fermented food / kombucha / kimchi" → Tyramine + histamine
- "I drank diet soda / chewed sugar-free gum" → Aspartame flag
- "I had energy drinks" → Caffeine + additives HIGH risk
- "I ate pickled food / vinegar-heavy food" → Histamine flag
- "I craved chocolate before my headache" → Possible prodrome (not trigger); document for pattern

### Risk Scoring Contribution
| Food Trigger Factor | Suggested Risk Weight |
|--------------------|----------------------|
| Red wine (any amount) | Very High (+3) |
| Alcohol > 2 units | Very High (+3) |
| Aged cheese consumed | High (+2) |
| Processed/cured meats | High (+2) |
| Dark chocolate (large amount) | Moderate (+2) |
| MSG-heavy meal (fast food, chips) | Moderate (+2) |
| Fermented foods (multiple servings) | Moderate (+2) |
| Citrus in large quantity | Low-Moderate (+1) |
| Diet soda / aspartame | Low-Moderate (+1) |
| Energy drink | High (+3, also caffeine) |
| Fresh whole foods meal | Protective (−1) |
| Trigger stacking (2+ high-risk foods) | COMPOUND: add all scores |

### Trigger Stacking Alert Rules (for RAG)
Flag as HIGH COMPOUND RISK if any combination:
- Red wine + poor sleep + stress
- Chocolate + menstrual phase + skipped meal
- Aged cheese + alcohol + dehydration
- Processed meat + MSG + caffeine withdrawal
- Any 2 HIGH foods + any 2 non-dietary HIGH triggers simultaneously
