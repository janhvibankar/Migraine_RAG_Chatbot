## 1. Overview

Red flags in the context of headache and migraine are clinical warning signs that suggest the headache may NOT be a primary migraine but rather a symptom of a serious, potentially life-threatening secondary condition. These must be identified and acted upon immediately. The mnemonic **SNOOP10** is widely used in clinical practice to screen for red flags.

---

## 2. SNOOP10 Mnemonic — Comprehensive Red Flag Framework

### S — Systemic Symptoms / Secondary Risk Factors

#### Systemic Symptoms
- Fever accompanying headache (especially >38°C / 100.4°F)
- Night sweats
- Unexplained weight loss (>5% body weight in <6 months)
- Myalgia (muscle pain), malaise, fatigue
- Rash (especially petechial or purpuric — suggests meningococcemia)
- Jaw claudication (pain in jaw while chewing — suggests giant cell arteritis)

#### Secondary Risk Factors
- Known or suspected HIV/AIDS (risk of opportunistic CNS infections)
- Active cancer or history of malignancy (risk of brain metastases)
- Immunocompromised state (organ transplant, chemotherapy, high-dose steroids)
- Pregnancy or postpartum period (risk of preeclampsia, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis)
- Anticoagulant therapy (risk of intracranial hemorrhage)
- Coagulopathy or bleeding disorder
- Recent head trauma (risk of subdural hematoma)

---

### N — Neurological Symptoms or Abnormal Neurological Signs

- **New focal neurological deficits:**
  - Unilateral arm/leg weakness or paralysis
  - Facial droop (asymmetry)
  - Dysarthria (slurred speech)
  - Aphasia (inability to speak or understand language)
  - Monocular blindness or visual field defects (not typical aura)
  - Diplopia (double vision)
  - Ataxia (loss of coordination)
  - Dysphagia (difficulty swallowing)
- Altered mental status, confusion, disorientation
- Seizures occurring with headache
- Loss of consciousness or syncope
- Papilledema (optic disc swelling — sign of raised ICP)
- Meningismus: neck stiffness (Kernig's and Brudzinski's signs)

> **Key Distinction:** Migraine aura is typically positive (scintillating scotoma, tingling), spreads gradually, and lasts 20–60 minutes. Sudden-onset deficits, prolonged deficits (>60 minutes), or motor deficits are red flags.

---

### O — Onset: Sudden / Thunderclap

#### Thunderclap Headache (TCH)
- Headache that reaches peak intensity within **60 seconds** of onset
- Described as "the worst headache of my life"
- Must be treated as **subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) until proven otherwise**

**Causes of Thunderclap Headache to Rule Out:**
| Condition | Key Feature |
|-----------|-------------|
| Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) | Ruptured aneurysm; ~50% mortality if untreated |
| Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST) | Progressive, associated with papilledema |
| Reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (RCVS) | Recurrent TCH over days/weeks |
| Cervical artery dissection | Neck pain + focal deficits |
| Hypertensive emergency | BP > 180/120 mmHg |
| Pituitary apoplexy | Bitemporal hemianopia, diplopia |
| Spontaneous intracranial hypotension | Worsens upright, improves supine |
| Ischemic stroke | Sudden neurological deficit |

---

### O — Onset After Age 50 (New Headache Pattern)

- New-onset headache in patients **>50 years** is a red flag
- Risk conditions:
  - Giant Cell Arteritis (GCA / Temporal Arteritis)
    - Temporal artery tenderness
    - Jaw claudication
    - Elevated ESR (>50 mm/hr) and CRP
    - Risk of irreversible blindness without immediate steroids
  - Intracranial neoplasm
  - Subdural hematoma

---

### P — Pattern Change / Progressive Headache

- Change in established headache pattern without clear explanation
- Increasing frequency or severity over weeks/months
- Headache that is **progressive** (worsening with each episode without returning to baseline)
- **Positional component:** Worsens when lying flat (suggests raised ICP)
- **Valsalva-triggered:** Worsens with coughing, sneezing, straining, or bending over
  - Suggests posterior fossa lesion or Chiari malformation

---

### P — Postural Component

- Headache that worsens significantly when **standing or sitting upright** and improves when **lying down**
  - Suggests **intracranial hypotension** (spontaneous CSF leak)
- Headache that worsens when **lying down** or bending forward
  - Suggests **raised intracranial pressure**

---

### P — Precipitated by Valsalva

- Cough headache
- Exertional headache (with exercise, sexual activity)
- These may indicate **Arnold-Chiari malformation**, **posterior fossa tumor**, or **cerebral venous pathology**

---

### S — Sleep-Related Headache

- **Hypnic headache** (exclusively wakes patient from sleep, >50 yrs): needs investigation
- Headaches consistently waking patient from sleep suggest raised ICP or sleep apnea
- **Cluster headache** wakes from sleep but has distinct pattern (periorbital, autonomic features)

---

### S — Seizures

- New-onset seizures occurring with headache
- Post-ictal headache is common, but first-time seizure always warrants neuroimaging

---

### S — Side-Locked Headache (Always Same Side)

- Strictly unilateral headache always on the same side (not alternating):
  - Risk of **arteriovenous malformation (AVM)**
  - Carotid/vertebral artery dissection
  - Intracranial neoplasm

---

### S — Sensitive Populations

#### Children
- New severe headache in a child, especially with vomiting, neck stiffness, or behavior change
- Posterior fossa tumors are the most common pediatric brain tumors

#### Elderly (>65 years)
- New headache always warrants evaluation
- Higher risk of subdural hematoma (even with minor/forgotten trauma), GCA, neoplasm

#### Pregnant / Postpartum (up to 6 weeks post-delivery)
- **Preeclampsia/Eclampsia:** Headache + hypertension + proteinuria
- **Cerebral Venous Sinus Thrombosis (CVST):** Common in postpartum period
- **PRES** (Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome)

## 3. Specific High-Priority Red Flag Conditions

### 3.1 Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH)
- **Presentation:** Thunderclap headache, worst of life, ± neck stiffness, photophobia, vomiting, loss of consciousness
- **Mortality:** Up to 50% within 30 days; survivors often have permanent deficits
- **Action:** Emergency (999/112/911) → CT head non-contrast → LP if CT negative

### 3.2 Bacterial Meningitis
- **Presentation:** Headache + fever + neck stiffness + photophobia (classic triad) ± non-blanching purpuric rash
- **Note:** Rash is a late and ominous sign
- **Action:** IMMEDIATE emergency care; do NOT wait for LP if rash present — give antibiotics empirically

### 3.3 Giant Cell Arteritis (Temporal Arteritis)
- **Presentation:** New headache age >50, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, visual disturbance (amaurosis fugax)
- **Risk:** Permanent blindness if untreated
- **Action:** Same-day urgent care; start high-dose steroids before biopsy results

### 3.4 Hypertensive Emergency
- **Presentation:** Severe headache + BP >180/120 mmHg ± altered consciousness, visual changes
- **Action:** Emergency department

### 3.5 Cervical Artery Dissection (Carotid or Vertebral)
- **Presentation:** Sudden-onset severe neck/head pain ± Horner's syndrome (ptosis, miosis), focal neurological signs
- **Triggers:** Neck manipulation, trauma, chiropractic treatment
- **Action:** Emergency; risk of ischemic stroke

### 3.6 Cerebral Venous Sinus Thrombosis (CVST)
- **Risk groups:** Pregnant/postpartum women, OCP users, hypercoagulable states
- **Presentation:** Progressive headache, papilledema, seizures, focal deficits
- **Action:** Emergency neuroimaging (MRI/MRV)

## 4. Red Flags vs Typical Migraine Features — Differentiation Table

| Feature | Typical Migraine | RED FLAG — Seek Emergency Care |
|---------|-----------------|-------------------------------|
| Onset | Gradual (30 min–several hrs) | Sudden/thunderclap (<60 sec) |
| Quality | Throbbing, pulsating | "Worst ever," explosive |
| Neurological symptoms | Aura: reversible, visual/sensory, <60 min | Sudden motor deficit, aphasia, >60 min deficits |
| Fever | Absent | Present |
| Neck stiffness | Absent | Present (meningismus) |
| Age at first onset | Teens–40s typical | >50 new headache |
| Consciousness | Normal | Altered/loss of consciousness |
| Postural variation | Mild relief lying down | Marked postural dependency |
| Response to prior treatment | Responds to usual migraine therapy | Does not respond to any treatment |
| Vomiting | Common with migraine | Projectile vomiting suggests raised ICP |
| Visual symptoms | Scintillating scotoma, gradual spread | Sudden monocular vision loss, field defect |

## 5. "First or Worst" Rule

> Any headache described as **"the first headache of this type"** OR **"the worst headache of my life"** must be evaluated as an emergency until serious secondary causes are excluded.

---

## 6. Red Flags Specific to Migraine Patients

Even established migraine patients can develop secondary headaches. Red flags IN a known migraineur include:

- Change in usual headache character, location, or frequency without explanation
- Headache not responding to previously effective medications
- Progressive increase in frequency/severity over months
- New neurological symptoms not matching prior aura pattern
- Headache triggered by physical exertion, coughing, sexual activity (if new)
- Headache occurring exclusively on waking from sleep
- Systemic illness accompanying headache

---

## 7. Medication-Related Red Flags

- Headache in a patient on **anticoagulants** (warfarin, heparin, DOACs) — risk of intracranial bleed
- Headache after **lumbar puncture** — post-dural puncture headache (postural, but also must rule out meningitis)
- Headache after starting **oral contraceptives** — risk of CVST; may need to stop OCP
- **Medication overuse headache (MOH):** Not life-threatening but represents a pattern change; >15 days/month analgesic use

---

## 8. Pediatric-Specific Red Flags

- Headache in a child <3 years (highly unusual; almost always secondary)
- Morning headache with vomiting (classic raised ICP sign in children)
- Headache worsening with Valsalva
- Declining school performance + headache
- Gait disturbance or clumsiness accompanying headache
- Occipital headache (posterior fossa tumor)

```
 - Time of headache onset (exact if possible)
 - Speed of onset

 - Current medications
 - Medical history (cancer, HIV, anticoagulants)
 - Last known normal neurological state
```

## 10. Diagnostic Workup Triggered by Red Flags

| Red Flag | First-Line Investigation |
|----------|--------------------------|
| Thunderclap headache | CT head non-contrast → LP (if CT negative) |
| Fever + neck stiffness | LP (after CT if mass lesion suspected) |
| Focal neurological deficit | CT/MRI brain with contrast |
| Papilledema / raised ICP | MRI brain + MR venography |
| Post-trauma headache | CT head non-contrast |
| Age >50 new headache | ESR, CRP, temporal artery biopsy |
| HIV/immunocompromised | CT head → LP (cryptococcal antigen, India ink) |
| Cancer history | MRI brain with gadolinium |
| Pregnancy/postpartum | MRI/MRV brain (avoid CT radiation if possible) |
| Hypertensive emergency | CT head + renal function + urinalysis |

---

## 11. Key Clinical Pearls for RAG Retrieval

- **"First or worst" = emergency until proven otherwise**
- **Thunderclap headache = SAH until proven otherwise**
- **Rash + headache + fever = meningococcal meningitis until proven otherwise (give antibiotics NOW)**
- **Jaw pain + scalp tenderness + new headache >50 = GCA; start steroids before biopsy**
- **Headache + focal deficit + cancer history = brain metastasis until proven otherwise**
- **Postpartum headache = always rule out CVST and preeclampsia**
- **Normal CT does NOT rule out SAH** — LP or CT angiography may be required
- A patient with known migraine is NOT protected from developing a secondary headache disorder
