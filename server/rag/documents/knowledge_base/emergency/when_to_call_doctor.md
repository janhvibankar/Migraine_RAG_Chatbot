## 1. Overview

This document provides a structured, tiered guide for determining when a person experiencing migraine or headache symptoms should contact or visit a healthcare provider. The guide operates across three escalation levels:

1. **LEVEL 1 — EMERGENCY (999/911/112): Call immediately**
2. **LEVEL 2 — URGENT (Same-day/within hours): See a doctor today**
3. **LEVEL 3 — SOON (Within days/weeks): Schedule an appointment**

---

## 2. LEVEL 1 — CALL EMERGENCY SERVICES IMMEDIATELY

> Do not drive. Do not wait. Call emergency services or go to the nearest Emergency Department NOW.

### 2.1 Thunderclap / "Worst Headache of Life"
- Headache that reaches maximum intensity within **60 seconds**
- Described as "the worst headache I have ever had"
- Explosive or "hit on the head" sensation
- **Reason:** Must rule out subarachnoid hemorrhage, which has ~50% mortality

### 2.2 Headache + Fever + Stiff Neck
- Combination of headache + fever + neck stiffness (cannot bring chin to chest)
- ± photophobia (sensitivity to light), phonophobia (sensitivity to sound)
- ± non-blanching skin rash (small red/purple spots that don't fade under a glass)
- **Reason:** Classic presentation of bacterial meningitis or meningococcal disease — life-threatening

### 2.3 Headache + Sudden Neurological Deficit
Call 999/911 immediately if headache is accompanied by any of:
- Sudden **weakness or numbness** of face, arm, or leg (especially one-sided)
- Sudden **difficulty speaking** or understanding speech (aphasia)
- Sudden **vision loss** in one or both eyes
- Sudden **double vision** (diplopia)
- Sudden **severe dizziness**, loss of balance, or inability to walk
- **Reason:** May indicate stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, or basilar artery occlusion

### 2.4 Headache After Head Injury
- New or worsening headache following any head trauma (even minor)
- Especially if accompanied by confusion, vomiting, or loss of consciousness
- **Reason:** Risk of epidural hematoma, subdural hematoma, or diffuse axonal injury

### 2.5 Headache + Loss of Consciousness / Collapse
- Headache immediately preceding or following fainting/loss of consciousness
- **Reason:** May indicate cardiac arrhythmia, SAH, or severe raised ICP

### 2.6 Headache + Seizure (New Onset)
- First-ever seizure occurring alongside a headache
- **Reason:** May indicate meningitis, encephalitis, or space-occupying lesion

### 2.7 Headache + Rash
- Red or purple spots/blotches that do **not** fade when pressed with a glass
- (Non-blanching purpuric or petechial rash)
- **Reason:** Meningococcal septicemia — life-threatening within hours

### 2.8 Headache + Very High Blood Pressure
- Severe headache + systolic BP ≥180 mmHg or diastolic BP ≥120 mmHg
- ± visual disturbance, altered consciousness, chest pain
- **Reason:** Hypertensive emergency; risk of hemorrhagic stroke

### 2.9 Headache in Pregnancy (2nd/3rd Trimester or Postpartum)
- Severe headache + high blood pressure + swelling/visual changes in late pregnancy or up to 6 weeks postpartum
- **Reason:** Preeclampsia/eclampsia — risk of maternal and fetal death

### 2.10 Prolonged Aura (Status Migrainosus Warning Signs)
- Neurological aura symptoms lasting **>60 minutes** that do not resolve
- Complete motor weakness during/after migraine aura
- **Reason:** Prolonged aura with motor features may indicate stroke; hemiplegic migraine needs exclusion of other causes

## 3. LEVEL 2 — SEEK URGENT MEDICAL CARE (Same Day / Within Hours)

> Contact your doctor immediately or visit an urgent care clinic or emergency department today.

### 3.1 Migraine Not Responding to Any Medication
- Migraine lasting >72 hours continuously despite use of:
  - Over-the-counter analgesics (paracetamol, ibuprofen, aspirin)
  - Prescribed triptans at maximum dose
  - Anti-emetics
- This is called **Status Migrainosus**
- Patient may require IV fluids, IV dihydroergotamine (DHE), steroids, or antiemetics

### 3.2 Vomiting Preventing Oral Medications
- Unable to keep any oral medication down due to severe nausea/vomiting
- Risk of dehydration requiring IV or IM medication administration

### 3.3 Migraine Longer Than 72 Hours
- Continuous migraine headache lasting beyond 72 hours (3 days) is abnormal
- Increased risk of migrainous infarction (stroke)

### 3.4 Headache in Known Cancer Patient
- Any new or changed headache in patient with history of cancer
- Especially lung, breast, melanoma, renal, or colorectal cancer (high brain metastasis risk)
- **Reason:** Brain metastases can present as progressive headache

### 3.5 Headache in HIV-Positive / Immunocompromised Patient
- New headache in HIV/AIDS, post-transplant, or on immunosuppressive therapy
- **Reason:** Risk of cryptococcal meningitis, CNS toxoplasmosis, CNS lymphoma

### 3.6 New Headache Pattern After Age 50
- No prior headache history + new headache after age 50
- **Reason:** Risk of giant cell arteritis (GCA), intracranial neoplasm, or subdural hematoma

### 3.7 Sudden Worsening of a Chronic Migraine
- Patient has known chronic migraine but today's episode is significantly worse than usual
- Especially if the character, location, or associated symptoms are different from typical episodes

### 3.8 Headache in a Child with Unusual Features
- Child with headache + morning vomiting
- Headache waking child from sleep
- Child with headache + clumsiness or gait problems
- Headache worsening over days/weeks in a child

### 3.9 Migraine + Visual Symptoms Not Resolving
- Persistent visual disturbance (scotoma, loss of vision) beyond 60 minutes after headache phase
- New visual symptoms not matching prior typical aura

### 3.10 Headache + Signs of Increased Intracranial Pressure
- Headache worse in the morning
- Headache worse when lying flat
- Headache worse with coughing, sneezing, or bending
- Papilledema seen on fundoscopy (if accessible)

## 4. LEVEL 3 — SCHEDULE A DOCTOR APPOINTMENT (Within Days to Weeks)

> Call your GP/neurologist and schedule an appointment soon. Not an emergency, but needs professional evaluation.

### 4.1 Increasing Frequency of Migraines
- Migraine attacks occurring more than **4–8 days per month** (threshold for preventive therapy)
- Episodic migraine transitioning to chronic (≥15 headache days/month for >3 months)

### 4.2 Medication Overuse Headache (MOH) Risk
- Using acute headache medications (triptans, opioids, combination analgesics, NSAIDs, paracetamol) on **≥10–15 days per month**
- Headaches occurring predictably on days without medication
- Medications becoming less effective over time
- **MOH Thresholds:**
  - Triptans, ergots, opioids, combination analgesics: ≥10 days/month
  - Simple analgesics (paracetamol, NSAIDs, aspirin): ≥15 days/month

### 4.3 Current Treatment No Longer Effective
- Triptans or other acute medications that previously worked are no longer providing adequate relief
- Requires re-evaluation and possibly a different triptan or class of medication

### 4.4 Significant Disability From Migraines
- Migraines causing missed work, school, or social activities more than once per month
- **MIDAS score >11** (Migraine Disability Assessment Score) indicates need for preventive therapy
- HIT-6 score ≥60 indicates severe impact on daily life

### 4.5 First-Time Diagnosis Suspicion
- Adult with recurrent unilateral throbbing headaches with nausea/vomiting/photo/phonophobia who has never been formally diagnosed
- Needs formal diagnosis per ICHD-3 criteria to confirm migraine vs tension-type vs cluster

### 4.6 Considering Preventive Therapy
- Discuss if:
  - ≥4 migraine days/month
  - Attacks significantly impact quality of life
  - Acute medications are ineffective or contraindicated
  - Patient prefers to reduce attack frequency
- **Preventive options to discuss:** Beta-blockers (propranolol, metoprolol), amitriptyline, topiramate, valproate, CGRP monoclonal antibodies (erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab)

### 4.7 New Aura Symptoms
- First-ever visual aura, sensory aura, or speech aura during a migraine
- Even if non-alarming, new aura type warrants evaluation
- Important to exclude hemiplegic migraine or TIA

### 4.8 Migraine in Special Populations

#### Migraine With Aura + Oral Contraceptive Use
- Combination of migraine with aura + OCP use = significantly elevated stroke risk
- Must discuss with doctor; usually advised to switch to progesterone-only or non-hormonal contraception

#### Migraine During Pregnancy
- Many standard migraine medications are contraindicated in pregnancy
- Safe options are limited; doctor consultation required to adjust treatment plan
- **Safe in pregnancy (with guidance):** Paracetamol, metoclopramide, aspirin (avoid 3rd trimester)
- **Generally avoid in pregnancy:** NSAIDs (esp. after 20 wks), triptans (caution), ergotamines (contraindicated), valproate (contraindicated)

#### Pediatric Migraine
- Child diagnosed with migraines — ensure age-appropriate treatment plans
- Address school impact, sleep, dietary triggers
- Preventive options differ from adults (cyproheptadine, amitriptyline, propranolol for children)

#### Elderly Patients New to Migraine
- New-onset migraine after age 50 always requires investigation to rule out secondary causes

### 4.9 Anxiety, Depression, or Sleep Disorders Comorbid with Migraine
- Migraine is bidirectionally linked with anxiety, depression, and sleep disorders
- These comorbidities worsen migraine burden and vice versa
- Integrated management (amitriptyline, CBT, sleep hygiene) should be discussed with doctor

### 4.10 Botox / CGRP Therapy Eligibility
- Chronic migraine (≥15 days/month, ≥8 of which are migrainous) + failed ≥3 preventive medications
- Patient may be eligible for:
  - **OnabotulinumtoxinA (Botox):** 155–195 units every 12 weeks
  - **CGRP monoclonal antibodies:** Erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab (monthly or quarterly SC injection)

## 5. Migraine-Specific Self-Assessment Tools

### 5.1 MIDAS Score (Migraine Disability Assessment)
Use to assess if preventive therapy is warranted.

| Days lost in past 3 months | Score |
|---------------------------|-------|
| Household chores missed | |
| Work/school days missed | |
| Missed family/social activities | |
| Reduced productivity at work (≥50% reduction) | |
| Reduced productivity in household (≥50% reduction) | |

**MIDAS Grading:**
- 0–5: Grade I (Little or no disability)
- 6–10: Grade II (Mild disability)
- 11–20: Grade III (Moderate disability) → **Doctor appointment recommended**
- ≥21: Grade IV (Severe disability) → **Urgent doctor appointment recommended**

### 5.2 ID Migraine Screener (3-Question Tool)
Ask in the past 3 months, did headache cause:
1. Nausea or stomach upset?
2. Sensitivity to light?
3. Limitation of activities for a day or more?

**Score ≥2/3 = 93% sensitivity for migraine** — warrants formal diagnosis

### 5.3 HIT-6 Score (Headache Impact Test)
- ≥60 = Severe impact → urgent appointment
- 56–59 = Substantial impact → schedule appointment
- 50–55 = Moderate impact → monitor and consider appointment
- <50 = Little to no impact → self-management may suffice

## 6. Information to Prepare Before Calling/Seeing a Doctor

Gathering this information before calling or visiting a doctor helps expedite assessment:

### Headache Description
- Location (unilateral/bilateral, frontal, temporal, occipital)
- Quality (throbbing, pressure, stabbing)
- Severity (0–10 VAS scale)
- Onset: sudden vs gradual; what was happening when it started

### Timeline
- Duration of current attack
- Total hours/days with headache this month
- When attacks started (first ever, or change in pattern)

### Associated Symptoms
- Nausea, vomiting
- Photophobia, phonophobia, osmophobia
- Aura type and duration
- Any neurological symptoms

### Triggers (Identified or Suspected)
- Hormonal (menstrual cycle phase)
- Sleep changes (too much/too little)
- Dietary (caffeine, alcohol, skipped meals)
- Stress (emotional, physical)
- Weather/barometric pressure changes
- Sensory (bright lights, strong smells)
- Physical activity level

### Medications Used
- Medication name, dose, and time taken
- Whether it worked, partially worked, or failed
- How many days per month acute medications are used
- Any current preventive medications

### Medical History
- Any known diagnoses (hypertension, clotting disorders, cancer, HIV)
- Pregnancy status
- Family history of migraine or stroke

## 8. When NOT to Call an Ambulance (Self-Manageable Attacks)

The following situations generally do NOT require emergency or urgent medical care if the patient has an established migraine diagnosis and no red flags:

- Typical migraine matching prior episodes in character and severity
- Migraine responding (even partially) to previously prescribed medication
- Attack with known identified triggers and no new features
- Nausea/vomiting that is controlled and patient can remain hydrated
- Migraine lasting <72 hours with gradual improvement
- Known aura matching prior episodes (visual, sensory) resolving within 60 minutes

**Self-management strategies for typical attacks:**
- Early use of triptan (at first sign of headache, not during aura)
- Anti-emetic (metoclopramide, domperidone, prochlorperazine)
- Hydration (oral if tolerated)
- Rest in dark, quiet room
- Cold/warm compress to head/neck
- Avoid screen time during acute attack
- Do not drive

## 9. Doctor Types and When to See Them

| Situation | Appropriate Provider |
|-----------|---------------------|
| Emergency symptoms (thunderclap, neuro deficit) | Emergency Medicine Physician (A&E/ER) |
| Urgent non-emergency, new pattern | GP/Primary Care Physician (same-day appointment) |
| Diagnosis, prevention, complex management | Neurologist / Headache Specialist |
| Medication overuse, chronic migraine | Neurologist |
| Hormonal migraine, OCP-related | OB-GYN or Endocrinologist |
| Pediatric migraine | Pediatric Neurologist |
| Migraine with anxiety/depression | Psychiatrist or Psychologist (alongside neurologist) |
| Physiotherapy for cervicogenic triggers | Physiotherapist |
| Dietary trigger management | Registered Dietitian |
| Biofeedback / CBT for migraine | Clinical Psychologist |

## 10. Telehealth Appropriateness for Migraine

### Appropriate for Telehealth / Phone Consultation
- Medication refill or renewal request
- Adjusting preventive therapy dose
- Discussing headache diary patterns
- MOH concerns (early stage)
- Non-urgent aura changes without red flags
- Mental health comorbidity management

### NOT Appropriate for Telehealth — Attend In Person
- Any red flag headache (see red_flags.md)
- Neurological examination required
- Blood pressure measurement needed
- First new headache of unknown type
- Headache in child under 5 years
- Suspected GCA (requires ESR, fundoscopy, biopsy)

## 11. Headache Diary — Key Data Points to Track

A headache diary is essential for clinical decision-making and should record:

- **Date and day of week** of each attack
- **Time of onset and resolution**
- **Duration** (hours)
- **Severity** (0–10)
- **Location and character**
- **Associated symptoms** (nausea, photophobia, aura type, aura duration)
- **Suspected triggers** (menstrual, dietary, sleep, stress, weather)
- **Medications used** (name, dose, time)
- **Medication response** (complete/partial/no relief)
- **Disability impact** (missed work/school/activity: Y/N)
- **Menstrual cycle day** (for females)

**Recommended apps:** Migraine Buddy, N1-Headache, Curelator, Manage My Pain

## 13. Key Clinical Pearls for RAG Retrieval

- **Any new headache in a patient over 50 = warrants medical evaluation**
- **Headache with fever + stiff neck = meningitis until proven otherwise**
- **Thunderclap headache = SAH until proven otherwise — CT then LP**
- **Migraine >72 hours = status migrainosus = urgent/emergency care**
- **>10–15 medication days/month = medication overuse headache risk**
- **Aura + OCP = elevated stroke risk = must discuss with doctor**
- **MIDAS ≥11 or HIT-6 ≥56 = preventive therapy should be discussed**
- **Chronic migraine (≥15 days/month) failing 3 preventives = CGRP or Botox eligible**
- **Normal CT does not rule out all serious causes — LP may still be needed**
- **Safety netting must always accompany any chatbot headache advice**
