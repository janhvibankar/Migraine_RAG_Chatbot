## SECTION 1: What Is Migraine?

---

**Q1.1: What is a migraine?**  
A migraine is a complex neurological disorder characterized by recurrent attacks of moderate-to-severe headache, typically unilateral (one-sided), pulsating in quality, and lasting 4–72 hours. It is often accompanied by nausea, vomiting, and heightened sensitivity to light (photophobia) and sound (phonophobia). Migraine is the second most disabling condition worldwide and the leading cause of disability in people aged 15–49.

---

**Q1.2: Is migraine just a bad headache?**  
No. Migraine is a neurological disease, not simply a severe headache. It involves dysfunction of brain networks — including the trigeminal pain pathway and cortical spreading depression — and produces a wide range of symptoms beyond head pain, including nausea, vomiting, sensory hypersensitivity, cognitive impairment ("brain fog"), fatigue, aura, and postdrome. Many patients are significantly disabled during attacks.

---

**Q1.3: How common is migraine?**  
- Affects approximately **1 billion people worldwide** (roughly 15% of the global population)
- Affects **~12–15% of adults** in Western countries
- 3x more common in **women** than men (after puberty)
- **Female prevalence:** ~18–25%
- **Male prevalence:** ~6–9%
- Peak prevalence: ages **25–55**
- Affects **~10% of children and adolescents**
- About **2–3% of the population** has chronic migraine (≥15 headache days/month)

---

**Q1.4: What causes migraine?**  
The exact cause is not fully understood, but the leading mechanisms include:

- **Cortical Spreading Depression (CSD):** A slowly propagating wave of neuronal and glial depolarization across the cortex, believed to underlie migraine aura and trigger the pain phase
- **Trigeminovascular activation:** Trigeminal nerve fibers innervating meningeal blood vessels release neuropeptides (CGRP, substance P) causing neurogenic inflammation and pain signal transmission
- **CGRP (Calcitonin Gene-Related Peptide):** A key neuropeptide elevated during migraine attacks; its role has led to the development of CGRP-targeted therapies
- **Genetic factors:** Migraine is highly heritable (~50% genetic contribution); FHM (Familial Hemiplegic Migraine) genes include CACNA1A, ATP1A2, SCN1A
- **Sensitization:** Central and peripheral sensitization of pain pathways during and between attacks
- **Brainstem dysfunction:** The periaqueductal gray (PAG) and other brainstem nuclei act as a migraine "generator"

---

**Q1.5: Is migraine hereditary?**  
Yes. Migraine has a strong genetic component:
- First-degree relatives of migraine sufferers have a **2–4x higher risk**
- Concordance in identical (monozygotic) twins: ~50%
- Familial Hemiplegic Migraine (FHM) is a rare, directly inherited autosomal dominant form
- Common migraine is polygenic — multiple genes each contribute a small increase in risk
- Having a parent with migraine increases a child's risk to ~50%; if both parents have migraine, risk rises to ~75%

---

**Q1.6: What is the difference between primary and secondary headache?**  
- **Primary headache:** The headache IS the condition; no underlying structural or systemic cause (e.g., migraine, tension-type headache, cluster headache)
- **Secondary headache:** The headache is a SYMPTOM of another condition (e.g., meningitis, brain tumor, subarachnoid hemorrhage, hypertension, medication overuse)
- Migraine is a primary headache disorder
- Red flags (SNOOP10) help distinguish primary from secondary headaches

---

**Q1.7: What are the different types of migraine?**

| Type | Key Features |
|------|-------------|
| Migraine Without Aura (MO) | Most common (~75%); headache without preceding neurological symptoms |
| Migraine With Aura (MA) | ~25%; focal neurological symptoms (visual, sensory, speech) preceding or during headache |
| Chronic Migraine | ≥15 headache days/month for >3 months, ≥8 of which are migrainous |
| Menstrual Migraine | Attacks exclusively or predominantly around menstruation (−2 to +3 days) |
| Hemiplegic Migraine | Aura includes motor weakness (hemiplegia); can be familial or sporadic |
| Vestibular Migraine | Recurrent vestibular symptoms (vertigo, dizziness) with migraine history |
| Retinal Migraine | Monocular visual symptoms (one eye only); rare; requires investigation |
| Brainstem Aura Migraine | Aura symptoms originating from brainstem (dysarthria, diplopia, tinnitus, vertigo) |
| Silent/Acephalgic Migraine | Aura without headache; common in older patients |
| Status Migrainosus | Migraine attack lasting >72 hours |
| Transformed Migraine | Episodic migraine evolving into chronic migraine (often with MOH) |

## SECTION 2: Symptoms and Phases

---

**Q2.1: What are the four phases of a migraine attack?**

**Phase 1 — Prodrome (Pre-headache phase):**
- Occurs hours to 1–2 days before headache
- Present in ~60–77% of migraineurs
- Symptoms: yawning, food cravings (especially carbohydrates), mood changes (depression, irritability, euphoria), neck stiffness, fatigue, increased urination, fluid retention, difficulty concentrating
- **Early warning significance:** Prodrome symptoms are the earliest detectable warning signs and are central to migraine early warning prediction systems

**Phase 2 — Aura:**
- Present in ~25–30% of migraineurs
- Typically occurs 20–60 minutes before headache (can overlap with headache)
- Focal neurological symptoms that spread gradually ("march") and are fully reversible
- Symptom types: visual (most common), sensory, language/speech, motor (hemiplegic only), brainstem, retinal

**Phase 3 — Headache:**
- Lasts 4–72 hours (untreated or unsuccessfully treated)
- Unilateral in ~60% of cases (can be bilateral)
- Pulsating/throbbing quality
- Moderate to severe intensity
- Worsened by routine physical activity
- Associated: nausea (≥80%), vomiting (~30%), photophobia (≥80%), phonophobia (≥75%), osmophobia (smell sensitivity)
- Allodynia (pain from normally non-painful stimuli, e.g., brushing hair, touching skin) in ~70% during attack

**Phase 4 — Postdrome (Migraine hangover):**
- Follows resolution of headache; lasts hours to 48 hours
- Symptoms: fatigue, cognitive impairment/"brain fog," mood changes, poor concentration, neck stiffness, altered appetite
- Often underrecognized; can be as disabling as the headache phase

---

**Q2.2: What is migraine aura?**  
Aura refers to transient, fully reversible focal neurological symptoms that typically develop gradually over 5–20 minutes and last no longer than 60 minutes. Types include:

- **Visual aura (most common, ~90% of aura cases):**
  - Scintillating scotoma: shimmering, flickering arc of light with a central blind spot
  - Fortification spectrum: zigzag patterns resembling a fortress wall
  - Photopsia: flashing lights or sparks
  - Blurring or loss of part of visual field (homonymous hemianopia)

- **Sensory aura (~30–40% of aura cases):**
  - Pins and needles (paresthesia) spreading from hand up arm to face/lips
  - Numbness following the paresthesia

- **Dysphasic/Language aura (~10–20% of aura cases):**
  - Word-finding difficulty
  - Paraphasia (substituting words)

- **Motor aura (Hemiplegic Migraine only):**
  - Unilateral weakness or hemiplegia
  - Must rule out TIA/stroke especially on first episode

- **Brainstem aura:**
  - Dysarthria, vertigo, tinnitus, diplopia, ataxia, decreased consciousness

**Key aura features distinguishing it from TIA:**
- Gradual spread/"march" of symptoms (TIA is sudden onset)
- Positive symptoms (scintillations, tingling) precede negative (loss of vision, numbness)
- Duration 5–60 minutes
- Followed by headache in most cases

---

**Q2.3: What is the prodrome and why is it important for early warning?**  
The prodrome is the pre-headache phase occurring 6–48 hours before the headache. It is critical for early warning prediction because:
- It is the earliest detectable biological signal of an impending migraine
- Recognizing prodrome allows early intervention with medication (before headache sets in, when triptans work best)
- Common prodrome symptoms include: **yawning** (one of the most reliable early markers), **food cravings**, **mood changes**, **neck stiffness**, **fatigue**, **photophobia**, **cognitive slowing**, and **increased sensitivity to smells**
- Hypothalamic activation is believed to drive prodrome symptoms, as PET studies show hypothalamic activation up to 24 hours before headache onset

---

**Q2.4: What does a migraine headache feel like?**  
- **Location:** One side of the head (unilateral) in ~60%; can be bifrontal or bilateral especially in children
- **Quality:** Throbbing, pulsating, or pounding (in contrast to the pressing/tightening quality of tension-type headache)
- **Intensity:** Moderate to severe (typically 6–9/10)
- **Movement sensitivity:** Worsened by routine physical activity such as climbing stairs or walking
- **Associated symptoms:** Nausea, vomiting, sensitivity to light/sound/smell, allodynia

---

**Q2.5: What is allodynia in migraine?**  
Allodynia is the experience of pain from stimuli that are not normally painful. In migraine:
- Affects approximately 60–70% of migraineurs during an attack
- Examples: pain from brushing hair, wearing glasses, shaving, wearing earrings, light touch to the scalp or face
- Indicates **central sensitization** — the trigeminal nucleus caudalis has become sensitized
- Once allodynia develops during an attack, triptans become significantly less effective
- **Clinical importance:** Taking triptans early (before allodynia onset) greatly improves treatment success

---

**Q2.6: What is the postdrome ("migraine hangover")?**  
The postdrome follows the resolution of head pain and lasts 4–48 hours. Symptoms include:
- Cognitive impairment (brain fog, poor concentration, memory difficulty)
- Fatigue and exhaustion
- Mood changes (depression, irritability, or conversely, euphoria)
- Neck stiffness and soreness
- Dizziness
- Altered appetite
- Scalp tenderness
The postdrome can be as functionally disabling as the headache itself and is often underreported.

---

**Q2.7: Can migraine occur without headache?**  
Yes. **Acephalgic migraine (silent migraine)** is migraine with aura but without the headache phase. The patient experiences full aura symptoms (visual disturbance, sensory symptoms, speech difficulty) without subsequent head pain. More common in older adults. It can be alarming when it occurs for the first time — first episode always warrants evaluation to rule out TIA.

## SECTION 3: Triggers

---

**Q3.1: What are common migraine triggers?**

### Hormonal Triggers
- Menstruation (estrogen withdrawal, most powerful trigger)
- Ovulation
- Oral contraceptive pill (especially the pill-free week)
- Perimenopause and menopause (fluctuating estrogen)
- Hormone replacement therapy (HRT)
- Pregnancy (can improve or worsen migraine)

### Sleep Triggers
- Too little sleep (sleep deprivation)
- Too much sleep ("weekend migraine" from sleeping in)
- Irregular sleep schedule
- Sleep disorders (insomnia, sleep apnea)
- Jet lag / circadian rhythm disruption

### Dietary Triggers
- **Skipping meals / fasting** (one of the most common)
- **Dehydration** (inadequate fluid intake)
- **Caffeine** — both excess and withdrawal
- **Alcohol** — red wine and other alcohols (tyramine, histamine, sulfites)
- **Tyramine-containing foods:** aged cheeses, cured/fermented meats, pickled foods, soy sauce, fava beans
- **MSG (monosodium glutamate):** processed foods, Chinese food, fast food
- **Nitrates/Nitrites:** processed meats (hot dogs, bacon, deli meats)
- **Artificial sweeteners:** aspartame (controversial evidence)
- **Chocolate** (controversial — may be a prodrome craving rather than a true trigger)
- **Citrus fruits** (reported by some patients)

### Stress and Psychological Triggers
- Acute stress (emotional or mental)
- **"Let-down" headache:** Migraine triggered when stress suddenly releases (e.g., weekend after a stressful work week)
- Anxiety and worry
- Depression
- Emotional trauma

### Environmental Triggers
- **Bright or flickering lights:** fluorescent lights, sunlight glare, strobing
- **Strong smells:** perfume, paint, cigarette smoke, cleaning products
- **Loud noise**
- **Weather changes:** especially drops in barometric pressure, storms, changes in temperature or humidity
- **Altitude change**
- **Strong winds** (reported in some geographic regions)

### Physical Triggers
- Intense or unaccustomed physical exercise
- Neck or shoulder muscle tension
- Poor posture (especially screen use)
- Teeth grinding (bruxism)
- Temporomandibular joint (TMJ) dysfunction
- Sexual activity (exertional headache — also a red flag on first occurrence)

### Medication Triggers
- Medication overuse / analgesic rebound
- Nitroglycerin / nitrates (vasodilators)
- Certain antihypertensives
- Hormone medications
- Opioid withdrawal

### Sensory Triggers
- High-altitude environments
- Motion sickness
- Flickering screens / video games / VR

---

**Q3.2: How do I identify my personal triggers?**  
- Keep a **headache diary** for at least 2–3 months, recording: date, time of onset, severity, duration, potential exposures (food, sleep, stress, menstrual cycle, weather), and medications
- Look for patterns across multiple attacks — a true trigger appears consistently before attacks
- **Important caveat:** What feels like a trigger may sometimes be a prodrome symptom (e.g., craving chocolate or salty foods is often a prodrome, not a trigger for the subsequent attack)
- Use validated tracking apps: Migraine Buddy, N1-Headache, Curelator Headache
- Note that triggers are **additive** — a single trigger may not cause an attack, but several together exceed the threshold

---

**Q3.3: What is the migraine threshold theory?**  
The migraine threshold (or "trigger threshold") theory proposes that each person with migraine has a genetically determined threshold for attack initiation. Triggers do not directly cause migraine; rather, they lower the threshold or raise the neuronal excitability level. When accumulated excitability exceeds the threshold, an attack fires:
- Days with multiple triggers or low resilience (poor sleep, hormonal changes) are highest risk
- This explains why the same trigger doesn't always produce an attack
- The threshold varies over time — it can be raised by good sleep, hydration, regular meals, and stress management

---

**Q3.4: Is caffeine a trigger or a treatment?**  
Both — and this is a key clinical nuance:
- **Caffeine as treatment:** At low-to-moderate doses (1–2 cups of coffee), caffeine potentiates analgesics and is an ingredient in combination headache medications (e.g., aspirin + paracetamol + caffeine); vasoconstrictive properties can help abort acute attacks
- **Caffeine as trigger:** Caffeine withdrawal headache (especially on weekends or after missed morning coffee); high daily caffeine use (>200 mg/day) raises baseline and leads to dependency and rebound
- **Recommendation:** Maintain consistent, moderate caffeine intake; avoid sudden changes in caffeine consumption; taper if trying to reduce intake

---

**Q3.5: Does weather cause migraine?**  
Yes — weather is one of the most reported triggers:
- **Barometric pressure changes** (especially drops) are the best-evidenced weather trigger; ~50% of migraineurs identify weather as a trigger
- Storm fronts, chinook winds, and rapid pressure changes are common precipitants
- High heat and high humidity
- Bright sunlight and glare
- Wind (especially warm, dry winds)
- Temperature extremes
Patients cannot control weather but can monitor pressure forecasts (apps like Migraine Forecast, Weather Migraine) and take pre-emptive action.

## SECTION 4: Diagnosis

---

**Q4.1: How is migraine diagnosed?**  
Migraine is a **clinical diagnosis** — there is no blood test, imaging, or biomarker that diagnoses it. Diagnosis is based on:

1. **Patient history and symptom pattern** meeting ICHD-3 diagnostic criteria
2. **Neurological examination** (should be normal in primary migraine)
3. **Exclusion of secondary headache** causes where red flags are present

Investigations (MRI, blood tests) are performed only when red flags are present, not routinely.

---

**Q4.2: What are the ICHD-3 diagnostic criteria for migraine without aura?**

At least **5 attacks** fulfilling criteria B–D:

**B.** Headache lasting **4–72 hours** (untreated or unsuccessfully treated)

**C.** At least **2 of 4** characteristics:
1. Unilateral location
2. Pulsating quality
3. Moderate or severe pain intensity
4. Aggravated by or causing avoidance of routine physical activity

**D.** During headache, at least **1 of the following:**
1. Nausea and/or vomiting
2. Photophobia AND phonophobia

**E.** Not better accounted for by another ICHD-3 diagnosis

---

**Q4.3: What are the ICHD-3 diagnostic criteria for migraine with aura?**

At least **2 attacks** fulfilling criteria B–C:

**B.** One or more of the following fully reversible aura symptoms:
1. Visual
2. Sensory
3. Speech and/or language
4. Motor (hemiplegic migraine only)
5. Brainstem
6. Retinal

**C.** At least **3 of 6** characteristics:
1. At least one aura symptom spreads gradually over ≥5 minutes
2. Two or more aura symptoms occur in succession
3. Each individual aura symptom lasts 5–60 minutes
4. At least one aura symptom is unilateral
5. At least one aura symptom is positive
6. The aura is accompanied, or followed within 60 minutes, by headache

---

**Q4.4: What is the ID Migraine Screener?**  
A validated 3-question screening tool for migraine diagnosis:

In the last 3 months, did your headache cause:
1. Nausea or upset stomach?
2. Sensitivity to light?
3. Limitations in your activities for a day or more?

**Scoring:** 2 or more YES answers = 93% sensitive and 82% specific for migraine diagnosis.

---

**Q4.5: Should I get an MRI for my migraine?**  
In most cases, no. Neuroimaging is **not required** for a typical migraine diagnosis with normal neurological examination. MRI or CT is recommended when:
- Red flags are present (sudden onset, neurological deficits, fever, age >50 new headache)
- The headache is atypical or doesn't fit primary headache criteria
- Neurological examination is abnormal
- The patient has failed standard treatments and the diagnosis is uncertain
- First-ever hemiplegic migraine or prolonged aura

---

**Q4.6: What conditions are often confused with migraine?**

| Condition | Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|------------------------|
| Tension-type headache | Bilateral, pressing/tightening (not pulsating), mild-moderate severity, no significant nausea or photophobia/phonophobia |
| Cluster headache | Strictly unilateral periorbital, excruciating, short (15–180 min), autonomic features (tearing, nasal congestion, ptosis), restlessness |
| Sinusitis headache | True sinusitis headache is rare; most "sinus headaches" are actually migraine; needs purulent discharge, fever, facial tenderness on imaging |
| TIA/Stroke | Sudden-onset deficit without gradual spread; no positive symptoms in TIA; requires urgent evaluation |
| Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension (IIH) | Obese women, papilledema, pulsatile tinnitus, visual disturbance, CSF opening pressure >25 cmH₂O |
| Trigeminal Neuralgia | Brief electric-shock-like lancinating pain, triggered by touch; no photophobia/nausea |
| Cervicogenic Headache | Originates from cervical spine; unilateral, reproduced by neck movement/pressure on occipital nerve |

## SECTION 5: Treatment — Acute (Abortive)

---

**Q5.1: What medications are used to treat a migraine attack?**

### Step 1 — Mild to Moderate Attacks
- **Paracetamol (acetaminophen):** 1000 mg; evidence is modest but useful for mild attacks
- **NSAIDs:** Ibuprofen 400–600 mg, naproxen 500 mg, aspirin 900 mg + metoclopramide 10 mg (evidence-based first-line)
- **Combination analgesics:** Aspirin + paracetamol + caffeine (e.g., Excedrin Migraine)
- **Anti-emetics alone or combined:** Metoclopramide 10 mg, prochlorperazine, domperidone (also treat nausea and improve gastric motility for drug absorption)

### Step 2 — Moderate to Severe Attacks / Failed Step 1
- **Triptans (5-HT1B/1D agonists):** First-line for moderate-severe migraine
  - Sumatriptan: 50–100 mg oral; 6 mg SC (fastest onset); 20 mg nasal spray
  - Rizatriptan: 10 mg oral (faster onset than sumatriptan oral)
  - Zolmitriptan: 2.5–5 mg oral or nasal spray
  - Eletriptan: 40–80 mg oral (higher efficacy, longer half-life)
  - Almotriptan, frovatriptan, naratriptan (longer acting, lower recurrence)
- **Ergotamines:** Ergotamine + caffeine; dihydroergotamine (DHE) nasal spray or IV/IM — less used due to side effects

### Step 3 — Severe / Refractory / Status Migrainosus
- **IV or IM administration** in emergency/urgent care setting:
  - IV prochlorperazine or metoclopramide (dopamine antagonists — highly effective)
  - IV valproate sodium
  - IV/IM ketorolac (NSAID)
  - IV magnesium sulfate (especially migraine with aura)
  - SC or IM sumatriptan
  - IV DHE (dihydroergotamine)
  - Dexamethasone (to prevent headache recurrence after acute treatment)

### Newer Agents
- **Gepants (CGRP receptor antagonists):** Ubrogepant, rimegepant, zavegepant — oral or nasal; no vasoconstriction; can be used when triptans are contraindicated (e.g., cardiovascular disease); rimegepant also has preventive properties
- **Ditans (5-HT1F agonists):** Lasmiditan — no vasoconstriction; useful in patients with cardiovascular risk factors

---

**Q5.2: How do triptans work?**  
Triptans are selective **5-HT1B/1D receptor agonists** (serotonin receptor agonists):
- **5-HT1B agonism:** Causes vasoconstriction of dilated meningeal blood vessels
- **5-HT1D agonism:** Inhibits release of CGRP and other neuropeptides from trigeminal nerve terminals; reduces neurogenic inflammation; blocks pain signal transmission in the trigeminal nucleus
- Result: Reversal of the peripheral sensitization underlying migraine pain
- **Contraindications:** Coronary artery disease, uncontrolled hypertension, hemiplegic migraine, basilar migraine, stroke history, pregnancy (relative), concurrent MAOI use
- **Key rule:** Take triptans early in the headache phase, before allodynia develops, for best efficacy

---

**Q5.3: Why doesn't my migraine medication work?**  
Common reasons for treatment failure:

- **Timing:** Taken too late (after allodynia onset) — triptans have significantly lower efficacy once central sensitization occurs
- **Gastroparesis:** Delayed gastric emptying during migraine slows oral drug absorption; consider nasal spray, SC injection, or rectal formulations; or take an anti-emetic like metoclopramide first to restore gastric motility
- **Dose:** Insufficient dose used
- **Wrong medication for attack severity:** Simple analgesics for severe attacks; use a triptan
- **Medication overuse headache (MOH):** Overuse of acute medications paradoxically worsens headache frequency and reduces medication efficacy
- **Wrong triptan:** Different triptans have different pharmacokinetic profiles; if one fails, trying another may be effective (up to 40% of non-responders to one triptan will respond to another)
- **Dehydration:** Oral medications absorb poorly when dehydrated
- **Misdiagnosis:** Headache may not be migraine, or there may be a comorbid condition

---

**Q5.4: What is medication overuse headache (MOH)?**  
MOH (also called "rebound headache" or "analgesic overuse headache") is a secondary headache disorder arising from overuse of acute headache medications:

- **Thresholds triggering MOH:**
  - Triptans, ergotamines, opioids, combination analgesics: **≥10 days/month** for >3 months
  - Simple analgesics (paracetamol, aspirin, NSAIDs): **≥15 days/month** for >3 months

- **Characteristics:**
  - Headache present >15 days/month
  - Headache worsens when medication is not taken
  - Medications become progressively less effective
  - Often presents as a dull daily baseline headache with superimposed migraine attacks

- **Treatment:** Medication withdrawal (detoxification) — often supervised; bridging therapy with naproxen, amitriptyline, or prednisone taper; transitioning to preventive therapy; behavioral support

---

**Q5.5: Can I take triptans and paracetamol together?**  
Yes. Combining a triptan with an NSAID (naproxen 500 mg) or paracetamol is evidence-based and improves efficacy compared to either drug alone. Fixed-dose combinations (e.g., sumatriptan/naproxen) are approved for this purpose. The combination also reduces headache recurrence.

---

**Q5.6: What is the best way to take migraine medication?**  
- **Timing:** As early as possible once headache begins (not during aura — triptans are not effective for aura and may not be necessary if taken during aura before headache)
- **Hydration:** Take medication with a full glass of water
- **Anti-emetic first:** If nausea is severe, take an anti-emetic 15–20 minutes before analgesic to improve gastric motility and drug absorption
- **Route:** If vomiting is present, oral medications may not be absorbed — use nasal spray, SC injection, suppository, or IM routes
- **Avoid:** Lying down immediately after taking triptans if nausea is severe (may worsen nausea)
- **Limit:** Maximum 2 triptan doses per 24 hours; maximum 10 triptan days/month

## SECTION 6: Treatment — Preventive (Prophylactic)

---

**Q6.1: When should preventive migraine treatment be started?**  
Preventive therapy is indicated when:
- **≥4 migraine days per month** (primary threshold)
- Acute medications are ineffective, contraindicated, or not tolerated
- Quality of life significantly impaired (MIDAS grade III–IV, HIT-6 ≥56)
- Acute medication overuse or risk of MOH
- Patient preference to reduce attack frequency
- Specific migraine subtypes: hemiplegic migraine, migraine with prolonged aura, basilar migraine, very frequent aura

---

**Q6.2: What preventive medications are available?**

### Established Oral Preventives
| Medication | Class | Evidence | Notes |
|-----------|-------|----------|-------|
| Propranolol | Beta-blocker | Level A | 40–240 mg/day; contraindicated in asthma, bradycardia, depression |
| Metoprolol | Beta-blocker | Level A | 50–200 mg/day |
| Amitriptyline | Tricyclic antidepressant | Level B | 10–150 mg at night; helps comorbid insomnia and depression |
| Topiramate | Anticonvulsant | Level A | 50–200 mg/day; cognitive side effects; weight loss; teratogenic |
| Valproate/Valproic acid | Anticonvulsant | Level A | 500–1500 mg/day; teratogenic (avoid in women of childbearing age) |
| Venlafaxine | SNRI | Level B | 75–150 mg/day; helps comorbid anxiety/depression |
| Candesartan | Angiotensin receptor blocker | Level B | 16 mg/day; useful in hypertensive migraineurs |
| Lisinopril | ACE inhibitor | Level B | 10–20 mg/day |
| Flunarizine | Calcium channel blocker | Level A (not available in US) | 5–10 mg at night |
| Pizotifen | Serotonin antagonist | Level B | 0.5–3 mg at night; weight gain |
| Magnesium glycinate/citrate | Supplement | Level B | 400–600 mg/day; especially helpful for menstrual migraine and migraine with aura |
| Riboflavin (Vitamin B2) | Supplement | Level B | 400 mg/day; well tolerated; urine discoloration |
| Coenzyme Q10 | Supplement | Level C | 300 mg/day |

### CGRP-Targeted Biologics (Monoclonal Antibodies)
| Medication | Target | Dosing | Route |
|-----------|--------|--------|-------|
| Erenumab (Aimovig) | CGRP receptor | 70–140 mg monthly | SC |
| Fremanezumab (Ajovy) | CGRP ligand | 225 mg monthly or 675 mg quarterly | SC |
| Galcanezumab (Emgality) | CGRP ligand | 120 mg monthly | SC |
| Eptinezumab (Vyepti) | CGRP ligand | 100–300 mg quarterly | IV infusion |

- Indicated for episodic migraine (≥4 days/month) or chronic migraine
- Faster onset than oral preventives (effect within 1 month)
- Better tolerability profile than oral preventives
- Approved after failure of ≥2–3 oral preventive trials in most guidelines

### OnabotulinumtoxinA (Botox)
- Approved **only for chronic migraine** (≥15 headache days/month, ≥8 migrainous)
- 155–195 units injected into 31–39 sites across head and neck muscles every 12 weeks
- Mechanism: inhibits presynaptic release of CGRP and other neuropeptides at peripheral trigeminal nerve terminals
- Typically requires 2–3 treatment cycles before full benefit is seen

---

**Q6.3: How long does preventive treatment take to work?**  
- **Oral preventives:** 8–12 weeks at therapeutic dose to assess efficacy; do not abandon therapy prematurely
- **CGRP monoclonal antibodies:** Faster onset — meaningful effect often within 1 month; full benefit by 3–6 months
- **Botox:** Often 2–3 treatment cycles (6–9 months) for full effect
- A successful outcome is defined as **≥50% reduction in headache days per month**

---

**Q6.4: What non-pharmacological preventive options exist?**

- **Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT):** Addresses stress, avoidance behavior, and psychological comorbidities; evidence-based as standalone or adjunct therapy
- **Biofeedback:** Teaches physiological self-regulation (skin temperature, muscle tension, heart rate variability); Level A evidence
- **Relaxation training:** Progressive muscle relaxation, deep breathing — reduces sympathetic activation
- **Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR):** Emerging evidence for reducing migraine frequency and improving quality of life
- **Aerobic exercise:** Regular moderate exercise (3–5x/week, 30–45 min) reduces migraine frequency; Level B evidence; effect comparable to topiramate in some studies
- **Sleep hygiene:** Regular sleep schedule, adequate sleep duration (7–9 hours), avoiding screens before bed
- **Dietary regularity:** Eating regular meals, maintaining hydration, limiting dietary triggers
- **Acupuncture:** Level A evidence (non-inferior to preventive pharmacotherapy in several RCTs)
- **Neuromodulation devices:**
  - Cefaly (external trigeminal nerve stimulator — eTNS)
  - SpringTMS / eNeura (single-pulse transcranial magnetic stimulator — sTMS)
  - gammaCore (non-invasive vagus nerve stimulator — nVNS)
  - Nerivio (remote electrical neuromodulation — REN)

## SECTION 7: Migraine and Lifestyle

---

**Q7.1: What lifestyle changes help reduce migraines?**

**Sleep Regularity:**
- Go to bed and wake up at the same time every day, including weekends
- Target 7–9 hours of sleep per night
- Avoid significant oversleeping (triggers "weekend migraine")
- Manage sleep disorders (insomnia, sleep apnea)

**Dietary Habits:**
- Eat regular meals — never skip breakfast; aim for meals every 4–5 hours
- Stay well hydrated (8–10 glasses of water per day; more in heat or exercise)
- Limit or eliminate identified dietary triggers
- Maintain consistent caffeine intake; avoid sudden changes

**Stress Management:**
- Daily relaxation practice (meditation, progressive relaxation, deep breathing)
- Regular downtime and pacing to avoid "let-down" headaches
- CBT or counseling for significant anxiety/depression
- Set boundaries to avoid chronic work stress

**Exercise:**
- Regular moderate aerobic exercise (brisk walking, cycling, swimming) 3–5x/week
- Avoid very intense sudden exercise if this is a known trigger; warm up gradually
- Exercise reduces stress, improves sleep, and reduces migraine frequency

**Screen Habits:**
- Use blue-light filter / night mode on devices
- Follow 20-20-20 rule (every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds)
- Ensure adequate room lighting; avoid working in dim rooms with bright screens

**Alcohol:**
- Reduce or eliminate alcohol, especially red wine, beer, and dark spirits
- If drinking, stay well hydrated and avoid drinking on an empty stomach

---

**Q7.2: Does exercise help or trigger migraine?**  
Both — it depends on the context:
- **Regular moderate aerobic exercise** reduces migraine frequency over time (comparable in efficacy to topiramate preventive therapy in some RCTs)
- **Sudden high-intensity exertion** can trigger an attack — especially if dehydrated, unfed, or in heat
- **To exercise safely:** Warm up gradually; stay hydrated before, during, and after; eat beforehand; avoid exercising in extreme heat; maintain consistent schedule

---

**Q7.3: Does diet affect migraine?**  
Yes. Dietary factors affect migraine in several ways:
- **Skipping meals:** One of the strongest triggers — causes hypoglycemia, activating trigeminal pain pathways
- **Dehydration:** Even mild dehydration (1–2% of body weight) can trigger attacks
- **Dietary triggers (variable by individual):** Tyramine, MSG, nitrates, alcohol, caffeine
- **Protective dietary patterns:** Mediterranean diet (anti-inflammatory); adequate magnesium intake; omega-3 fatty acids
- **Note:** Food cravings (for chocolate, carbs) before a migraine are often prodrome symptoms, not triggers — the food did not cause the migraine

---

**Q7.4: How does sleep affect migraine?**  
Sleep and migraine have a strong bidirectional relationship:
- Both **too little** and **too much** sleep trigger migraines
- **REM sleep** is particularly important — disruption of REM sleep is associated with increased attack frequency
- **Insomnia** is 2–3x more common in migraineurs and worsens migraine burden
- **Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA)** is associated with increased migraine frequency; treating OSA can reduce migraine
- Morning migraines (waking with headache) may be related to sleep apnea or REM-phase trigeminal activation
- Migraine attacks themselves can disrupt sleep, creating a vicious cycle

---

**Q7.5: Does stress cause migraine?**  
Stress is the most commonly reported migraine trigger (reported by 50–80% of patients):
- Acute emotional or psychological stress activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and increases cortisol and catecholamine release, which can lower the migraine threshold
- **"Let-down" headache** is a well-documented phenomenon: migraines triggered by the release of stress (e.g., onset on Friday evening after a stressful work week, or on the first day of vacation) — possibly mediated by rapid cortisol drops
- Chronic stress contributes to central sensitization and migraine chronification
- **Management:** CBT, mindfulness, pacing, biofeedback, regular schedule adherence

---

## SECTION 8: Migraine in Special Populations

---

**Q8.1: Does migraine change during the menstrual cycle?**  
Yes — the menstrual cycle is one of the most powerful influences on migraine:

- **Menstrual migraine** (attacks occurring −2 to +3 days relative to menstruation) affects ~50% of female migraineurs
- **Pure menstrual migraine** (attacks exclusively at menstruation, no other times): ~10–14%
- **Mechanism:** The sharp drop in estrogen levels before menstruation (the "estrogen withdrawal" trigger) lowers the migraine threshold; prostaglandin release during menstruation also contributes
- Menstrual migraines are characteristically **more severe, longer-lasting, and more resistant to treatment** than non-menstrual attacks
- **Management options:**
  - Pre-emptive NSAIDs (naproxen 500mg BID) starting 2–3 days before expected onset
  - Pre-emptive triptans (frovatriptan, naratriptan — longer acting) 2–3 days before expected onset
  - Transdermal estrogen patches (100 μg) applied perimenstrually to stabilize estrogen levels
  - Continuous OCP use to eliminate the pill-free withdrawal week
  - Standard migraine preventive therapy for non-pure menstrual migraine

---

**Q8.2: Does migraine improve during pregnancy?**  
- **Migraine without aura:** Improves in 60–80% of patients, often dramatically, especially from the 2nd trimester onward — due to stable, rising estrogen levels
- **Migraine with aura:** Less improvement; aura frequency may remain unchanged or worsen
- **1st trimester:** Often the worst period — nausea, hormonal flux, restricted medication options
- **Postpartum:** Migraine frequently returns (within days to weeks of delivery) due to estrogen drop; breastfeeding may be protective (maintains estrogen)
- **Safe medications in pregnancy:** Paracetamol (first choice), metoclopramide, prochlorperazine
- **Avoid in pregnancy:** NSAIDs after 20 weeks, triptans (caution — some limited use may be acceptable), ergotamines (contraindicated), valproate (teratogenic — neural tube defects, FETAL valproate syndrome), topiramate (teratogenic)

---

**Q8.3: Can children have migraines?**  
Yes — migraine affects approximately **10% of school-age children** and **28% of adolescents**. Features differ from adult migraine:

- **Duration:** Shorter — as brief as 2 hours (vs 4 hours minimum in adults)
- **Location:** Often **bilateral** (frontal or temporal) in younger children; unilateral more common in adolescents
- **Vomiting:** More prominent than in adults
- **Sleep:** Children often sleep off migraine and feel better on waking
- **Childhood migraine equivalents (precursors):**
  - Abdominal migraine: Recurrent episodic central abdominal pain with nausea/vomiting; no headache
  - Cyclic vomiting syndrome: Recurrent stereotyped episodes of severe nausea/vomiting
  - Benign paroxysmal vertigo of childhood

**Treatment in children:**
- **Acute:** Ibuprofen 10 mg/kg (first choice); paracetamol 15 mg/kg; sumatriptan nasal spray (approved ≥12 yrs); rizatriptan (approved ≥6 yrs in US)
- **Preventive:** Propranolol, amitriptyline (low dose at night), cyproheptadine (younger children), topiramate, flunarizine

**Non-pharmacological:** Sleep hygiene, regular meals, hydration, school management plan, CBT

---

**Q8.4: Does migraine change at menopause?**  
- **Perimenopause:** Migraine often **worsens** — erratic estrogen fluctuations increase attack frequency and severity
- **Post-menopause (natural):** Migraine typically **improves** — stable (low) estrogen removes the trigger of estrogen fluctuation
- **Surgical menopause:** Abrupt estrogen drop often causes severe migraine worsening
- **HRT and migraine:**
  - Oral HRT: pulsatile estrogen levels; can worsen migraine
  - **Transdermal HRT** (patches, gels): more stable estrogen delivery; preferred in migraineurs
  - **Continuous combined HRT** preferred over cyclical to avoid progestogen withdrawal headaches

---

**Q8.5: Is it safe to use the contraceptive pill if I have migraine?**  

| Migraine Type | OCP Safety |
|--------------|-----------|
| Migraine without aura | Generally acceptable; monitor for worsening |
| Migraine with aura | **Contraindicated with combined (estrogen-containing) OCP** — significantly increased ischemic stroke risk (WHO Category 4) |
| Migraine with aura + smoking | Absolute contraindication to combined OCP |
| Migraine with aura + age >35 | Absolute contraindication to combined OCP |

**Safer alternatives for contraception in migraine with aura:**
- Progesterone-only pill (mini-pill)
- Hormonal IUS (Mirena)
- Barrier methods
- Copper IUD
- Implant (nexplanon — progesterone only)

---

**Q8.6: Can migraine cause a stroke?**  
- **Migrainous infarction** is a rare but recognized complication — ischemic stroke occurring during a migraine aura (aura symptoms do not resolve within 60 minutes and imaging confirms infarction)
- Estimated incidence: <1% of strokes; <1 per 100,000 per year
- **Risk factors that elevate stroke risk in migraineurs:**
  - Migraine with aura (2–4x baseline stroke risk)
  - Female sex + combined OCP use + migraine with aura (highest risk combination)
  - Smoking
  - Hypertension
  - Age >45
- **Migraine without aura:** No significantly elevated stroke risk
- **PFO (Patent Foramen Ovale):** More common in migraine with aura patients; closure does not reliably reduce migraine

## SECTION 9: Chronic Migraine and Refractory Migraine

---

**Q9.1: What is chronic migraine?**  
Chronic migraine (CM) is defined by the ICHD-3 as:
- **≥15 headache days per month** for >3 months
- **≥8 of those days** meeting criteria for migraine or responding to migraine-specific treatment
- NOT attributable to medication overuse or another cause

- Affects approximately **2–3% of the general population**
- Often evolves from episodic migraine (episodic-to-chronic transformation)
- Associated with significant disability, psychiatric comorbidity, and reduced quality of life

---

**Q9.2: What causes episodic migraine to become chronic?**  
Risk factors for chronification (transformation from episodic to chronic migraine):

**Modifiable:**
- Medication overuse (strongest modifiable risk factor)
- Obesity (BMI >30)
- Poor sleep quality
- High caffeine intake
- Stressful life events
- Anxiety and depression
- Snoring/sleep apnea

**Non-modifiable:**
- Female sex
- Low socioeconomic status
- Genetic predisposition
- Head injury history

---

**Q9.3: What is refractory migraine?**  
Refractory migraine refers to migraine that is unresponsive or inadequately responsive to multiple standard treatments. Definitions vary, but typically includes:
- Failure of ≥3 adequate trials of preventive medications from different drug classes
- Inadequate response to acute treatments including triptans, NSAIDs, and/or ergotamines
- Persistent significant disability despite optimized treatment
- Often requires specialist (headache center) management, CGRP antibodies, Botox, or experimental therapies

## SECTION 11: Migraine Early Warning and Prediction

---

**Q11.1: Can migraines be predicted?**  
Yes — to a meaningful degree. Migraine prediction draws on:
- **Prodrome symptoms:** Yawning, food cravings, neck stiffness, mood changes (identifiable hours to 2 days before headache)
- **Trigger pattern recognition:** Identifying personal triggers from headache diary data
- **Wearable data:** Heart rate variability (HRV), sleep patterns, activity levels, skin conductance
- **Barometric pressure monitoring:** Pressure drops correlate with attacks in susceptible individuals
- **Hormonal cycle tracking:** Menstrual cycle phase prediction
- **Machine learning models:** Applied to longitudinal patient diary data to generate personalized attack probability scores

---

**Q11.2: What are the earliest warning signs of an impending migraine?**

**Hours to 2 days before (Prodrome):**
- Excessive yawning (one of the most reliable and earliest markers — driven by hypothalamic dopaminergic activity)
- Strong food cravings (especially sweet or salty foods)
- Mood changes: depression, irritability, or unusual euphoria/elation
- Increased thirst and urination
- Neck stiffness or soreness
- Fatigue and lethargy
- Difficulty concentrating or "foggy" thinking
- Increased sensitivity to light, sound, or smell even before headache
- Fluid retention or bloating
- Pallor

**Minutes to 1 hour before (Aura, if present):**
- Visual disturbances (scintillating scotoma, zigzag lines, flashes)
- Tingling or numbness in face, hand, or arm spreading gradually
- Difficulty finding words

---

**Q11.3: How can I track my migraine patterns for prediction?**  
- Use a **headache diary** consistently — record date, time, severity, duration, potential triggers (sleep, food, stress, hormones, weather), and medications
- **Apps:** Migraine Buddy (most widely used, AI insights), N1-Headache (causal analysis), Curelator (pattern identification), Manage My Pain, Headache Diary Pro
- **Wearables:** Track sleep quality, HRV, activity; some research suggests HRV drops preceding migraine attacks
- **Key variables to track:**
  - Sleep duration and quality each night
  - Meals (timing, composition, skipped meals)
  - Hydration
  - Stress level (0–10 scale)
  - Menstrual cycle day
  - Weather (barometric pressure if possible)
  - Alcohol consumption
  - Exercise
  - Screen time
  - Mood and energy

---

**Q11.4: What is the role of AI/ML in migraine prediction?**  
Machine learning and AI are being actively applied to migraine prediction:
- **Personalized trigger identification:** ML models can identify which triggers are most predictive for an individual from diary data, reducing false positives from population-level associations
- **Attack probability scoring:** Time-series models (e.g., LSTM, random forest) trained on individual longitudinal data can output day-ahead attack probability
- **Wearable integration:** Detecting physiological precursors (HRV changes, skin conductance, temperature) in the hours before an attack
- **NLP on symptom diaries:** Extracting prodromal symptom patterns from free-text or structured diary entries
- **Research tools:** N-of-1 trial methodologies and platforms like Curelator Headache use ML to identify n=1 trigger-outcome relationships
- **Predictive biomarkers under research:** CGRP levels (elevated interictally in chronic migraine), blood biomarkers, neuroimaging signatures

## SECTION 12: General Patient Questions

---

**Q12.1: Will my migraine ever go away?**  
- Migraine is a chronic neurological condition for most people, but its course is variable:
- In many individuals, **frequency and severity decrease with age**, particularly after age 50–60
- **Post-menopausal women** often see significant improvement
- **Men** frequently see improvement in middle age
- A minority of patients experience complete remission
- Effective management (preventive therapy, lifestyle modification, trigger management) can dramatically reduce burden even when migraine doesn't fully resolve
- Without treatment, episodic migraine can progress to chronic migraine

---

**Q12.2: Is there a cure for migraine?**  
Currently, there is **no cure** for migraine. However:
- **Effective preventive therapies** can reduce attack frequency by 50% or more in most patients
- **Acute therapies** (especially triptans and gepants) can abort attacks effectively
- **CGRP-targeted therapies** represent a major advance in treatment specific to the underlying biology
- Ongoing research is investigating CGRP pathway modulation, neuroinflammation, and biomarker-guided treatment
- Lifestyle and behavioral interventions provide significant, durable improvements

---

**Q12.3: Can I drive during a migraine?**  
**No.** Driving during a migraine is dangerous:
- Visual disturbances (aura) severely impair vision
- Cognitive impairment, slowed reaction times, and photophobia reduce driving ability
- Many acute migraine medications (particularly those causing sedation — e.g., opioids, prochlorperazine, antihistamines) further impair driving
- Triptans and anti-emetics may cause drowsiness
- **Recommendation:** Never drive during an aura or active headache phase; arrange alternative transport or wait until fully recovered

---

**Q12.4: Can I exercise during a migraine?**  
- **During a moderate-severe attack:** Exercise is generally not recommended; physical activity worsens migraine pain; rest in a dark, quiet room is preferable
- **During a mild/prodrome phase:** Light activity may be tolerable; some patients report that gentle exercise (walking) during early prodrome may avert a full attack
- **Between attacks:** Regular exercise is strongly recommended as a preventive strategy

---

**Q12.5: What should I do when a migraine starts?**  
Acute migraine action plan:
1. **Recognize the attack early** — act at first sign of headache (not aura)
2. **Take acute medication immediately** (triptan or NSAID + anti-emetic as prescribed)
3. **Hydrate** — drink 2 glasses of water
4. **Stop activities** — go to a dark, quiet room; reduce screen exposure
5. **Rest or sleep** if possible — sleep is one of the most effective migraine aborters
6. **Apply a cold or warm compress** to head/neck
7. **Avoid strong smells, noise, and bright light**
8. **Do not drive**
9. **Track the attack** in your headache diary (onset time, severity, medications, response)
10. **Follow up** if attack is unusually severe, prolonged, or not responding to usual treatment

---

**Q12.6: Are migraines dangerous?**  
For the vast majority of people, migraine itself is not dangerous — it is painful and disabling but not life-threatening. However:
- **Migrainous infarction** (stroke during aura) is a rare complication
- **Status migrainosus** (>72 hrs) can lead to dehydration and hospital admission
- **Elevated stroke risk** exists in migraine with aura patients with additional risk factors (OCP, smoking, hypertension)
- The greatest danger is **misdiagnosing a secondary headache** (such as SAH) as migraine — hence the importance of red flag recognition

---

**Q12.7: Does migraine affect mental health?**  
Yes, significantly:
- **Depression:** 2–4x more common in migraineurs; both conditions share serotonergic and inflammatory pathways
- **Anxiety:** The most common psychiatric comorbidity; anticipatory anxiety about attacks ("fear of next migraine") worsens quality of life and can trigger attacks
- **PTSD:** Elevated in chronic migraine
- **Suicidality:** Elevated risk in patients with chronic migraine + depression; routine screening recommended
- **Cognitive effects:** During attacks — attention, processing speed, and memory are impaired; interictal cognitive dysfunction also reported, especially in chronic migraine
- **Integrated treatment** of both migraine and psychiatric comorbidities (CBT, amitriptyline, venlafaxine, or duloxetine for dual benefit) is more effective than treating either in isolation

---

**Q12.8: What is vestibular migraine?**  
Vestibular migraine is a migraine variant characterized by:
- Recurrent vestibular symptoms (spontaneous vertigo, positional vertigo, visual vertigo)
- Duration: 5 minutes to 72 hours
- Current or prior history of migraine with or without aura
- Migraine features (headache, photophobia, phonophobia) with ≥50% of vestibular episodes
- Not better explained by another vestibular disorder

It is the most common cause of spontaneous recurrent vertigo and is frequently underdiagnosed. Treatment follows standard migraine therapy.

---

**Q12.9: What supplements help with migraine?**

| Supplement | Recommended Dose | Evidence Level | Notes |
|-----------|-----------------|----------------|-------|
| Magnesium glycinate/citrate | 400–600 mg/day | Level B | Particularly effective for menstrual migraine and migraine with aura; deficiency common in migraineurs |
| Riboflavin (Vitamin B2) | 400 mg/day | Level B | Well tolerated; urine turns yellow-orange |
| Coenzyme Q10 | 100–300 mg/day | Level C | Mitochondrial support; slow onset (3 months) |
| Melatonin | 3 mg at night | Level C–B | Especially for migraine disrupting sleep |
| Omega-3 fatty acids | 1–3 g EPA/DHA/day | Emerging | Anti-inflammatory; may reduce frequency |
| Feverfew | 50–150 mg/day | Level C | Modest evidence; GI side effects |
| Butterbur (Petasites) | 75 mg BID | Level A (historical) | Effective but withdrawn from many markets due to hepatotoxicity; use only PA-free extract with caution |

---

**Q12.10: How do I talk to my doctor about migraine?**  
Prepare for your appointment:
- Bring your **headache diary** (or app export) showing frequency, severity, and triggers over 1–3 months
- Note all **current and past medications** tried (doses, efficacy, side effects)
- Describe how migraines **impact your daily life** (work, school, family — MIDAS/HIT-6 scores)
- List **associated symptoms** (aura type, nausea, photophobia, allodynia)
- Describe your **typical attack pattern** (onset, duration, phases)
- Ask specifically:
  - "Is my current treatment optimal?"
  - "Should I be on preventive therapy?"
  - "Am I at risk for medication overuse?"
  - "Should I see a neurologist or headache specialist?"
  - "Are CGRP therapies an option for me?"
