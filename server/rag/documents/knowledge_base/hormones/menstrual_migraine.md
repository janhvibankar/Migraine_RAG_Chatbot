# Menstrual Migraine

## Overview

Menstrual migraine (MM) is a subtype of migraine that is temporally associated with the menstrual cycle. It affects approximately 50–70% of women who have migraine, making it one of the most clinically significant hormone-related headache disorders. Menstrual migraines are typically more severe, longer in duration, and more resistant to standard acute treatments compared to non-menstrual migraines.

---

## Classification and Definitions

### Pure Menstrual Migraine Without Aura (PMM)
- Migraine attacks occur **exclusively** on days −2 to +3 of menstruation (day 1 = first day of bleeding)
- Attacks occur in at least 2 out of 3 consecutive menstrual cycles
- No attacks occur at other times of the cycle
- Prevalence: ~7–14% of women with migraine

### Menstrually Related Migraine Without Aura (MRM)
- Migraine attacks occur on days −2 to +3 of menstruation in at least 2 out of 3 cycles
- Additional attacks may occur at other times of the cycle
- More common than PMM; affects ~35–51% of women with migraine

### Non-Menstrual Migraine
- Attacks have no consistent temporal relationship with the menstrual cycle

> **Classification Source:** ICHD-3 (International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition), Appendix A1.1.1 and A1.1.2

## Pathophysiology

### Estrogen Withdrawal Hypothesis
- The primary trigger for menstrual migraine is the **sharp decline in estrogen (estradiol)** in the late luteal phase (2–3 days before menstruation)
- Estrogen withdrawal sensitizes trigeminal neurons and increases neuroinflammatory susceptibility
- Progesterone withdrawal may play a secondary role

### Prostaglandins
- During menstruation, prostaglandins (especially PGE2 and PGF2α) are released from the uterine endometrium
- Prostaglandins can trigger vasodilation, sensitize pain receptors, and lower the migraine threshold
- This mechanism explains the co-occurrence of dysmenorrhea and menstrual migraine

### Serotonin (5-HT) Fluctuations
- Estrogen modulates serotonin synthesis, reuptake, and receptor sensitivity
- Estrogen withdrawal decreases serotonin availability, increasing susceptibility to cortical spreading depression (CSD) — the neurological event underlying migraine aura

### CGRP (Calcitonin Gene-Related Peptide)
- Estrogen influences CGRP expression in trigeminal ganglia
- Perimenstrual estrogen fluctuations upregulate CGRP release, a key mediator of migraine pain
- Elevated plasma CGRP levels have been documented during menstrual migraine attacks

### Magnesium Deficiency
- Women with menstrual migraine show lower ionized magnesium levels in the luteal phase
- Magnesium depletion increases NMDA receptor activity, lowering the migraine threshold

### Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Ovarian (HPO) Axis
- Dysregulation of the HPO axis may amplify the sensitivity of the trigeminal pain system
- The hypothalamus is involved in premonitory symptoms (yawning, food cravings, mood changes) days before the attack

## Hormonal Context Across the Menstrual Cycle

| Phase | Days | Estrogen | Progesterone | Migraine Risk |
|-------|------|----------|--------------|---------------|
| Menstrual | 1–5 | Low (post-drop) | Low | **Very High** |
| Follicular | 6–13 | Rising | Low | Low–Moderate |
| Ovulatory | 14 | Peak, then drop | Rising | Moderate (mid-cycle) |
| Luteal | 15–28 | Moderate | High | Moderate |
| Late Luteal (Premenstrual) | 25–28 | **Sharply declining** | **Sharply declining** | **High** |

## Clinical Features

### Attack Characteristics
- **Duration:** 24–72 hours (often longer than non-menstrual migraines)
- **Severity:** Typically moderate to severe
- **Pain quality:** Unilateral, pulsating/throbbing
- **Associated symptoms:** Nausea, vomiting, photophobia, phonophobia
- **Disability:** Higher disability scores compared to non-menstrual attacks
- **Treatment resistance:** Lower response rate to triptans; higher relapse rate after initial relief

### Premonitory Symptoms (Prodrome) — Early Warning Signals
Appear 6–48 hours before attack onset:
- Fatigue and yawning
- Neck stiffness or pain
- Mood changes (irritability, depression, euphoria)
- Food cravings (especially chocolate, carbohydrates)
- Cognitive changes ("brain fog")
- Fluid retention / bloating
- Increased sensitivity to light or sound
- Polyuria or fluid retention

### Aura
- Menstrual migraines are **predominantly without aura**
- If aura is present, it is not hormonally driven in the same way — PMM and MRM are specifically defined as "without aura"
- Women who experience aura should avoid combined oral contraceptives (COCs) due to increased stroke risk

---

## Triggers Specific to Menstrual Migraine

- Estrogen withdrawal (primary)
- Sleep disruption (common premenstrually)
- Stress and mood fluctuations (PMS co-occurrence)
- Dietary triggers (increased premenstrual cravings — chocolate, alcohol, salty foods)
- Dehydration
- Skipped meals or fasting
- Prostaglandin surge during menstruation

---

## Diagnosis

### Diagnostic Criteria (ICHD-3 Appendix)
**Pure Menstrual Migraine Without Aura:**
A. Attacks in a menstruating woman fulfilling criteria for migraine without aura
B. Occurring exclusively on days −2 to +3 of menstruation in at least 2 of 3 cycles
C. No attacks at any other time of the cycle

**Menstrually Related Migraine Without Aura:**
A. Attacks in a menstruating woman fulfilling criteria for migraine without aura
B. Occurring on days −2 to +3 of menstruation in at least 2 of 3 cycles
C. Additional attacks may occur outside this window

### Tools for Diagnosis
- **Headache diary:** 3 months minimum; record attack onset, severity, duration, menstrual day
- **Prospective tracking** is preferred over retrospective recall
- Visual analog scales (VAS) for pain severity
- MIDAS or HIT-6 for disability assessment

## Treatment

### Acute Treatment

#### Triptans
- First-line acute treatment for moderate-to-severe menstrual migraine
- **Sumatriptan:** 50–100 mg oral, or 6 mg subcutaneous
- **Rizatriptan:** 10 mg oral
- **Naratriptan:** 2.5 mg oral (slower onset, longer half-life — useful for prolonged attacks)
- **Zolmitriptan:** 2.5–5 mg oral or nasal spray
- Note: Menstrual migraines often require re-dosing due to higher relapse rates

#### NSAIDs
- **Naproxen sodium:** 550 mg (start 2 days before expected onset)
- **Ibuprofen:** 400–600 mg
- Useful both as acute and mini-prophylactic treatment
- Address prostaglandin-mediated component

#### CGRP Receptor Antagonists (Gepants)
- **Ubrogepant** (Ubrelvy): 50–100 mg — acute treatment
- **Rimegepant** (Nurtec ODT): 75 mg — also used for prevention
- Particularly useful when triptans are contraindicated (e.g., cardiovascular disease)

#### Ergotamine / Dihydroergotamine (DHE)
- DHE nasal spray or injectable for severe, refractory attacks
- Less commonly used due to side effects

#### Antiemetics
- Metoclopramide, ondansetron, prochlorperazine for nausea/vomiting

---

### Preventive / Prophylactic Treatment

#### Short-Term (Perimenstrual) Prophylaxis
Initiated 2–3 days before expected menstruation, continued through first 3–5 days of menses:

| Drug | Dose | Notes |
|------|------|-------|
| Naproxen sodium | 550 mg twice daily | Anti-prostaglandin; first-line |
| Frovatriptan | 2.5 mg twice daily × 6 days | Longest half-life triptan; strong evidence |
| Naratriptan | 1 mg twice daily × 5 days | Well tolerated |
| Zolmitriptan | 2.5 mg twice–three times daily | Effective; shorter half-life |
| Estradiol gel/patch | 1.5 mg/100 mcg patch applied days −3 to +3 | Stabilizes estrogen drop |
| Magnesium glycinate | 400–600 mg daily (cycle-long) | Reduces frequency; safe |

#### Long-Term Preventive Treatment
Indicated when menstrual migraines occur ≥4 days/month or cause significant disability:

- **Beta-blockers:** Propranolol (40–240 mg/day), Metoprolol
- **Tricyclic antidepressants:** Amitriptyline (10–75 mg at bedtime)
- **Antiepileptics:** Topiramate (25–100 mg/day), Valproate (500–1500 mg/day)
- **CGRP monoclonal antibodies:**
  - Erenumab (Aimovig): 70–140 mg/month SC
  - Fremanezumab (Ajovy): 225 mg/month or 675 mg quarterly SC
  - Galcanezumab (Emgality): 120 mg/month SC
  - Eptinezumab (Vyepti): 100–300 mg IV quarterly
- **Rimegepant (Nurtec):** 75 mg every other day — dual acute and preventive

#### Hormonal Approaches
- **Continuous combined oral contraceptives (COC):** Eliminate menstrual cycle; contraindicated with aura
- **Progestogen-only contraceptives:** Levonorgestrel IUS (Mirena), desogestrel pill
- **GnRH analogues:** Suppress ovulation; used in refractory cases; add-back therapy needed
- **Estrogen add-back (HRT-like):** Transdermal estradiol patch during the withdrawal phase

## Hormonal Contraception Considerations

| Contraceptive | Migraine Without Aura | Migraine With Aura |
|---|---|---|
| Combined oral contraceptive (COC) | May worsen or improve; monitor | **Contraindicated** (stroke risk) |
| Progestogen-only pill | Generally safe | Safe |
| Levonorgestrel IUS (Mirena) | Generally safe | Safe |
| Copper IUD | No hormonal effect | Safe |
| Patch/ring (combined) | Caution | Contraindicated |
| Depo-Provera injection | May help or worsen | Safe |

## Comorbidities

- **Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS) / PMDD:** High co-occurrence; both driven by hormonal fluctuation
- **Dysmenorrhea:** Prostaglandin link
- **Endometriosis:** Higher migraine prevalence
- **Depression and anxiety:** Bidirectional relationship
- **Fibromyalgia:** Shared central sensitization pathways
- **Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS)**

## Early Warning Prediction Signals

Key data points for a RAG-based early warning system:

### Hormonal Signals
- Menstrual cycle day (day −2 to +3 = high-risk window)
- Self-reported cycle phase
- Estimated ovulation day (if tracked)
- Hormonal contraceptive use and type

### Prodromal Symptom Signals (24–48 hours before attack)
- Fatigue score increase
- Neck stiffness/pain
- Mood changes (irritability, low mood)
- Yawning frequency
- Food cravings
- Photophobia onset
- Cognitive sluggishness

### Physiological Biomarkers (if available)
- Heart rate variability (HRV) — decreases premenstrually
- Sleep quality and duration
- Skin temperature changes
- Blood pressure fluctuations

### Behavioral/Environmental Inputs
- Dietary log (alcohol, chocolate, skipped meals)
- Hydration level
- Sleep disruption
- Stress rating
- Physical activity level
- Weather/barometric pressure change

## Prognosis and Natural History

- Menstrual migraines often **worsen in the perimenopause** due to erratic estrogen fluctuations
- Post-menopause, many women experience **improvement** (especially if natural menopause)
- Surgical menopause (oophorectomy) can worsen migraine due to abrupt estrogen loss
- Pregnancy typically causes **significant improvement** in the 2nd and 3rd trimesters (rising stable estrogen)

---

## Key Facts for RAG Retrieval

- Menstrual migraine window: Day −2 to +3 of menstruation
- Primary trigger: Estrogen withdrawal (not low estrogen per se)
- Most common type: Menstrually Related Migraine (MRM) > Pure Menstrual Migraine (PMM)
- Best short-term prevention: Frovatriptan or Naproxen sodium perimenstrually
- Best long-term prevention: CGRP monoclonal antibodies, topiramate, propranolol
- Aura + hormonal contraceptive: Avoid COC (stroke risk)
- Magnesium supplementation reduces frequency in menstrual migraine
- CGRP is a key biomarker elevated during attacks
- Prodrome begins 6–48 hours before attack — optimal early warning window
- Disability is higher for menstrual migraines than non-menstrual migraines
