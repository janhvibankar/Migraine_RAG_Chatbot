# Pregnancy and Migraine

## Overview

Migraine affects approximately 18–25% of women of reproductive age, making it one of the most commonly encountered neurological conditions during pregnancy. Pregnancy exerts complex, phase-dependent effects on migraine — often leading to improvement in the second and third trimesters, but posing significant diagnostic and treatment challenges throughout. Understanding the interaction between migraine and pregnancy is critical for clinical management, medication safety, and postpartum risk assessment.

---

## Epidemiology

- Migraine prevalence in women of reproductive age: 18–25%
- Approximately 1 in 5 pregnant women has a history of migraine
- Up to **70–80% of migraineurs** experience improvement during pregnancy, primarily in the 2nd and 3rd trimesters
- Approximately **4–8%** experience worsening of migraine during pregnancy
- First-trimester worsening is common due to hormonal volatility and nausea/vomiting
- Postpartum period: **Significant relapse risk** within the first 6 weeks after delivery

---

## Hormonal Mechanisms in Pregnancy

### Estrogen Stabilization — The Improvement Mechanism
- During the 2nd and 3rd trimesters, estrogen levels rise **steeply and remain continuously elevated**
- This eliminates the cyclical estrogen withdrawal that triggers menstrual migraine
- Stable high estrogen raises the migraine threshold by modulating serotonin, CGRP, and trigeminal sensitivity

### First Trimester Volatility
- In the first trimester, estrogen levels are **rising rapidly but irregularly**
- Morning sickness, fatigue, sleep disruption, and dehydration are common confounders and migraine triggers
- Many women experience **no improvement** or **worsening** during the first trimester

### Postpartum Estrogen Withdrawal
- After delivery, estrogen levels **drop sharply** — mimicking the pattern of menstrual migraine triggers
- This is the primary driver of postpartum migraine relapse
- Breastfeeding may partially delay this drop by suppressing ovulation (prolactin-mediated)

### Progesterone Role
- Progesterone also rises throughout pregnancy
- Its role in migraine is less clear; may have mild inhibitory effect on trigeminal activation

## Migraine Course by Trimester

| Period | Estrogen Status | Migraine Pattern | Clinical Notes |
|--------|----------------|-----------------|----------------|
| Pre-conception | Cyclical fluctuation | Menstrual pattern | Optimize treatment before conception |
| 1st Trimester (wks 1–12) | Rising, volatile | Often worsens or unchanged | Nausea/vomiting confounds; most medication restrictions |
| 2nd Trimester (wks 13–26) | High, stable | **Significant improvement in ~70%** | Non-pharm strategies emphasized |
| 3rd Trimester (wks 27–40) | Very high, stable | Continued improvement | New headache = rule out preeclampsia |
| Postpartum (0–6 wks) | **Sharp drop** | **High relapse risk** | Sleep deprivation amplifies risk |
| Breastfeeding | Suppressed cycling | Variable | Prolactin may stabilize; menstrual return = risk |

## Diagnostic Considerations During Pregnancy

### Key Challenge: Secondary Headache Exclusion
All new or changed headaches during pregnancy require thorough evaluation to exclude dangerous secondary causes.

### Red Flags ("Thunderclap" or Dangerous Headache Signs)
- Sudden severe "worst headache of life"
- New headache after 20 weeks gestation → **rule out preeclampsia**
- Headache with hypertension (≥140/90 mmHg)
- Headache with visual disturbances, edema, proteinuria → **preeclampsia/eclampsia**
- Headache with fever → meningitis, encephalitis
- Headache with focal neurological deficits → stroke, cerebral venous thrombosis (CVT)
- Postpartum headache with neurological signs → CVT, postdural puncture headache, PRES

### Differential Diagnosis for Headache in Pregnancy

| Condition | Key Features |
|---|---|
| Migraine | History of migraine; typical pattern; no BP elevation |
| Tension-type headache | Bilateral, pressing, no nausea |
| Preeclampsia | ≥20 weeks; hypertension; proteinuria; visual symptoms |
| Eclampsia | Above + seizures |
| Cerebral Venous Thrombosis (CVT) | Progressive; focal deficits; hypercoagulable state |
| Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension | Obesity; papilledema; pulsatile tinnitus |
| Subarachnoid Hemorrhage | Sudden; severe; neck stiffness |
| Postdural Puncture Headache | Post-epidural; positional; bilateral |
| PRES (Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome) | Hypertension; visual changes; seizures |

### Imaging During Pregnancy
- **MRI without gadolinium:** Safe in pregnancy; preferred over CT
- **CT with lead shielding:** Acceptable when clinically necessary (radiation to fetus is minimal)
- **MRI with gadolinium:** Avoid in 1st trimester; use only if benefits clearly outweigh risks
- **Lumbar puncture:** Safe if indicated for meningitis/SAH exclusion

## Medication Safety in Pregnancy

### Acute Treatment

#### Acetaminophen (Paracetamol)
- **First-line** acute treatment in pregnancy
- Generally considered safe throughout pregnancy
- Dose: 500–1000 mg; do not exceed 3g/day
- Recent studies suggest possible subtle neurodevelopmental associations with prolonged use — use the minimum effective dose for shortest time needed

#### NSAIDs (Ibuprofen, Naproxen, Aspirin)
- **1st Trimester:** Use with caution; possible increased risk of miscarriage
- **2nd Trimester:** Relatively safer; short-term use acceptable
- **3rd Trimester (after 20 weeks):** **Avoid** — risk of premature ductus arteriosus closure and oligohydramnios
- Low-dose aspirin (75–150 mg/day) is used for preeclampsia prevention — different risk profile

#### Triptans
- Not formally approved for use in pregnancy
- **Overall evidence suggests triptans do not significantly increase teratogenic risk**
- Sumatriptan has the most safety data (largest pregnancy registry); no consistent pattern of birth defects
- May be used when benefit outweighs risk, especially for severe attacks refractory to acetaminophen
- **Avoid in first trimester** if possible; use lowest effective dose
- Theoretical concern: vasoconstriction could affect uterine blood flow

#### Antiemetics
- **Ondansetron (Zofran):** Widely used; some data suggest possible cardiac septum defects with 1st trimester use — use judiciously
- **Metoclopramide:** Considered relatively safe; useful for nausea and as acute migraine adjunct
- **Prochlorperazine:** Can be used; monitor for extrapyramidal effects
- **Promethazine:** Generally acceptable; sedating

#### Opioids
- **Avoid routine use** for migraine in pregnancy
- Butorphanol and codeine sometimes used in very refractory cases under specialist guidance
- Risk of neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome with chronic use
- Do not use in 3rd trimester near term (respiratory depression in neonate)

#### Ergotamines / DHE
- **Absolutely contraindicated** in pregnancy
- Potent uterotonics — can cause uterine contractions, spontaneous abortion, fetal ischemia

#### CGRP Pathway Treatments (Gepants, Monoclonal Antibodies)
- **Insufficient safety data in pregnancy**
- All CGRP monoclonal antibodies (erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab, eptinezumab) should be **discontinued before conception** or as soon as pregnancy is confirmed
- Gepants (ubrogepant, rimegepant): **Avoid in pregnancy** — no human safety data
- CGRP plays a role in placental vascular regulation; theoretical concern for placental insufficiency

---

### Preventive Treatment in Pregnancy

#### Magnesium
- **Safest preventive option in pregnancy**
- Magnesium oxide or glycinate: 400–600 mg/day
- Used for both migraine prevention and preeclampsia prevention (IV magnesium sulfate for eclampsia)
- No known teratogenicity at supplemental doses

#### Beta-Blockers
- **Propranolol** and **Metoprolol** are most commonly used
- Generally considered acceptable in pregnancy, especially 2nd and 3rd trimester
- Risks: neonatal bradycardia, hypoglycemia, intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), possible preterm birth
- Taper before delivery if possible
- Preferred option when preventive treatment is clearly necessary

#### Tricyclic Antidepressants
- **Amitriptyline:** Low doses (10–25 mg) used for chronic pain and migraine prevention
- Possible association with neonatal withdrawal; avoid near term
- 3rd trimester use: neonatal symptoms (jitteriness, irritability)
- Modest risk; use when benefit justifies

#### Topiramate
- **Avoid in pregnancy**
- Associated with oral clefts and small for gestational age (SGA) infants
- FDA Pregnancy Category D (positive evidence of fetal risk)
- Should be discontinued before conception when possible

#### Valproate / Valproic Acid
- **Contraindicated in pregnancy**
- Highest teratogenicity among antiepileptic drugs: neural tube defects, cardiac defects, cognitive impairment
- Absolute contraindication in women of childbearing potential without reliable contraception

#### Amitriptyline vs SNRIs
- SSRIs/SNRIs (e.g., venlafaxine) sometimes used for comorbid depression + migraine
- Neonatal adaptation syndrome possible with third-trimester SSRI exposure (transient)
- Risk-benefit discussion with psychiatry/neurology advised

## Non-Pharmacological Management in Pregnancy

Preferred approach, especially in 1st trimester:

- **Sleep hygiene:** Maintain consistent sleep schedule; naps if needed
- **Hydration:** 8–10 glasses of water/day; avoid dehydration
- **Dietary trigger avoidance:** Caffeine moderation, avoid alcohol, regular meals (no skipping)
- **Magnesium-rich diet:** Leafy greens, nuts, seeds, legumes
- **Stress reduction:** Mindfulness, prenatal yoga, breathing exercises
- **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT):** Proven effective for chronic migraine
- **Biofeedback:** Non-invasive; evidence-based for migraine prevention
- **Acupuncture:** Some evidence for headache reduction; considered safe in pregnancy
- **Cold/warm compresses:** Topical relief during attacks
- **Rest in dark, quiet room:** Reduces photophobia/phonophobia
- **Caffeine:** Small amount (e.g., one cup of coffee) can abort mild attacks; total intake <200 mg/day in pregnancy

## Postpartum Migraine

### Pathophysiology of Postpartum Migraine
- Estrogen and progesterone drop precipitously after delivery of the placenta
- This mirrors the premenstrual estrogen withdrawal pattern
- Migraine relapse typically occurs within **3–6 days postpartum** (peak risk: days 3–5)
- Sleep deprivation, dehydration, stress, and breastfeeding challenges amplify risk

### Risk Factors for Postpartum Migraine
- History of menstrual migraine (strongest predictor)
- History of migraine with aura
- Multiparity
- Non-breastfeeding (earlier return of menstrual cycle)
- Epidural/spinal anesthesia (differentiate from post-dural puncture headache)
- Pre-existing depression/anxiety

### Postpartum Migraine vs. Post-Dural Puncture Headache (PDPH)
| Feature | Postpartum Migraine | PDPH |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | Days 3–5 | 24–48 hours post-procedure |
| Position | Non-positional | **Positional** (worse upright, better supine) |
| Location | Unilateral/bifrontal; throbbing | Bilateral; frontal/occipital |
| Associated symptoms | Nausea, photo/phonophobia | Neck stiffness, tinnitus |
| Treatment | Triptans, NSAIDs | Caffeine, hydration, epidural blood patch |

### Postpartum Red Flags — Dangerous Headache
- Severe "thunderclap" headache — rule out **cerebral venous thrombosis (CVT)**
- Headache with fever → meningitis
- Headache with hypertension → postpartum preeclampsia (can occur up to 6 weeks postpartum)
- Headache with visual loss → CVT, PRES
- New neurological deficits → stroke

### Treatment Options Postpartum

#### Breastfeeding Compatibility

| Drug | Breastfeeding Safety |
|---|---|
| Acetaminophen | ✅ Safe |
| Ibuprofen | ✅ Safe (preferred NSAID) |
| Naproxen | ⚠️ Caution (longer half-life) |
| Sumatriptan | ✅ Acceptable (low milk transfer) |
| Rizatriptan | ✅ Acceptable |
| Ergotamine | ❌ Contraindicated |
| Codeine | ❌ Avoid (neonatal CNS depression risk) |
| Metoclopramide | ✅ Acceptable; may increase milk supply |

| CGRP mAbs | ⚠️ Unknown; caution advised |

## Pre-Conception Planning

### Recommendations Before Pregnancy
- Discontinue teratogenic preventives: **Valproate, Topiramate** — switch to safer alternatives
- Taper/discontinue CGRP monoclonal antibodies at least **5 half-lives before conception** (~5–6 months for monthly mAbs)
- Establish non-pharmacological strategies
- Optimize magnesium supplementation (400–600 mg/day)
- Initiate folic acid supplementation (400–800 mcg/day minimum; 5 mg/day if on antiepileptics)
- Discuss acute treatment plan: acetaminophen ± triptan (sumatriptan preferred)
- Review all preventive options with neurologist

## Migraine Aura and Pregnancy-Specific Risks

### New Onset Aura in Pregnancy
- New-onset aura during pregnancy warrants **urgent neurological evaluation**
- Differential includes: stroke, CVT, TIA, preeclampsia with neurological features
- MRI (without gadolinium) should be performed

### Stroke Risk
- Migraine with aura is an **independent risk factor for ischemic stroke**
- Pregnancy itself increases stroke risk (hypercoagulable state, hemodynamic changes)
- Combined risk in pregnant women with migraine with aura: significantly elevated
- Avoid COC contraception in women with migraine with aura

### Cerebral Venous Thrombosis (CVT) in Pregnancy/Postpartum
- Occurs in approximately 12/100,000 deliveries
- Risk period: late pregnancy and **first 6 weeks postpartum**
- Symptoms: progressive headache, seizures, focal deficits
- Diagnosis: MRV (MR venography)
- Treatment: anticoagulation (LMWH)

## Early Warning Prediction Signals in Pregnancy Context

For RAG-based migraine early warning system — pregnancy-specific signals:

### Gestational Phase Signals
- Current trimester (T1 = higher risk; T2/T3 = usually lower)
- Days postpartum (days 3–6 = high risk window)
- Breastfeeding status
- Resumption of menstrual cycle postpartum

### Prodromal Symptoms
- Fatigue (account for pregnancy baseline fatigue)
- Neck pain/stiffness
- Mood changes, irritability
- Yawning, food cravings
- Light/sound sensitivity onset
- Cognitive changes ("pregnancy brain" vs. migraine prodrome)

### Clinical Monitoring Flags
- Blood pressure elevation → distinguish migraine from preeclampsia
- Proteinuria if dipstick available
- Visual symptoms → aura vs. preeclampsia
- Fever → infection
- Onset timing relative to delivery

### Lifestyle/Behavioral Inputs
- Sleep quality and duration (postpartum sleep deprivation is a major trigger)
- Hydration
- Meal timing (fasting trigger)
- Stress score
- Caffeine intake changes
- Breastfeeding frequency (hormonal modulation)

## Key Facts for RAG Retrieval

- 70–80% of migraineurs improve in 2nd and 3rd trimesters
- Primary mechanism: sustained elevated estrogen eliminates withdrawal trigger
- First trimester: often worsens; most restrictive for medications
- Safest acute treatment: acetaminophen (paracetamol) ± sumatriptan if needed
- Safest preventive: magnesium 400–600 mg/day
- Absolutely contraindicated: ergotamines, valproate (any trimester)
- CGRP monoclonal antibodies: discontinue before or at conception
- Postpartum peak migraine risk: days 3–5 after delivery
- New headache after 20 weeks: always exclude preeclampsia
- Migraine with aura + pregnancy: elevated stroke risk; avoid COC
- Postpartum headache + positional symptoms: think post-dural puncture, not migraine
- CVT: rare but dangerous; occurs in postpartum period; treat with anticoagulation
- Breastfeeding-safe options: acetaminophen, ibuprofen, sumatriptan, propranolol
- Folic acid supplementation critical pre-conception and in T1
