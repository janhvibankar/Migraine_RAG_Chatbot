# Prescription Medications for Migraine

> **Disclaimer:** This document is intended for use in a clinical decision support RAG system. It is an educational reference only and does not substitute for individualized medical advice from a licensed healthcare professional. Prescription medications require physician evaluation and authorization.

---

## 1. Overview

Prescription medications for migraine are used in two contexts:
1. **Acute/Abortive Treatment** — taken at onset to stop or shorten an attack.
2. **Preventive/Prophylactic Treatment** — taken daily to reduce attack frequency, severity, and duration.

The choice of therapy depends on attack frequency, severity, disability, comorbidities, and patient preference.

---

## 2. Acute/Abortive Prescription Medications

### 2.1 Triptans (5-HT1B/1D Agonists)

Triptans are the gold-standard prescription treatment for moderate-to-severe migraine. They work by binding to serotonin (5-HT1B/1D) receptors, causing cerebral vasoconstriction and inhibiting release of neuropeptides (CGRP, substance P) from trigeminal nerve terminals.

**Common Mechanism:** Activation of 5-HT1B receptors on cranial blood vessels → vasoconstriction; activation of 5-HT1D receptors on trigeminal neurons → inhibition of neurogenic inflammation.

#### Available Triptans (Comparative Overview)

| Drug | Dose | Route | Onset | Half-life | Notes |
|------|------|-------|-------|-----------|-------|
| Sumatriptan | 25–100 mg oral; 6 mg SC; 5–20 mg nasal | Oral, SC, nasal, transdermal | 30–60 min (oral) | 2 hours | First triptan; SC form fastest onset; gold-standard comparator |
| Rizatriptan | 5–10 mg | Oral, ODT | 30–45 min | 2–3 hours | ODT dissolves without water; useful during nausea; higher efficacy at 10 mg |
| Zolmitriptan | 2.5–5 mg | Oral, nasal | 30–60 min | 3 hours | Nasal spray useful in severe nausea/vomiting; also OTC in some countries |
| Eletriptan | 20–80 mg | Oral | 30–60 min | 4 hours | Higher lipophilicity; 80 mg most effective but higher side effects |
| Almotriptan | 12.5 mg | Oral | 30–60 min | 3–4 hours | Good tolerability profile |
| Frovatriptan | 2.5 mg | Oral | 2–4 hours | 26 hours | Long half-life; useful for menstrual migraine; mini-prevention |
| Naratriptan | 1–2.5 mg | Oral | 1–3 hours | 6 hours | Slower onset; fewer side effects; useful for prolonged attacks |

**Efficacy:**
- Sumatriptan SC 6 mg: ~70–80% 2-hour pain relief; fastest onset among triptans.
- Oral triptans: ~50–70% 2-hour pain relief (moderate-to-severe migraine).
- Earlier treatment (during mild pain phase) generally increases response rates.

**Side Effects (Class):**
- Triptan sensations: tingling, flushing, chest tightness, heaviness, paresthesia (mechanism: 5-HT1B–mediated, generally not cardiac in nature).
- Dizziness, somnolence, nausea.
- Rare: serotonin syndrome (particularly with MAOIs, SSRIs, SNRIs).
- Coronary vasospasm (theoretical/rare; avoid in CAD).

**Contraindications (All Triptans):**
- Coronary artery disease (CAD), angina, prior MI
- Stroke or TIA history
- Peripheral vascular disease
- Uncontrolled hypertension
- Hemiplegic migraine (traditional contraindication; limited evidence, but caution advised)
- Basilar migraine (traditional; being revisited)
- Concurrent ergotamine or ergot derivative use (within 24 hours)
- Concurrent MAOI use (particularly rizatriptan)
- Pregnancy: use with caution; avoid unless benefit outweighs risk (limited safety data; sumatriptan has most data)
- Severe hepatic/renal impairment (varies by specific drug)

**MOH Risk:** HIGH — triptans limited to ≤10 days/month.

### 2.2 CGRP Receptor Antagonists (Gepants) — Acute Use

Calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) plays a central role in migraine pathophysiology. Gepants block CGRP receptors without causing vasoconstriction, offering an alternative for patients with cardiovascular contraindications to triptans.

| Drug | Dose | Route | Onset | Notes |
|------|------|-------|-------|-------|
| Ubrogepant | 50–100 mg | Oral | ~1.5 hours | FDA-approved 2019; no vasoconstriction |
| Rimegepant | 75 mg | Oral/ODT | ~1.5 hours | FDA-approved 2020; also has preventive indication (every other day dosing) |
| Atogepant | 10–60 mg | Oral | — | Primarily used for prevention; also studied for acute use |

**Mechanism:** Competitive antagonism at CGRP receptor → inhibits trigeminal pathway sensitization and neurogenic inflammation without vasoconstriction.

**Advantages over Triptans:**
- No cardiovascular contraindications (no vasoconstriction).
- May be used in hemiplegic or basilar-type migraine.
- Lower MOH risk than triptans (gepants do not appear to cause MOH at typical doses).

**Side Effects:** Nausea, somnolence, dry mouth; generally well tolerated.

**Contraindications:**
- Strong CYP3A4 inhibitors/inducers (drug interactions).
- Severe hepatic impairment.
- Pregnancy: insufficient data; avoid.

### 2.3 Lasmiditan (5-HT1F Agonist — Ditans)

| Attribute | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| Drug | Lasmiditan |
| Dose | 50–200 mg oral |
| Route | Oral |
| FDA Approval | 2019 |
| Mechanism | Selective 5-HT1F agonist → inhibits trigeminal neuronal activity without vasoconstriction |

**Advantages:** No vasoconstrictive effects; usable in cardiovascular disease.

**Limitations:**
- CNS side effects: dizziness, somnolence, paresthesia (prominent).
- Driving impairment: patients advised not to drive for ≥8 hours after dose.
- Schedule V controlled substance (USA).
- MOH risk may be present.

---

### 2.4 Ergot Alkaloids

| Drug | Dose | Route | Notes |
|------|------|-------|-------|
| Ergotamine tartrate | 1–2 mg | Oral, sublingual | Less specific than triptans; significant side effect profile |
| Dihydroergotamine (DHE) | 1 mg SC/IM/IV; 2 mg nasal | SC, IM, IV, intranasal | More tolerable than ergotamine; used in refractory migraine; IV DHE protocol used in status migrainosus |

**Mechanism:** Partial agonist at serotonin (5-HT1), dopamine, and adrenergic receptors; causes vasoconstriction.

**Side Effects:** Nausea, vomiting (requires antiemetic co-administration), peripheral vasoconstriction, ergotism (with overuse).

**Contraindications:**
- CAD, peripheral vascular disease, stroke
- Pregnancy (oxytocic effects — **absolute contraindication**)
- Uncontrolled hypertension
- Concurrent triptan use (within 24 hours)
- Concurrent potent CYP3A4 inhibitors (macrolides, azole antifungals, HIV protease inhibitors)

**MOH Risk:** HIGH — limit to ≤10 days/month.

---

### 2.5 Antiemetics (Adjunct Acute Treatment)

Used to treat migraine-associated nausea/vomiting and improve absorption of oral medications.

| Drug | Dose | Notes |
|------|------|-------|
| Metoclopramide | 10–20 mg oral/IV | Also analgesic adjuvant; IV form for ED/clinic use; dopamine antagonist; risk of EPS with repeated doses |
| Prochlorperazine | 5–10 mg oral/IM/IV | Dopamine antagonist; effective for pain relief in acute migraine (not just antiemetic); risk of tardive dyskinesia with long-term use |
| Domperidone | 10 mg oral | Peripheral dopamine antagonist; less CNS side effects than metoclopramide (doesn't cross BBB well) |
| Ondansetron | 4–8 mg oral/IV | 5-HT3 antagonist; good antiemetic; limited analgesic effect in migraine; useful in severe nausea |
| Chlorpromazine | 12.5–25 mg IV/IM | Phenothiazine; used in refractory/ED migraine; sedating |

---

### 2.6 Opioids and Barbiturates (Not Recommended)

- **Not recommended** as first-line or routine therapy per current guidelines (AHS, AAN, IHS).
- Associated with highest MOH risk.
- Risk of dependency, tolerance, and hyperalgesia.
- May be considered only in specific refractory cases under close supervision.
- **Butalbital combinations** (e.g., Fioricet) specifically highlighted in guidelines as medications to avoid or minimize due to high MOH risk.

## 3. Preventive (Prophylactic) Prescription Medications

### 3.1 Indications for Preventive Therapy

Consider preventive treatment when:
- Migraine occurs ≥4 headache days/month with significant disability.
- Acute medications are contraindicated, overused, or ineffective.
- Patient prefers to reduce attack frequency.
- Hemiplegic migraine, prolonged aura, migrainous infarction risk.
- Very severe or prolonged individual attacks.

### 3.2 Beta-Blockers

| Drug | Dose | Evidence Level |
|------|------|---------------|
| Propranolol | 40–240 mg/day | Level A (highest) |
| Metoprolol | 50–200 mg/day | Level A |
| Timolol | 10–30 mg/day | Level A (FDA approved for migraine prevention) |
| Atenolol | 50–200 mg/day | Level B |
| Nadolol | 20–160 mg/day | Level B |

**Mechanism:** Likely involves inhibition of central β-adrenergic pathways and serotonergic modulation; reduces cortical hyperexcitability.

**Side Effects:** Fatigue, exercise intolerance, bradycardia, hypotension, depression, sexual dysfunction, sleep disturbances, bronchospasm.

**Contraindications:**
- Asthma / COPD (relative — cardioselective beta-blockers have less risk)
- Heart block (2nd/3rd degree)
- Decompensated heart failure
- Severe peripheral vascular disease
- Diabetes (masks hypoglycemia symptoms — relative)
- Depression (relative)
- Raynaud's phenomenon (relative)

---

### 3.3 Antiepileptic Drugs (AEDs)

| Drug | Dose | Evidence | Notes |
|------|------|----------|-------|
| Topiramate | 25–200 mg/day | Level A | Most studied; also FDA approved; weight loss side effect; cognitive side effects ("dopamax"); teratogenic |
| Valproate / Sodium Valproate | 500–1500 mg/day | Level A | FDA approved; teratogenic (**avoid in women of childbearing potential if possible**); weight gain, hepatotoxicity, PCOS risk |
| Gabapentin | 900–2400 mg/day | Level C/B | Some evidence; more studied for other conditions |
| Lamotrigine | 50–300 mg/day | Limited for typical migraine; may help migraine with aura specifically |

**Topiramate Special Notes:**
- Cognitive side effects (word-finding difficulty, memory) in up to 30%.
- Paresthesia (common, usually benign).
- Glaucoma (acute angle-closure, rare — discontinue immediately if vision changes).
- Kidney stones (carbonic anhydrase inhibition).
- Metabolic acidosis.
- Teratogenic — requires contraception in women of childbearing potential.

**Valproate Special Notes:**
- **Absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy** (neural tube defects, cognitive effects on fetus).
- Requires monitoring of liver function and blood counts.
- Polycystic ovary syndrome risk with long-term use.

---

### 3.4 Antidepressants

| Drug | Class | Dose | Evidence | Notes |
|------|-------|------|----------|-------|
| Amitriptyline | TCA | 10–75 mg/day at night | Level B | Most evidence among antidepressants; anticholinergic side effects; sedating (take at night); beneficial if comorbid insomnia or depression |
| Nortriptyline | TCA | 10–75 mg/day at night | Level C/B | Better tolerated than amitriptyline |
| Venlafaxine | SNRI | 37.5–150 mg/day | Level B | Useful in comorbid anxiety/depression |
| Duloxetine | SNRI | 30–60 mg/day | Limited evidence in migraine specifically; more data in chronic pain |

**TCAs Side Effects:** Sedation, dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, weight gain, cardiac conduction effects (QTc prolongation in overdose), orthostatic hypotension.

**Contraindications (TCAs):**
- Recent MI, arrhythmia, heart block
- MAOIs (serotonin syndrome risk)
- Glaucoma (narrow angle)
- Urinary retention (BPH)
- Pregnancy: limited safety data; use with caution.

### 3.5 CGRP Pathway Preventive Therapies (Monoclonal Antibodies)

Revolutionary class approved specifically for migraine prevention.

| Drug | Target | Dose/Frequency | Route | FDA Approval |
|------|--------|---------------|-------|-------------|
| Erenumab (Aimovig) | CGRP Receptor | 70–140 mg/month | SC | 2018 |
| Fremanezumab (Ajovy) | CGRP Ligand | 225 mg/month OR 675 mg/quarter | SC | 2018 |
| Galcanezumab (Emgality) | CGRP Ligand | 120 mg/month (240 mg loading dose) | SC | 2018 |
| Eptinezumab (Vyepti) | CGRP Ligand | 100–300 mg/quarter | IV infusion | 2020 |

**Mechanism:** Monoclonal antibodies targeting either the CGRP ligand or its receptor, blocking CGRP-mediated trigeminal sensitization.

**Efficacy:**
- Reduce monthly migraine days by approximately 50% in ~50% of patients.
- Generally effective within 1–3 months.
- Responders often see benefit within first month.

**Advantages:**
- Once-monthly or quarterly dosing.
- Excellent tolerability (no significant CNS side effects).
- No MOH risk.
- No hepatotoxicity or teratogenicity concerns (though not recommended in pregnancy due to lack of data).

**Side Effects:**
- Injection site reactions (SC forms).
- Constipation (erenumab — receptor blockade affects CGRP-mediated GI motility).
- Nasopharyngitis.

**Contraindications:**
- Hypersensitivity to the specific biologic.
- Pregnancy and breastfeeding: insufficient safety data; avoid unless benefits clearly outweigh risks.
- Caution in ischemic vascular disease (CGRP has vasodilatory protective effects; theoretical concern, not yet demonstrated clinically).

### 3.6 OnabotulinumtoxinA (Botox)

| Attribute | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| Indication | Chronic migraine only (≥15 headache days/month) |
| Dose | 155–195 units |
| Administration | 31–39 injection sites across head/neck muscles |
| Frequency | Every 12 weeks |
| FDA Approval | 2010 (chronic migraine) |

**Mechanism:** Inhibits presynaptic release of acetylcholine and neuropeptides (CGRP, substance P) from peripheral sensory neurons → reduces trigeminal sensitization.

**Efficacy:** Reduces chronic migraine days by ~8–9 days/month vs ~6 days with placebo (PREEMPT trials).

**Side Effects:** Neck pain, injection site pain, ptosis (eyelid drooping, temporary), muscle weakness in injected areas.

**Contraindications:**
- Episodic migraine (not indicated).
- Neuromuscular junction disorders (myasthenia gravis, Eaton-Lambert syndrome).
- Hypersensitivity to botulinum toxin.
- Active infection at injection sites.

## 4. Menstrual Migraine — Specific Treatments

Migraine associated with menstruation (perimenstrual period) may require specific strategies:

- **Short-term prevention (mini-prophylaxis):**
  - Frovatriptan 2.5 mg BID for 3–6 days around menstruation.
  - Naproxen sodium 550 mg BID around menstruation.
  - Ergotamine (limited use).
  - Transdermal estrogen supplementation (with gynecology input).

- **Continuous hormonal contraception** (to eliminate hormone fluctuation as a trigger) — requires gynecology/endocrinology co-management.

## 5. Status Migrainosus (Prolonged Attack >72 hours)

| Treatment Option | Route | Notes |
|-----------------|-------|-------|
| IV Dihydroergotamine (DHE) | IV (with metoclopramide premedication) | Raskin protocol |
| IV Valproate | IV | 500–1000 mg over 30 minutes |
| IV Corticosteroids | IV | Dexamethasone 10 mg; reduces recurrence; not for routine use |
| IV Magnesium sulfate | IV | 1–2 g over 20 minutes; particularly for aura-associated attacks |
| IV Ketorolac | IV/IM | NSAID; 15–30 mg |
| IV Prochlorperazine/Metoclopramide | IV | Effective; risk of akathisia |

## 6. Drug Selection Decision Guide

```
Acute Attack:
    │
    ├── Mild-moderate: OTC analgesics → if fail → triptans
    ├── Moderate-severe: Triptan first-line
    │       ├── CV contraindication → Gepant (ubrogepant/rimegepant) or lasmiditan
    │       ├── Nausea/vomiting prominent → SC/nasal triptan OR zolmitriptan nasal
    │       └── Menstrual migraine → Frovatriptan (long half-life)
    └── Refractory → DHE, IV protocols

Preventive (≥4 migraine days/month):
    │
    ├── Hypertension comorbid → Beta-blocker, candesartan
    ├── Depression/anxiety comorbid → Venlafaxine, amitriptyline
    ├── Epilepsy comorbid → Valproate, topiramate (dual use)
    ├── Obesity → Topiramate (weight loss effect)
    ├── Underweight → Amitriptyline, valproate (weight gain)
    ├── Chronic migraine (≥15 days/month) → OnabotulinumtoxinA, CGRP mAbs
    ├── No comorbidities → Any Level A agent; CGRP mAb if prior failures
    └── Pregnancy planning → Avoid valproate, topiramate; magnesium is safest
```
