## 1. OVERVIEW

Among all lifestyle factors, **routine regularity** — the consistency and predictability of daily behaviors — is arguably the most impactful single variable in migraine management. The migraine brain is fundamentally intolerant of variability. Disruptions to meal timing, sleep schedules, activity patterns, medication timing, and daily rhythm are among the most commonly reported and reproducible triggers across migraine populations.

The core principle: **the migraine brain thrives on rhythm and is destabilized by change.**

### 1.1 Why Routine Matters for Prediction
- Routine disruptions are identifiable, specific, time-stamped events that can be logged and correlated with attack onset
- Irregular routines compound with other triggers (sleep, stress, diet) multiplicatively
- Regularity is a modifiable variable — the chatbot can actively reinforce it
- Detecting a routine disruption is an early, actionable predictive signal

### 1.2 Biological Basis — Chronobiology of Migraine
- The hypothalamus functions as the brain's master clock (suprachiasmatic nucleus, SCN)
- Migraine originates partly from hypothalamic dysregulation — the hypothalamus is active in prodrome and may initiate attacks
- Circadian rhythm stability directly affects serotonin production, cortisol patterns, sleep architecture, and autonomic tone — all migraine-relevant systems
- Any behavior that disrupts circadian rhythmicity raises the probability of a hypothalamically-initiated migraine cascade

## 2. SLEEP ROUTINE

### 2.1 Sleep Schedule Consistency
Sleep timing consistency is the single most important routine variable for migraine prevention:

| Sleep Variable | Optimal Target | High-Risk Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Bedtime variability | ≤30 minutes deviation from set time | >1.5h deviation on any night |
| Wake time variability | ≤30 minutes deviation | >1.5h deviation (especially sleeping in) |
| Weekend drift | None — same schedule 7 days/week | "Social jet lag" — later on weekends |
| Total sleep | 7–9 hours consistently | <6h or >9.5h |
| Napping | Short naps ≤20 min if needed | Long naps (>60 min) alter night sleep |

### 2.2 Weekend and Holiday Schedule Disruption
The "weekend migraine" or "let-down migraine" is directly caused by routine disruption:
- Sleeping in by 1.5–2+ hours → shifts cortisol awakening response, delays caffeine intake, alters circadian phase
- Key intervention: maintain wake time within 30 minutes even on days off

### 2.3 Napping Rules for Migraineurs
- Short naps (15–20 min) can restore alertness without disrupting night sleep
- Naps >45 minutes enter slow-wave sleep — waking from N3 causes sleep inertia and can trigger migraine
- Avoid napping after 3 PM — reduces sleep pressure and delays sleep onset
- Napping during postdrome can be therapeutic — an exception to general nap guidance

## 3. MEAL TIMING ROUTINE

### 3.1 Why Meal Regularity Matters
Skipping meals and extended fasting are among the **most consistently reported migraine triggers** across studies — affecting 40–57% of migraineurs. Mechanisms:
- Hypoglycemia (blood glucose drop) triggers neurochemical changes including serotonin fluctuation
- Fasting elevates cortisol (physiological stress response)
- Ketone production during fasting can alter brain pH and neuronal excitability
- Delayed meals disrupt gastric emptying patterns, worsening nausea susceptibility

### 3.2 Optimal Meal Timing Pattern

| Meal | Target Timing | Key Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Breakfast | Within 1 hour of waking | Never skip; sets glucose baseline for the day |
| Lunch | 4–5 hours after breakfast | Prevents mid-day glucose trough |
| Dinner | 4–6 hours after lunch | Consistent timing daily |
| Snacks | If gap >5 hours between meals | Prevents extended fasting |
| Maximum fasting window (waking hours) | ≤5 hours between eating events | Strictly avoid >6h without food |

### 3.3 High-Risk Meal Routine Patterns
- **Breakfast skipping:** Most common single meal-related trigger; especially dangerous when combined with morning exercise
- **Working through lunch:** Occupational hazard — common in high-stress jobs; fasting + stress compound
- **Irregular dinner timing:** Eating very late or very early disrupts gastric and circadian rhythms
- **Dieting / caloric restriction:** Intentional fasting, intermittent fasting, and crash diets are high-risk
- **Travel disruption:** Time zone changes disrupt meal timing alongside sleep — compound risk

### 3.4 Food Quality as Part of Meal Routine
Beyond timing, meal composition affects migraine risk within routine context:
- High-glycemic meals (white bread, sugar) → rapid glucose spike and crash → trigger window
- Low-protein meals → lower tryptophan availability → reduced serotonin synthesis
- Consistent, balanced meals (complex carb + protein + healthy fat) stabilize blood glucose and neurotransmitter precursors

## 4. CAFFEINE ROUTINE

### 4.1 Caffeine's Dual Role in Migraine
Caffeine is uniquely positioned — it can both treat and trigger migraine:
- **Acute benefit:** Caffeine potentiates analgesics (adenosine antagonism reduces pain signaling); present in combination OTC migraine remedies
- **Chronic risk:** Regular consumption creates adenosine receptor upregulation — creating caffeine dependence and withdrawal headache

### 4.2 Caffeine Routine Rules for Migraineurs

| Pattern | Risk Level | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| Regular daily intake, consistent timing | ⚠️ Moderate (dependence risk) | If consumed, keep consistent daily dose and timing |
| Consumption >200mg/day consistently | ⚠️ Moderate-High | Consider gradual reduction |
| Variable intake (some days high, some low) | 🔴 High | Withdrawal pattern — major trigger |
| Skipping morning coffee after regular use | 🔴 High | Acute withdrawal headache within 12–24h |
| Caffeine after 2 PM | ⚠️ Moderate | Disrupts sleep architecture |
| Complete caffeine cessation (abrupt) | 🔴 High | Must taper gradually |

### 4.3 Caffeine Tapering Protocol (Educational)
For users who wish to reduce caffeine intake:
- Reduce by no more than 10% of daily intake per week
- Maintain timing consistency throughout taper
- Taper over 6–12 weeks for heavy users (>300mg/day)
- Expect mild withdrawal symptoms; severe persistent headache warrants medical advice

### 4.4 Common Caffeine Sources and Approximate Content

| Source | Caffeine (mg) |
|---|---|
| Espresso (single shot, 30ml) | 63mg |
| Drip coffee (240ml) | 95–165mg |
| Instant coffee (240ml) | 60–80mg |
| Black tea (240ml) | 40–70mg |
| Green tea (240ml) | 20–45mg |
| Cola drinks (355ml) | 34–46mg |
| Energy drinks (250ml) | 80–160mg |
| Dark chocolate (40g) | 20–30mg |
| Decaf coffee (240ml) | 2–15mg |

## 5. MEDICATION TIMING ROUTINE

### 5.1 Importance of Consistent Medication Timing
For patients on preventive migraine medications, consistent daily timing is critical:
- Many preventives (beta-blockers, antidepressants, CGRP mAbs) require stable blood levels
- Irregular dosing creates trough-and-peak fluctuations that may paradoxically increase attack risk
- Missing doses of certain medications (especially beta-blockers) can cause rebound effects

### 5.2 Chatbot Reminders and Medication Routine
- The chatbot can log whether the user took their preventive medication at the usual time
- If a user reports missing a dose, the chatbot should advise them to contact their prescribing physician — not advise on catch-up dosing
- Acute medication (triptans, NSAIDs) timing relative to attack onset is critical — the chatbot can reinforce treating early in the attack for best efficacy

### 5.3 Medication Overuse Headache (MOH) Pattern
Irregular or excessive use of acute medications creates a MOH pattern — a routine-related risk:
- NSAIDs: risk if used >15 days/month
- Triptans, opioids, combination analgesics: risk if used >10 days/month
- MOH creates a baseline of daily/near-daily headache — the chatbot should flag this pattern and recommend physician consultation

## 6. SCREEN TIME AND DIGITAL ROUTINE

### 6.1 Screen Exposure as a Routine Variable
Screen time patterns are increasingly relevant as a modern migraine trigger dimension:

| Behavior | Migraine Relevance |
|---|---|
| Prolonged uninterrupted screen use | Eye strain → frontal headache; may trigger migraine |
| Bright screen in dark room | High contrast → photostimulation; trigger risk |
| Blue light exposure before bed | Suppresses melatonin → disrupts sleep onset |
| High-refresh-rate flickering (older monitors) | Photic trigger risk |
| Small phone screen with close proximity | Accommodation strain; posture-related neck tension |

### 6.2 Recommended Screen Routine Practices
- **20-20-20 rule:** Every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds
- Enable night mode / warm display after sunset
- Maintain screen brightness at ambient light level (not brighter than surroundings)
- Screen-free 60 minutes before bed
- Use matte screen protectors or anti-glare monitors if photosensitivity is a trigger
- Ergonomic screen positioning: eye level, arm's length distance

## 7. POSTURE AND NECK CARE ROUTINE

### 7.1 Neck Tension and Migraine
Cervicogenic contributions to migraine are well-established:
- The trigeminal nucleus caudalis extends into the upper cervical spinal cord (C1–C3) — neck pain and migraine share neural pathways
- Sustained poor posture (desk work, phone use) causes chronic trapezius, sternocleidomastoid, and suboccipital muscle tension
- Neck stiffness is a common prodromal symptom — but can also be a trigger in its own right
- Cervicogenic headache (headache originating from neck structures) is a distinct entity that frequently complicates migraine

### 7.2 Recommended Neck and Posture Routine

| Practice | Frequency | Benefit |
|---|---|---|
| Cervical range-of-motion stretches | Morning and evening | Reduces suboccipital tension |
| Shoulder rolls | Every 1–2 hours at desk | Prevents trapezius accumulation |
| Ergonomic workstation setup | Ongoing | Neutral spine reduces chronic tension |
| Mindful posture checks | Every hour | Prevents forward head posture |
| Heat/cold compress to neck | As needed | Reduces acute muscle tension |

### 7.3 Ergonomic Workstation Checklist
- Monitor top edge at or just below eye level
- Screen distance: arm's length (50–70cm)
- Chair height: feet flat on floor, knees at 90°
- Lumbar support: maintains natural lumbar curve
- Keyboard/mouse: elbows at 90°, wrists neutral
- Phone: avoid prolonged head-tilt for phone calls — use headset or speaker

## 8. STRESS AND WIND-DOWN ROUTINE

### 8.1 The Role of Routine in Stress Management
A predictable daily routine is itself a stress-reduction strategy — the brain allocates less energy to decision-making and uncertainty when behaviors are habitual. For migraineurs:
- Predictable low-stress anchor points in the day (morning routine, lunch break, evening wind-down) act as cortisol regulators
- Absence of routine increases decision fatigue and background stress load
- The transition from high-stress work to relaxation is a known migraine window — the "let-down" effect

### 8.2 Evening Wind-Down Routine
A structured wind-down routine of 45–60 minutes before sleep is highly beneficial:

| Time Before Sleep | Activity |
|---|---|
| 60 min | Dim household lights; stop work/email |
| 45 min | Light stretching or yoga |
| 30 min | Screen-free activity (reading, journaling, light music) |
| 15 min | Relaxation technique (breathing, PMR, meditation) |
| Bedtime | Consistent; cool, dark, quiet room |

### 8.3 Morning Routine
Morning routines set the neurobiological tone for the day:
- **Consistent wake time** (most important element)
- Hydrate immediately on waking (200–400ml water before caffeine)
- Eat within 1 hour of waking
- Avoid checking phone/email for first 15–30 minutes (reduces cortisol spike)
- Light exposure — natural daylight within 30 minutes of waking anchors circadian rhythm
- Brief body scan or mindful breathing (5 minutes) — reduces morning anxiety

## 9. SOCIAL AND OCCUPATIONAL ROUTINE

### 9.1 Occupational Routine Disruptions
Work-related routine disruptions are among the most common real-world migraine triggers:

| Occupational Factor | Migraine Risk | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Irregular work shifts / shift rotation | 🔴 High | Chronic circadian disruption |
| Prolonged desk work without breaks | 🟡 Moderate | Eye strain, neck tension, sedentary risk |
| High cognitive demand periods (deadlines) | 🟡 Moderate | Stress + missed meals + posture |
| Business travel / jet lag | 🔴 High | Compound disruption |
| Remote work (loss of structure) | 🟡 Moderate | Irregular meals, less movement |
| Loud or bright work environments | 🟡 Moderate | Environmental trigger exposure |

### 9.2 Travel Routine Management
Travel disrupts nearly every routine variable simultaneously:
- Sleep timing disrupted (time zones, hotel environments)
- Meal timing unpredictable
- Hydration neglected (airport security, busy schedules)
- Medications may be left behind or poorly timed
- Environmental triggers (air pressure changes, unfamiliar lighting, crowds)

**Chatbot Travel Advisory:**
When user reports upcoming travel, flag as elevated risk period and recommend:
1. Maintain home sleep schedule as closely as possible for first 1–2 days
2. Pack medications in carry-on, not checked luggage
3. Pre-hydrate before and during flights
4. Set medication and meal alarms for home-schedule timing
5. Identify a quiet, dark space at destination for potential attacks

### 10.1 Routine Disruption as an Actionable Predictive Signal
Routine deviation is one of the most valuable inputs because it is:
- Specific (identifiable behavior)

### 10.3 Risk Score Contribution

| Routine Event | Risk Adjustment |
|---|---|
| Skipped breakfast | +2 |
| Skipped any other meal | +1 |
| Meal gap >6h | +2 |
| Caffeine skip after regular use | +3 |
| Wake time >1.5h later than usual | +2 |
| Travel / jet lag active | +3 |
| Multiple routine disruptions same day | +2 additional (stacking) |
| All routines consistent (sleep, meals, caffeine, meds) | −1 (protective) |

### 10.4 Compounding Rule
When 3 or more routine disruptions occur on the same day, the risk level should automatically escalate to at least **Moderate (🟡)** regardless of other symptom inputs. Routine stacking is a highly reliable attack predictor.

## 11. CHATBOT INTERACTION GUIDELINES

### 11.1 Daily Check-In Framework
The chatbot should include routine-specific questions in every daily check-in:
- "Did you eat breakfast this morning?"
- "Was your caffeine intake about the same as usual, and at your normal time?"
- "Did you wake up at your usual time today?"
- "Were there any major changes to your usual schedule today?"

### 11.2 Psychoeducation Messages
When users report routine disruptions, provide brief education:
> "Skipping breakfast is one of the most common migraine triggers. Your body's blood sugar dropped lower than usual this morning, which can activate migraine pathways. Your risk is elevated today — try to eat a small meal or snack soon, stay well hydrated, and keep an eye on early warning symptoms."

### 11.3 Positive Reinforcement
When users report consistent routines, acknowledge it:
> "Your routine looks consistent today — same sleep schedule, meals on time, and normal caffeine intake. Routine consistency is one of the best things you can do to prevent migraine attacks. Keep it up."
